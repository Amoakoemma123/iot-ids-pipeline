## Title

**What Re-running a Machine-Learning IoT Intrusion Detection Series Taught Us: Failure Modes and the Checks That Catch Them**

## Abstract

Evaluation pitfalls in machine-learning intrusion detection are well catalogued, yet they persist. We re-ran six of seven unpublished, largely AI-generated studies of IoT intrusion detection with a corrected pipeline, five seeds for main comparisons, tree-ensemble references on the same features, scripted numbers and repeated review; the main conclusion of five changed. We document 18 failure modes with their effect, the conclusion each changed, how each was found and the check that catches it. Three are software faults that evaluation-pitfall taxonomies do not list, and a fourth is only partly covered. A batch-normalised CNN on min–max scaled flow counts scored 0.028 macro-F1, below a majority-class predictor, where a random forest on the same features scored 0.927; the original study read this as the features carrying no information. A differentially private training loop never updated the trainable weights and was reported as a utility collapse; implemented correctly, privacy cost 17 points at ε = 2.9. A framework default left 26 runs evaluated at weights other than their best. A static audit of 9 published code releases found the duplicate and baseline faults in every assessable release (none of the 8 removed duplicates; none contains a tuned tree baseline), instances of two software faults and risk configurations for two more. We quantify what AI reviewer instances found and missed, and give a costed checklist, a check library and a script that reproduces the headline findings.


**Keywords:** intrusion detection; Internet of Things; machine learning; evaluation methodology; reproducibility; data leakage; experimental pitfalls

## 1 Introduction

Machine-learning intrusion detection for IoT networks is a large literature in which new architectures are compared on a few public benchmarks, usually with one training run per configuration. The weaknesses of this practice are known. Sommer and Paxson [2010] explained why network intrusion detection is a hard setting for machine learning; Arp et al. [2022] listed ten pitfalls of machine learning in security and found them widespread in top-tier papers; Apruzzese et al. [2023] argued that intrusion detection research rarely allows a pragmatic assessment of operational value; and studies of the benchmarks have documented labelling errors, flow-construction errors and features that separate classes for reasons unrelated to attacks [Engelen et al. 2021; Flood et al. 2024]. What these accounts rarely give is the size of each pitfall's effect on a concrete study, how the pitfall was discovered, or the cheap check that would have caught it before submission.

This paper gives such evidence from one series of seven studies by our group. The source matters for how far the evidence generalises, so we state it first. The studies were unpublished drafts, written in 2026 with an AI model generating most of the code and drafting the manuscripts, and single-seed (Section 3.1). They are plausibly weaker than the published literature, and the effect sizes below describe how wrong these drafts were, not how much each pitfall distorts published work. What a single-group case study offers in exchange is complete provenance: every draft, line of code, intermediate file and review report is available, so each failure can be traced to its cause and its effect measured, which audits of published work cannot do. Two further things reduce the limitation. First, many of the failures arose not in the drafting but in code and data handling that is common to the area (Section 4). Second, we audited the public code of 9 published intrusion-detection papers on the same benchmarks for the same failure modes (Section 5.3).

We re-ran six of the studies with a corrected pipeline and stricter controls, and re-scoped the seventh, an integrated framework built on the other six, as the present paper. Table 1 summarises how each study's main conclusion changed.

The failures behind these changes were mostly ordinary, and Table 2 maps each to existing taxonomies. Most appear in evaluation-pitfall taxonomies. Three software faults do not, and a fourth is only partly covered: a network that fails without error on badly scaled inputs, a framework default that silently keeps the wrong weights, normalisation statistics that make evaluation-mode results unreliable, and, partly covered, timing and size measurements that do not measure what the text says. They belong to categories of deep-learning fault taxonomies [Humbatova et al. 2020; Islam et al. 2019], but are not discussed there as threats to the validity of a security evaluation, which is how they acted here: each produced, or could have produced, a plausible number that was read as a finding. Several failures were introduced during the re-run itself, by authors who were looking for exactly these problems, and were caught only by automated checks or review.

Our contributions are: (1) a catalogue of 18 failure modes from one series, mapped to evaluation-pitfall and deep-learning fault taxonomies, with each mode's prevalence, its effect on reported numbers and the study conclusion it changed, every number generated from released result files; (2) an account of how each failure was found, including quantified outcomes of AI reviewer instances and the failures they missed; (3) a static audit of published code for the same failure modes, including the software faults; and (4) a costed checklist with a check library, a script that reproduces the headline findings, and questions an operator can ask of a published or vendor result. Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 describes the series and the re-run, Section 4 presents the catalogue, Section 5 analyses detection routes, AI assistance and the external audit, Section 6 gives the checklist, and Sections 7 and 8 discuss limitations and conclude.

## 2 Related Work

**Pitfalls of machine learning in security.** Sommer and Paxson [2010] argued that intrusion detection differs from closed-world learning in its error costs, its semantic gap and the difficulty of sound evaluation. Arp et al. [2022] catalogued ten pitfalls, from sampling bias and data snooping to inappropriate baselines, measures and threat models, and found them widespread in 30 top-tier security papers; Pendlebury et al. [2019] showed that spatial and temporal bias inflates malware-detection results; and Olszewski et al. [2023] documented why many machine-learning papers from tier-1 security venues could not be reproduced from their artefacts. Unlike these audits, we re-ran our own studies with access to every line of code, intermediate file and draft, which lets us trace each failure to its cause and measure its effect, at the price of a sample of one group.

**Defects of intrusion-detection benchmarks.** CICIDS2017 [Sharafaldin et al. 2018] has documented errors in traffic generation, flow construction and labelling [Engelen et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2022], and correcting them changes detection results [Lanvin et al. 2023]. Flood et al. [2024] described recurring design flaws in benchmark datasets, among them class-separating TTL values, and showed that setting protocol and TTL to benign values evades every model they tested on UNSW-NB15 [Moustafa and Slay 2015]. Models often fail across datasets [D'hooge et al. 2020; Sarhan et al. 2022] and on withheld attack types [Kus et al. 2022]. NSL-KDD was created by removing the redundant records of KDD'99 [Tavallaee et al. 2009]. We also used TON_IoT [Moustafa 2021] and N-BaIoT [Meidan et al. 2018].

**Leakage, variance and reproducibility.** Kaufman et al. [2012] formalised leakage; Kapoor and Narayanan [2023] traced it through hundreds of papers in 17 fields, the REFORMS checklist [Kapoor et al. 2024] turns such issues into reporting items, and Lones [2024] lists pitfalls across the workflow, including duplicates, which also contaminate image benchmarks [Barz and Denzler 2020]. Seeds, data sampling and hyperparameters can change benchmark conclusions [Bouthillier et al. 2021; Henderson et al. 2018], identical runs differ through implementation non-determinism [Pham et al. 2020; Zhuang et al. 2022], few papers document enough to be reproduced [Gundersen and Kjensmo 2018; Pineau et al. 2021], and gains are often reported without identifying their source [Lipton and Steinhardt 2019]. Trees remain strong on tabular data such as flow records [Grinsztajn et al. 2022; Shwartz-Ziv and Armon 2022].

**Faults in deep-learning software and in specific methods.** Studies of deep-learning bugs classify faults such as wrong input normalisation and API misuse [Zhang et al. 2018; Islam et al. 2019; Humbatova et al. 2020]; normalising inputs to similar ranges is long-standing advice [LeCun et al. 1998]. These studies treat faults as defects to fix; our concern is a fault that yields a plausible number reported as a security finding. Method-specific traps are also known: private training needs per-example clipping and a matching accountant [Abadi et al. 2016] and is often implemented wrongly [Tramèr et al. 2022; Cebere et al. 2026], which auditing can expose [Nasr et al. 2021]; federated results depend on the partition [Kairouz et al. 2021; Li et al. 2022]; explanation methods differ in accuracy and stability [Warnecke et al. 2020]; and continual-learning results depend on the scenario [van de Ven et al. 2022].

**Fabricated references.** Large language models fabricate a substantial share of the references they generate and introduce errors into real ones [Walters and Wilder 2023; Bhattacharyya et al. 2023; Agrawal et al. 2024]. We found such references in two of the original studies (Section 5.2).

## 3 The Series and the Re-run

### 3.1 The original studies

The original series comprised seven studies of machine-learning intrusion detection for IoT networks, written and built in 2026 with an AI model generating most of the code and drafting the manuscripts under the authors' direction, and sharing one data pipeline: (1) a lightweight 1D convolutional network (CNN) of about 24,000 parameters compared with classical baselines; (2) adversarial robustness under PGD [Madry et al. 2018] and a class-conditional adversarial training method (CCAT); (3) continual learning over sequences of attack classes with replay, EWC [Kirkpatrick et al. 2017], LwF [Li and Hoiem 2018] and GEM [Lopez-Paz and Ranzato 2017]; (4) federated learning with FedAvg [McMahan et al. 2017], FedProx [Li et al. 2020], FedAdam [Reddi et al. 2021] and SCAFFOLD [Karimireddy et al. 2020], including differentially private training; (5) explanations with SHAP [Lundberg and Lee 2017], LIME [Ribeiro et al. 2016] and counterfactuals; (6) fusion of semantic groups of flow features; and (7) an integrated framework combining components of the other six. All seven used TON_IoT and UNSW-NB15; five also used CICIDS2017. Feature selection used mutual information followed by recursive feature elimination [Guyon et al. 2002], minority classes in the training split were oversampled with SMOTE [Chawla et al. 2002], and each experiment was run with one seed (42). None was published. Before revision, a review of all seven by separate instances of an AI model found internal numerical inconsistencies, a row-index feature among the model inputs and single-seed headline claims, and rated every study as needing major revision.

### 3.2 The re-run

We re-ran Studies 1 to 6 with a corrected pipeline. The changes common to all re-runs were:

- **Data.** Identifier columns dropped; exact duplicates removed after every column drop; a guard that no test record appears in the training split; feature selection, scaling and oversampling fitted on training data only. CICIDS2017 was replaced by N-BaIoT where a third dataset was needed, because of its documented labelling and flow-construction errors [Engelen et al. 2021; Lanvin et al. 2023] and to fit the compute budget; we did not measure the original studies' CICIDS2017 results, so failures specific to them are not in the catalogue.
- **Training.** Five seeds for the main comparisons (three for some adversarial-training variants, for all explanation analyses, and for the differentially private and supplementary federated runs); best weights restored explicitly and the restored epoch recorded; hyperparameters, checkpoints and thresholds selected on validation data.
- **References.** A tuned tree ensemble (random forest or gradient-boosted trees) trained on the same features as every neural model, and a single-network or centralised reference wherever a more complex design was evaluated.
- **Statistics.** Paired comparisons over seeds, confidence intervals, and a Holm-corrected family [Holm 1979] for confirmatory claims; exploratory analyses labelled as such. In this paper, "a ± b" is a mean and standard deviation over seeds unless stated as a 95% confidence interval, and † marks a comparison that was significant after Holm correction in the corrected study's confirmatory family.
- **Reporting.** Every number in each manuscript is a placeholder filled by a script from the result files; a second script checks each qualitative statement (for example, that replay beats every other strategy on all three datasets) against the same files; every reference was resolved through Crossref, DBLP or arXiv and checked against the claim it supports.
- **Review.** Before this paper was written, Study 3 went through one review round (its report was not kept), Study 4 five, Study 5 three and Study 6 two; Studies 1 and 2 had none. Each round used up to four reviewers given only the manuscript, the code and the result files: one acting as a journal referee, one re-deriving every number, one comparing the methods section with the code, and one checking the references. The reviewers were separate instances of the same AI model that wrote the re-run code (Section 5.2), so we call them separate rather than independent. Failures found in a round were fixed, re-run where needed, and the study reviewed again.

All runs used one laptop (Intel Core i5-10200H, 16 GB RAM, NVIDIA GTX 1650 with 4 GB). Code, result files, review reports and the value-generation and checking scripts are released (https://github.com/Amoakoemma123/iot-ids-pipeline). The corrected studies are reported in full in six separate manuscripts, all currently under review at other journals; this paper uses their results only to illustrate failure modes.

### 3.3 What counts as a failure

We count as a failure any defect in data handling, training, protocol, measurement, baselines, statistics or reporting that changed a reported number or conclusion, or would have changed one had it not been caught. Defects that we suspected, tested and found not to matter are reported separately (Section 4.6), because knowing which checks are cheap to skip is also useful. For each failure, Section 4 gives the symptom, the effect before and after correction, how it was found and the check that would have caught it earlier. "After" numbers come from the final result files of the corrected studies; "before" numbers are re-derived from the original result files, which survive for all seven studies. Where a number or a detection record has no result file behind it, we say so; two kinds of number come from other records (the 1.9% copy rate in F2, from a review report and the commit that fixed it, and the review counts of Section 5.2, compiled from the review reports, project log and commits). Two kinds of failure are included: those in the original studies, and those introduced during the re-run and caught before submission (marked "re-run" in Table 2).

We say a mode changed a conclusion on its own when correcting that mode alone changes it. Where the correction required a new experiment, the experiment counts as a correction if it replaces the flawed one with a corrected version of the same comparison: size-budgeted, tuned trees in place of one large untuned forest (F16), and a threat model an attacker can realise in place of an unconstrained perturbation (F11). An analysis that answers a question the original study never asked counts as a new analysis. By this definition, four of the five changed conclusions of Table 1 trace to at least one mode that changed it on its own: Study 1 to F14 and F16, Study 2 to F11 and F18, Study 4 to F7 (the private-training result) and F16 (the centralised reference came from a different recipe), and Study 6 to F4 and F16. Study 5's change cannot be traced to a single mode: the original 0.67 was one comparison, without an interval, of the global SHAP ranking with aggregated LIME rankings on TON_IoT, whereas the corrected study measured agreement per alert, which is a change of protocol rather than the correction of one failure, and its hardening claim was qualified by a control (F13c) rather than reversed. F16 also changed two secondary claims of Study 4 (on SCAFFOLD and FedAdam). F16 and F17 each removed Study 7's headline on its own (the framework scored below Study 1's forest, and the headline was not among its own seeds), which is why Study 7 was re-scoped rather than counted among the five. Study 3 counts as unchanged: its main conclusion, that replay forgets least, held, while its secondary claim about EWC did not. The TTL attack (F3) and the natural partitions and own-traffic evaluation (F10) are new analyses.

### 4.1 Data

**F1. Identifier used as a feature.** UNSW-NB15 ships with an `id` column, a row index. The shared loader did not drop it, recursive feature elimination kept it, and it became the top-ranked SHAP feature for both the random forest (0.0484) and the CNN (0.0614) in the original explanation study, which called this "a valuable XAI finding" and kept the model. The same column sat inside the original fusion study's "flow" stream and among the integrated framework's top-10 SHAP features. Because the file is ordered by class (47,911 leading benign rows), the index carries label information. With the selected features held fixed, removing `id` lowered macro-F1 by 0.031 [0.016, 0.046] when duplicates were present but only 0.007 [−0.006, 0.020] (not significant) once they were removed, while accuracy fell by 0.053 and 0.051 (means over three seeds; the macro-F1 effects are for seed 42 with bootstrap intervals). On these ten features the leak thus survived deduplication on accuracy but not on macro-F1; with all features, on the same file and the same kind of random split, the macro-F1 effect grew after deduplication instead (Section 5.3). A vanishing effect on one metric and one feature set is therefore no evidence that a suspicious feature is harmless. *Check:* drop identifiers by name before anything else; treat any top-ranked feature without a causal story as a leak until an ablation says otherwise, on every reported metric.

**F2. Duplicates within and across splits.** The series described its data as deduplicated. On UNSW-NB15 this was false: deduplication compared full rows, and the unique `id` made every row unique. Once `id` was dropped, deduplication removed 67,601 of 175,341 rows (38.6%), and the "generic" attack class shrank from 40,000 to 4,181 records. In the original split, 44.0% of test records had an exact copy (features and label) in the training data. A second layer appeared in review: the corrected loader first deduplicated and then dropped further columns, which, according to the review record and the commit that fixed it (no result file was kept), left 1.9% of test records with an exact copy in training; deduplicating after every column drop removed them. A third layer remains by design: on the features a model actually sees after selection, 13.7% of UNSW-NB15 test records match a training record with the same label and 16.3% match one with any label (0.1% on TON_IoT). These are distinct flows that the selected features cannot distinguish, so removing them would change the task; we report scores on the clean subset (no feature match) instead. The copies did not inflate the forest's score. In Study 5, the forest scored 0.6089 on the full test set and 0.6472 on the subset without any-label copies; in Study 6, the forest's margin over late fusion grew from +0.034 to +0.073 on the same kind of subset. The copies are concentrated in the classes the forest confuses most (in Study 5, generic 78% and analysis 53% of those classes' test records have a same-label copy). The copy check itself can fail: rounding features to eight decimals reported 17.1% of TON_IoT test records as copies, against 0.1% with exact float32 equality. *Check:* deduplicate after the last column drop; assert that no test record occurs in the training split; measure copies on the selected, scaled features with exact equality and report a clean-subset score.

**F3. Testbed artefacts.** After `id` was removed, SHAP ranked the source TTL first on UNSW-NB15. The value 31 occurs in 39,123 rows, none of them attacks, and a rule using TTL alone reaches 63.7% ten-class accuracy against a majority-class share of 49.0%. Flood et al. [2024] documented this artefact and showed that setting protocol and TTL to benign values evades every model they tested. The initial TTL is set by the sender, so in our adversarial re-run an attacker who only chose one of the benign values (31, 254, 62, 1), with all other fields and derived features consistent, evaded the CNN on 83.8% of detected attack flows but gradient-boosted trees on only 36.5%. Ports on TON_IoT were a smaller artefact: removing source and destination ports lowered all six models tested by 0.013 to 0.015 (all p < 0.001; random forest −0.015, gradient-boosted trees at learning rate 0.1 −0.015), with the largest per-class losses for the forest on ransomware (−0.055), xss (−0.049), mitm (−0.030). *Check:* ablate fields set by the testbed rather than by the attack (TTL, ports, addresses, timestamps) and report the attack that changes only those fields.

### 4.2 Training

**F4. Silent or graded failure of a network on badly scaled inputs.** Flow counts such as bytes and packets are heavy-tailed: after min–max scaling, the median of every TON_IoT volume feature was at most 0.0002 and its standard deviation at most 0.014. A compact CNN with batch normalisation [Ioffe and Szegedy 2015] trained on these features without error and scored 0.028 macro-F1, below the 0.037 of a majority-class predictor (benign false-positive rate 0.753); a random forest on the same seven features scored 0.927. The original fusion study had reported the same failure (macro-F1 0.017) as evidence that "the seven flow-level statistics […] carry negligible discriminative information". With a quantile transform of the network inputs and nothing else changed, the stream scored 0.904, late fusion rose from 0.176 to 0.953, and the stream ranking reversed. The first re-run repeated the failure because it inherited min–max scaling. The continual-learning re-run hit a milder form, compounded by a small training budget: its joint-retraining upper bound reached 0.768 against a forest's 0.965, hiding most of replay's benefit (replay rose from 0.507 to 0.872 after an asinh map and a larger budget). A five-seed factorial showed that the input map mattered at least as much as the budget (+0.103 for the map at the small budget and +0.051 at the larger one, against +0.066 for the larger budget with min–max inputs and +0.015 with the asinh map). In the lightweight-model study the effect was graded: the quantile map gained +0.047 on TON_IoT and +0.020 on UNSW-NB15, and the original gap of 0.076 to the forest shrank to about 0.01, of which the quantile map accounts for +0.047 on TON_IoT and other changes to the pipeline for the rest. A rule that flags runs peaking in the first few epochs catches the fusion case (best epoch at most 4 of 60), only some continual-learning runs (stopped after 8–35 epochs with patience 5) and none of the lightweight-model runs (best epochs 54–92); the tree reference caught all three. *Check:* train a tree ensemble on the same features as every network; investigate any network far below it, near a majority-class predictor, or peaking in the first epochs; report the share of runs that reach the epoch cap.

**F5. Framework default keeps last-epoch weights.** In tf.keras 2.10, the version used here, `EarlyStopping(restore_best_weights=True)` restores the best weights only when early stopping fires; a run that reaches the epoch cap keeps its last-epoch weights [Keras 2022]. Keras 3.0 moved the restore to the end of training [Keras 2023], so the hazard affects code and results produced with earlier versions that behave like 2.10, which include ours. In the second round of the fusion re-run, 30 runs reached the 60-epoch cap, and 26 of them were evaluated at weights other than their best epoch, including runs behind the only significant comparison. A reviewer comparing the methods section with the code found the behaviour by reading the installed library source; the documentation does not mention it. With an explicit best-weight callback and a 120-epoch budget, the comparison survived (late minus early fusion +0.005, Holm-adjusted p = 0.006), and 1 of 90 main and capacity-control runs reached the cap. *Check:* restore best weights in your own code; record and assert the restored epoch in every result file.

**F6. Normalisation statistics make evaluation-mode results unreliable.** In the original continual-learning study all strategies trained the first task identically, yet the first-task TON_IoT score ranged from 0.245 to 0.980 across strategies (naive 0.979, EWC 0.581, replay 0.245). The study attributed the EWC case to GPU non-determinism and did not mention replay's, which flattered replay's headline forgetting score (0.252): forgetting is measured from each task's peak [Chaudhry et al. 2018], so a model with a low peak has little to forget. The code combined batch normalisation with momentum 0.99, early stopping on an evaluation-mode metric, no minimum epoch count, no weight restoring and non-deterministic kernels; we believe slow running statistics produced one-class predictions that triggered early stopping, but no controlled run isolates this, so the cause is uncertain. The corrected study (momentum 0.9, minimum epochs, restored weights, deterministic kernels, a cached first-task model) gave identical first-task scores for all seven strategies on all three datasets. In the federated re-run the mechanism was measured: with server-averaged running statistics, validation macro-F1 changed by 0.110 on average between consecutive rounds, against 0.016 with group normalisation [Wu and He 2018; Hsieh et al. 2020] and 0.018 after federated re-estimation of the statistics. *Check:* plot evaluation-mode metrics per epoch or round and treat swings larger than the effects being compared as a failure; recalibrate normalisation statistics or use group normalisation.

**F7. Private training that never trained.** The original federated study reported that differentially private FedAvg "collapses utility across all tested noise levels" (best macro-F1 0.050) and blamed clipping. In the code, the loss was reduced outside the gradient tape, so every gradient was `None`; a filter that skipped `None` gradients removed all of them and the optimiser step did nothing. The result file shows the symptom: macro-F1 0.047 at the first checkpoint and 0.006 on average afterwards, below the 0.037 of a majority-class predictor and consistent with a constant prediction of a minority class. The trainable weights never changed; what did change between checkpoints were the batch-normalisation running statistics, which are updated in the forward pass without gradients and were averaged across clients. Clipping was also per batch rather than per example [Abadi et al. 2016], normalisation statistics were shared without noise, and the accountant assumed sampling that training did not use. With per-example clipping, Poisson sampling and an RDP accountant [Mironov 2017; Mironov et al. 2019] at δ = 10⁻⁵, privacy cost 17 points at ε = 2.9 (macro-F1 0.683 ± 0.057 against 0.856 ± 0.046 for the same code without clipping or noise; means with 95% confidence intervals, n = 3): costly, but no collapse. *Check:* assert that parameters change after a step; run the private code path with noise and clipping off and compare with non-private training; use a maintained per-example clipping implementation and accountant.

**F8. Unreported non-determinism.** All original studies fixed seed 42 "for reproducibility". On GPUs this does not make training repeatable. In the federated re-run, rerunning identical configurations changed validation-selected test macro-F1 by up to 0.109 (largest absolute difference over 15 reruns), comparable to several of the effects the study tested; in the fusion re-run, 118 repeated runs changed by 0.004 on average and 0.024 at most. In the lightweight-model study, TensorFlow warned in the run logs that XLA compilation ignores the seed for the dropout random operations; two pairs of identical runs (a CNN and an MLP) differed by up to 0.0016 in float32 and 0.0062 after int8 quantisation. Tree ensembles were exactly reproducible, including their failures (F16). No conclusion of the corrected studies flipped because of this spread, but in the federated study it set the size of effect that could be read at all. *Check:* repeat at least one configuration and report the spread next to the effect sizes; make training deterministic where the framework allows it.

### 4.3 Protocol

**F9. Selection on test data or on the wrong objective.** The original continual-learning study chose its hyperparameters by "selecting the configuration with the lowest test FM per dataset". The original federated study reported, for every configuration, the maximum test macro-F1 over 10 to 25 checkpoints: averaged over three seeds, best-checkpoint and final-round scores were 0.824 and 0.788 for IID FedAvg and 0.583 and 0.345 under strong label skew (Dirichlet α = 0.1 [Hsu et al. 2019]). Its headline gap to centralised training, "only 3.5 percentage points", used seed 42's best checkpoint (0.845); against a same-recipe centralised model (0.899 ± 0.012, 95% confidence interval) the validation-selected IID gap is −0.042 [−0.059, −0.025]†. Selection on validation data can still reward the wrong thing: the continual-learning re-run first tuned EWC and LwF on current-task macro-F1, which rewards plasticity and chose the weakest regulariser at the edge of both grids; tuning on macro-F1 over all classes seen chose interior values (EWC λ = 100, LwF α = 4). The federated re-run also had grid-edge optima in two review rounds and once tuned two methods at one learning rate and ran them at another. *Check:* the test set influences no choice; tune on the claimed objective, under the settings used; flag grid-edge optima; report validation-selected and final-round scores.

**F10. Unrealistic federated partitions.** The original federated study simulated heterogeneity with Dirichlet label skew on one fixed partition. Partitioning TON_IoT by destination host, with the host assignment redrawn across repetitions, gave a label skew similar to α = 0.5 (the two largest classes made up 52% of a client's data against 56%) but a larger cost relative to centralised training (−0.179 [−0.284, −0.074] against −0.059 [−0.101, −0.016], unadjusted 95% intervals; not significant after Holm correction, p = 0.119, and confounded by client size). With the assignment fixed, as in the original design, FedAvg reached 0.770 ± 0.031; redrawn, 0.720 ± 0.099 (95% confidence intervals). Federation beat local training on the common test set by +0.190 [+0.117, +0.262]†, yet on each site's own traffic the local model was better in 17 of 20 site–repetition pairs on TON_IoT and in 43 of 45 device–repetition pairs on N-BaIoT (on average on all 9 devices); this analysis was exploratory, and the advantage was smaller under a blocked split. *Check:* use natural partitions where possible, redraw them across repetitions, and evaluate each client on its own traffic as well as on a common test set.

**F11. Unrealistic adversarial threat model.** The original adversarial study let PGD perturb every normalised feature, one-hot flags and ports included, within an L∞ ball, counted any misclassification of any correctly classified record, and reported 99.9% success at ε = 0.3. The re-run limited the attacker to padding, extra packets, delays and, on UNSW-NB15, the initial TTL, with a budget β relative to each flow's volume and duration, recomputed derived features, and counted only detected attack flows classified as benign. The direction of the change depended on the reference attack. Against a weak ε-PGD of the original kind (the original reported ε = 0.3; the re-run's reference used ε = 0.05), the constraints lowered success against the TON_IoT CNN (75.1% at ε = 0.05 to 51.4% for the strongest realistic attack at β = 0.5) and raised it on UNSW-NB15 (63.2% to 99.3%), where the free TTL choice dominates (F3); against a strengthened feature-space attack on the mutable features, the unconstrained model overstated evasion everywhere except on the UNSW-NB15 trees. Through the quantile transform, even a strong white-box PGD in the action space stayed 0.4–5.8 percentage points below a gradient-free grid search [Athalye et al. 2018; Carlini et al. 2019]. The threat model and the operating point also decided which defence looked best. Under the unconstrained attack, adversarial training on all features looked far more robust than training on the attacker's actions (27.5% against 77.9% of detected TON_IoT flows evaded) until a BPDA version of the attack evaded 91.4%. Under the realistic attack every defence, at its own operating point, lowered TON_IoT evasion (5.5%–26.2% against 51.4%), but every defence also raised the benign false-positive rate, and against the undefended model at the same decision rule and false-positive rate none significantly reduced the share of attack flows missed or evaded (38.3% with action-space training against 46.9%, Holm p = 0.584; TRADES 92.7%). On UNSW-NB15 the action-space defences held at the matched rate, and over a range of false-positive rates the TON_IoT gain of action-space training appeared under one decision rule but not another. *Check:* define the threat model in the problem space [Pierazzi et al. 2020; Apruzzese et al. 2022], recompute derived features, count only evasions of detected attacks, run a gradient-free search beside gradient attacks, and compare defences at matched false-positive rates, over a range and under more than one decision rule.

**F12. Sequence model on shuffled records.** The original lightweight-model study trained an LSTM on windows of ten consecutive records after a stratified shuffle. Its methods section acknowledged that shuffling "partially violated" temporal order; its discussion credited the LSTM's TON_IoT score to order being "approximately preserved". The windows were sets of random flows. The re-run removed the model, because no dataset as split preserves flow order. *Check:* a sequence model needs data in temporal order and a split that respects it.

**F13. Explanation-evaluation artefacts.** Three arose in the explanation re-run, each caught in review. (a) LIME features were ranked by raw coefficient rather than by contribution; ranking by contribution changed SHAP–LIME top-5 Jaccard on the TON_IoT forest from 0.39 [0.37, 0.41] to 0.24 [0.23, 0.26] (chance 0.12), although SHAP stayed more faithful under both (this review left no report on disk; the project log and the fixing commits record it). (b) SHAP GradientExplainer defaults (200 samples, a seed from NumPy's global state) made the CNN's explanations look unstable: self-agreement rose from 0.50 [0.48, 0.52] to 0.60 [0.58, 0.63] with 1,000 samples. (c) Counterfactual retraining cut the share of attacks evading the TON_IoT forest from 41% to 16%, but as many benign-donor copies cut it to 19% (UNSW-NB15: 43% undefended, 32% with counterfactuals, 41% with the copy control). Against an attacker combining three searches, counterfactuals beat the copy control in every seed on both datasets (18% against 23% on TON_IoT, 38% against 44%), so the control changed the reading but did not erase the result; against partial moves on TON_IoT, however, the copy control was better (6% against 11%, in 3 of 3 seeds). The original study had no such control, but the corrected hardening claim did not depend on it. *Check:* rank attributions by contribution; state and vary explainer sample counts and seeds; give every intervention a control with the same perturbation but no explanation.

### 4.4 Measurement, baselines and statistics

**F14. Measurements that do not measure what is claimed.** The original lightweight-model study described latency as the mean over 10,000 single-sample forward passes. The code timed one call on a batch of 1,000 records, divided by 1,000, and repeated this 5 times, so "0.19 ms per sample" for the CNN and 0.06 ms for the forest were batch-amortised. Measured per record on one CPU thread, the most accurate gradient-boosted trees took 9.8 ms against 0.42 ms for the best CNN. These are single-record calls to scikit-learn's implementation, dominated by its per-tree loop (1,210 trees per record for the best TON_IoT model in seed 0); a compiled library (LightGBM) evaluated each tree of a single record 59–108 times faster (0.07–0.14 µs per tree; Study 1's latency results), so the lesson is to measure the implementation that will be deployed, not that trees are inherently slow. Model sizes were file sizes: the original forest's pickle was 99,118 KB, whereas a compact encoding of the same configuration takes 10,171 KB (forest pickles were 9.6–9.9× and boosting pickles 8–16× their compact size), and the CNN's Keras file was 349 KB against 94 KB of float32 weights. The claimed size ratio, "271×" and "constant across all datasets", was 284× on TON_IoT and 685× on UNSW-NB15 even by the study's own files. Metric definitions can mislead in the same way: the original adversarial study defined attack success differently in its text and in a figure caption, and the continual-learning study's forgetting score rewarded a collapsed model (F6). *Check:* measure what a deployment would see (per-record CPU latency, compact model size), state the method, and test that a reported quantity responds as expected to a known change.

**F15. Macro-F1 without an operating point.** The original studies reported macro-F1 and accuracy only. Macro-F1 averages over classes and says little about the benign false-positive rate, which determines alert volume [Sommer and Paxson 2010; Arp et al. 2022]. Reporting the benign false-positive rate changed the reading of two corrected studies. In the adversarial study, the threat-matched defence cost the TON_IoT CNN only −0.029 macro-F1 but raised its benign false-positive rate from 0.015 to 0.044 (adversarial training on all features: 0.084); every network defence raised it on both datasets, and once the undefended model was given the same false-positive rate none of the defences significantly reduced the share of TON_IoT attack flows missed or evaded (F11). In the continual-learning study, replay came within 0.050 of joint retraining in macro-F1 but ended with a benign false-positive rate of 0.132 against 0.032. At an assumed 1,000,000 benign flows a day, these rates mean 15,000 false alerts for the undefended CNN against 44,000 for the defended one, and 132,000 for replay against 32,000 for joint retraining. *Check:* report the benign false-positive rate, or detection at a fixed false-positive rate, and the implied alert volume at a stated base rate.

**F16. Weak, mismatched or untuned baselines.** Five of the original studies compared their designs with weak or mismatched references. The fusion study compared fusion only with its own branches; tuned tree ensembles on all features beat every fusion network on TON_IoT (+0.008 over late fusion) and UNSW-NB15 (+0.034) and tied on N-BaIoT, also after the fusion networks were tuned (best tuned network below the forest by +0.007 and +0.034); tuning early fusion alone matched the gain of late fusion (tuned late minus tuned early 0.000 on TON_IoT), and semantic and random groupings of the same sizes differed by only +0.001 and +0.006. The federated study's centralised reference came from a different recipe, and its untuned optimisers produced spurious findings: FedAdam's reported divergence was under-stepping, and SCAFFOLD's 10.8-point advantage over SGD FedAvg became +0.020 [−0.001, +0.042] (not significant) against a tuned one. The lightweight-model study compared a CNN with one large forest; against size-budgeted trees its conclusion did not hold: at the size of the largest int8 network with its input table (204 KB on TON_IoT and 145 KB on UNSW-NB15), the best tree model was more accurate than the best network, by 0.004 and 0.012, and from 201 KB on TON_IoT and 97 KB on UNSW-NB15 a tree model was significantly more accurate than every network. The continual-learning study had neither a joint-retraining bound nor a non-neural reference, and the integrated framework scored below Study 1's forest (0.957) and CNN (0.880). Reference models fail too: scikit-learn's histogram gradient boosting [Pedregosa et al. 2011], with early stopping as configured in the series, collapsed at the default learning rate of 0.1 in 6 of 20 TON_IoT runs (four leaf settings × five seeds), the worst at 0.334; the collapses were deterministic per seed and recurred in the adversarial re-run. Halving the learning rate removed most of them (worst run 0.798), but the cause was the early stopping: with `n_iter_no_change` = 20, as configured (the library default is 10), scikit-learn stops 20 iterations after the best internal-validation score and keeps those iterations, in which boosting had diverged. Evaluated at the best iteration, no run collapsed (worst TON_IoT run 0.942 at the default learning rate). *Check:* include a tuned tree ensemble on the same features, a capacity-matched single network and the simplest configuration that could explain a gain; tune and seed baselines with the same budget as the proposed method, and evaluate early-stopped models at their best validation iteration.

**F17. Single seeds, irreproducible headlines and pseudo-replication.** The original studies used one seed. The integrated framework's abstract reported macro-F1 0.746 on TON_IoT "(seed=42 primary run; multi-seed mean ± std across 3 seeds: 0.5251 ± 0.2559 […])". The spread was disclosed, but the headline came from a separate run: the multi-seed rerun of seed 42 scored 0.670, and the other seeds 0.740 and 0.165. The ± was a population standard deviation; the sample value is 0.313. Before adaptation, the framework predicted a single class (macro-F1 0.018). Seeds mattered elsewhere too: the success of the strongest realistic attack against the TON_IoT CNN had a standard deviation of 7.4 percentage points across five model seeds, where the original adversarial study's only replication, five attack restarts on one model, gave an attack-success standard deviation of 0.002 on TON_IoT. The first fusion re-run compared five seeds of the semantic split with ten random splits by a Mann–Whitney test, although the random splits reused the same seeds; the final version paired each semantic run with the random splits of the same seed. *Check:* at least five seeds for the main comparisons, headline numbers taken from the same runs as the reported spread, paired tests where runs share seeds, sample standard deviations, and a declared family of confirmatory tests.

### 4.5 Reporting

**F18. Numbers inconsistent with each other or with the code.** The original studies contained numbers that contradicted each other: the same baseline CNN had macro-F1 0.880, 0.889 and 0.935 in different tables of the adversarial study; the lightweight-model study gave two test-set sizes and SHAP values that do not appear in its result file; the adversarial study claimed that CCAT preserved rare-class detection while its own table showed the TON_IoT rare-class F1 falling to 0.267, and reported CCAT's CICIDS2017 macro-F1 as 0.531 in one place and 0.579 in another for the same setting; the integrated framework described a gap of 13.5 points as "within 5–7 pp". Text also contradicted code (F12, F14). In the corrected studies every number is generated from result files; the numbers audits of Studies 5 and 6 still found a handful of errors per round, all in prose, captions, or tables and figures drawn from superseded result directories. *Check:* generate every number in the text from result files, check qualitative statements with a script, and regenerate figures in the same build.

### 4.6 Checked and found immaterial or absent

**Preprocessing fitted with future data (tested, immaterial).** The original continual-learning study fitted feature selection and scaling on the full training split, which contains the classes of future tasks. Refitting on the first task only changed final macro-F1 by at most 0.029 across three datasets and three strategies. The only nominally significant difference was on TON_IoT joint retraining, where fitting on all training data, the original practice, scored lower than fitting on the first task only (all-data minus first-task-only −0.016, uncorrected p = 0.0084); a scaler fitted on the first task could have clipped later tasks' values, but only 0.002% of TON_IoT test values fell beyond the compression map's clipping limit, so this did not drive the difference. **Oversampling before the split (absent).** SMOTE before splitting is a common leak; the shared pipeline applied it to the training split only, so there was nothing to test. Both checks remain worth running: with features whose ranges shift across tasks the first outcome could differ, and preprocessing fitted with test data was common in the published code we audited (Section 5.3).

## 5 How the Failures Were Found

### 5.1 Detection routes

The 18 failure modes were found by six routes. **Reference models** found the training failures: a tree ensemble on the same features exposed F4 in three studies. **Code reading** by a reviewer comparing the methods section with the code found the framework and implementation failures (F5, F7, F12, F14, F13a and F13b) and the second layer of duplicates (F2); none is visible in results alone. **Controls added in the re-run** found the protocol failures: random feature groupings, copy controls (requested in review), natural partitions, problem-space attacks, oracle preprocessing and factorial runs (F10, F11, F13c, Section 4.6). **Numbers audits and scripted checks** found the inconsistencies of F18 and stale figures. **Seed spread** found the reference-model collapses of F16 and part of F17. The explainer itself found F1 and F3: SHAP ranked the row index first in the original study, which recorded the finding and kept the model. A finding is only useful if the protocol forces a response to it.

To compare the checks, we judged which of them would have flagged each mode, whether or not it was the route by which the failure was actually found; a check flags a mode if applying it as described in Table 5 to the original study would have produced a warning. By this judgement, code-versus-methods review flags the most modes, six (F2, F5, F7, F12, F13, F14), but it is also the most expensive check. A tree reference on the same features flags four: F4 (all three instances), F7 (a frozen model, which a majority-class baseline also flags), F16 and the single-class framework of F17. Seed repetition flags three (F8, the collapses in F16, F17) and scripted numbers one (F18). The tree reference costs minutes per configuration and was the only check that caught F4 in Studies 1 and 3, where the network trained past the first few epochs and looked healthy. In Studies 1, 6 and 7, a tuned tree on the same features contradicts the original conclusion on its own.

### 5.2 AI assistance as a factor

AI assistance played a part on both sides. In the original studies, an AI model generated most of the code and drafted the manuscripts under the authors' direction, without the checks of Section 3.2: no scripted numbers, no reference verification and no review with access to code and results. In the re-run, an AI model (Claude, Anthropic) again wrote most of the analysis code and drafted the manuscripts, and separate instances of the same model acted as reviewers. Most failures in Table 2 therefore originated in AI-generated code or text, original or re-run, and the comparison this paper can make is between AI-assisted work with and without systematic checks, not between human and AI authorship.

Several failures were introduced by AI-written code during the re-run: the inherited min–max scaling that broke the fusion and continual-learning networks (F4), the reliance on the Keras restore default (F5), the current-task tuning criterion (F9), the eight-decimal copy check (F2), the raw-coefficient LIME ranking (F13), the default learning rate of the reference trees (F16), and smaller bugs that did not reach a manuscript (a model file overwritten by a later experiment, a transfer attack that silently skipped one model). Each was caught before submission, by the tree reference, a reviewer instance, a scripted check or the seed spread.

Table 4 summarises what the reviewer instances raised in the 26 review reports kept for Studies 4 to 6, with outcomes compiled from the reports, the project log, response files and commits.

| Reviewer role | Raised | Fixed in full or part | Rejected or no action | Outcome not recorded | Changed a number or conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Referee | 160 | 99 | 1 | 60 | 36 |
| Numbers audit | 124 | 54 | 0 | 70 | 21 |
| Methods versus code | 148 | 86 | 3 | 58 | 34 |
| References | 34 | 24 | 0 | 10 | 0 |
| All | 466 | 263 | 4 | 198 | 91 |

One methods-versus-code item was superseded by a later rebuild of the code and appears in no outcome column, so that row and the All row sum to one less than the number raised. The table covers reviews made before this paper was written; Studies 1 and 2 have since been reviewed as well, and those rounds are not included.

Of the items whose outcome is recorded, 99% were fixed in full or in part and 1% were rejected or needed no action; at least 91 changed a reported number or conclusion. The low rejection rate should not be read as precision: an author instance may accept a reviewer instance's point too readily. The instances also missed failures that other routes caught: the inconsistency between the two copy definitions of Study 5, found while preparing this paper; the metric dependence of the identifier effect and the raw-coefficient LIME ranking, found only by a later fresh review; that 30 rather than 13 runs had reached the epoch cap in Study 6; the federated batch-normalisation swing, found by inspecting training curves; and the reuse of superseded runs in Study 6, found when a script crashed. Errors were also introduced while addressing review comments on this paper and caught by the next round.

The original drafts also contained references that do not exist (three in Study 5, two in Study 1). Fabricated references with plausible metadata are the documented pattern of language-model output [Walters and Wilder 2023; Bhattacharyya et al. 2023]; every reference in the corrected manuscripts, including this one, was resolved before submission.

Our hypothesis is that AI assistance changed the cost of checks more than the nature of failures: the AI-written code carried the defaults and inherited choices that a hurried human might also have carried, and the failures it introduced are ordinary ones in this catalogue, while systematic checks became cheap enough to run on every study and every round. Without a human-written comparison we cannot test this. Reviewer instances share the author model's training, so their errors may be correlated with the author's; we relied on result-based checks wherever one existed and treated a review that found nothing as weak evidence. We did not organise an independent human re-derivation of the findings. Instead, so that the headline findings can be checked without trusting either the authors or the reviewer instances, the artefact includes a script (`checks/reproduce_headline.py`) that reproduces the evidence for F4, F5, F7 and F17 from the original files in seconds on a CPU. For F5 it does not rely on reading the library source: it drives the installed Keras callback through three epochs in which early stopping never fires and shows that the model ends with the last epoch's weights rather than the best.

### 5.3 External check: published artefacts

To check whether the failure modes also occur in published work, we audited the public code of published intrusion-detection papers on UNSW-NB15 or TON_IoT. The selection rule was fixed before any code was read: search GitHub for repositories on these datasets, sort by stars, and take repositories in order that link to a peer-reviewed paper resolvable on Crossref and contain training or preprocessing code, stopping at nine. Of 404 repositories found, the first nine qualifying ones implement papers published between 2019 and 2026 in journals and conference proceedings. We report aggregate counts only and do not identify the papers: the aim is to show that the failure modes occur in published work, not to assess any one study, and a static reading of committed code cannot show which code produced a paper's published numbers. All use UNSW-NB15 or its NetFlow version, none the original TON_IoT release, and one repository does not contain its UNSW-NB15 preprocessing. Selection required that the repository link to or name the paper it implements; in five of the nine the account name matches an author or the authors' laboratory, and for the other four the repository does not say whether it is the authors' own release; counts for the five are given in parentheses where they differ in kind. The data bundled with one repository appear to be a synthetic test sample; its data-handling codes describe what the code does to whatever data it is given. An AI agent coded the audit by reading the committed code only, never running it, and recorded file and line for each finding. A second agent recoded the seven columns of the first pass independently; the codings agreed on 52 of 63 cells literally and on 60 of 63 after reconciling a difference in coding convention, and the second coding is used below. The main AI session, outside the coding agents, then re-checked 6 findings against the cited lines of the committed code (test-data preprocessing in three repositories and one software-fault or protocol finding in each of three others); all were confirmed, and no finding was checked by a person. The software-fault columns (F4, F5, F6 and F14) were added in a second pass, after the nine repositories had been selected and after our own results were known, and were coded by one agent only. A finding means that the committed code does something, not that the published numbers came from it; the audit shows that the failure modes exist in published work, not how common they are. The paper reports counts only; the list of audited repositories and the per-repository evidence, with file and line references, are available to the editor and reviewers on request and are not released publicly.

**Data handling.** None of the 8 repositories whose data handling could be assessed removed duplicates or checked for overlap between training and test data (F2; none of the 4 assessable confirmed author releases). All 8 that could be assessed kept the TTL features and none ablated them in any experiment (F3); TTL is a shipped feature of the benchmark, so the concern is reliance on it without an ablation, not its presence. Scaling or feature transforms were fitted with test data in 6 of 9 (the one repository without its UNSW-NB15 preprocessing was assessed on its other dataset): on all records before splitting, on training and test data together, or refitted on the test file. In our series this defect did not matter (Section 4.6), but it is common. Identifiers were less common than in our drafts: 2 of 8 assessable repositories kept identifier-like inputs (ports and timestamps in one; flags derived from IP addresses among the message features of a temporal-graph model, whose IP:port node identities are a standard design and are not counted), and none kept the UNSW-NB15 `id` column.

**Baselines and protocol.** None contains a tuned tree ensemble trained on the same features (F16): 4 include an untuned or fixed-parameter forest or boosting model, 2 only neural baselines and 3 no baseline code. The test set was used to select checkpoints, features or training restarts in 4 of 9 (F9), and a single seed or split in 5 of 9 (F17).

**Software faults.** The audit found demonstrated instances of two software faults (an F5 analogue and F14) and risk configurations for two (F4 and F6). Of the repositories with a neural network, 8 of 8 fed it heavy-tailed counts that were unscaled or only linearly scaled (min–max, standardised or robust-scaled, which preserve the heavy tail), with no log, power or quantile transform (F4; 3 of them with batch normalisation); whether this impaired their models cannot be determined statically. The exact Keras default of F5 appears in none, but of the repositories with early stopping or checkpointing, 3 of 5 evaluate weights other than the selected best: a best checkpoint saved and never reloaded, or best weights held by reference so that they track the live model. Of the repositories with batch normalisation and either federated averaging or early stopping, 2 of 3 average its running statistics across non-IID clients or early-stop on evaluation-mode metrics, configurations that can produce F6 but that we could not show did so, and of those that report timing, 1 of 4 divides one test pass by the batch size and calls the result an average (F14).

To size the duplicate and identifier effects under the random-split protocol several of these repositories use, we trained an untuned random forest on all features of the UNSW-NB15 training file, the same file the series split at random, with a stratified 70/30 split over three seeds. Without deduplication, 43.3% of test records had an exact copy in the training split. Keeping `id` raised macro-F1 by +0.013 to +0.021 without deduplication and by +0.037 to +0.041 with it. In F1, with ten selected features held fixed, the macro-F1 effect vanished after deduplication instead: the size of an identifier leak depends on the feature set and the model as well as on the metric.

## 6 A Checklist

Table 5 condenses the catalogue into checks, ordered by the stage of a study at which each applies, with an approximate cost and the failure modes each check would have flagged in this series. The automatable checks are implemented in a small library, `checks/ids_checks.py` in the repository, which takes plain arrays and does not depend on our pipeline: `identifier_columns` (names that look like identifiers or testbed fields), `exact_copies` (test rows seen in training, by exact float32 equality, with and without labels, and a clean-subset mask), `tree_reference` and `majority_f1` (an untuned random forest on the same features, which suffices to expose a failed network but should be tuned before it is used as a comparator, and a majority-class baseline), `flag_network` (a network far below the tree, near the majority baseline, peaking early or at the cap), `cap_share`, `grid_edge`, `rerun_spread`, `false_alarms_per_day`, and a Keras callback `RestoreBest` that always restores the best weights and records the restored epoch. The placeholder-and-verify build used for every manuscript is in the same repository.

**Questions to ask of a published or vendor ML-IDS result.** Several failure modes translate directly into deployment risk. Before relying on a reported score, an operator can ask:

- How does the model compare with a tuned tree ensemble trained on the same features? (F4, F16)
- What is the benign false-positive rate, and how many alerts a day does it imply at our traffic volume? (F15)
- How much of the score survives removing duplicates, identifiers and testbed fields such as TTL and ports? (F1–F3)
- Was robustness evaluated against attacks an adversary can carry out on real traffic, with a gradient-free search? (F11)
- What is the per-record latency of the implementation to be deployed, on the target hardware? (F14)
- Was the model evaluated on each site's own traffic or only on a pooled test set? (F10)
- Are the numbers means over several seeds, with their spread, from the same runs as the headline? (F17)
- Does the test split respect time or site, or were records split at random? (open: Section 7)

## 7 Limitations

The evidence comes from one series by one group, on three public benchmarks and one laptop, and the series was unpublished, AI-drafted and plausibly weaker than published work; other groups and settings will have other failure modes and different effect sizes. The external audit (Section 5.3) is static, small and limited to artefacts with public code, which may differ from papers without code, and it was coded by AI agents, with 6 findings re-checked by a further AI session and none by a person. The catalogue is limited to failures that were found: our detection routes were the ones we used, so failures that none of them could catch are missing, and the counts describe this series, not the field. Effect sizes are specific to the datasets and models. Some mechanisms are inferred rather than isolated, notably the cause of the original first-task collapse (F6). All evaluation used random splits of static benchmarks; we did not test temporal or cross-dataset splits, which the literature identifies as a further source of optimism [Pendlebury et al. 2019; D'hooge et al. 2020]. Finally, the reviewers were instances of the same AI model that wrote much of the re-run code; we discuss this in Section 5.2, but we cannot measure how many failures a different reviewer would have found. The same applies to this paper: it was drafted by that model, reviewed by instances of it and its external audit was coded by its agents, and no person re-derived the catalogue; the reproduction script covers a subset of the modes. The model versions used for the original studies were not recorded; the re-runs, reviews, audit and this paper used Claude models through Claude Code between October and November 2026, without the version of each session being recorded. Two of the changed conclusions rest partly on three seeds: Study 5's analyses and the private-training result of Study 4. Of the 466 review items, 198 have no recorded outcome; 263 (56%) of all items are known to have been fixed in full or in part, and the acceptance rate quoted in Section 5.2 refers to the items with a recorded outcome.

## 8 Conclusion

Re-running six intrusion-detection studies with stricter controls changed the main conclusion of five of them. The failures behind the changes were mostly ordinary: an identifier and duplicates in the data, a network silently broken by input scaling, a framework default, a private-training loop that did nothing, timing and size measurements that measured something else, test-set selection, weak baselines, macro-F1 without an operating point, and single seeds. Several recurred during the re-run despite the authors looking for them, and were caught only because cheap checks ran on every study. The best check for its cost was a tree ensemble trained on the same features as every neural model; code-versus-methods review caught more modes, at far higher cost. We suggest that authors and reviewers of machine-learning intrusion detection papers ask for it, along with seeds, validation-only selection, the benign false-positive rate, and numbers generated from released result files.
