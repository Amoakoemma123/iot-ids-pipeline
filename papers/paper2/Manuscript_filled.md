## Title

**What Can an Attacker Actually Change? Evasion and Hardening of Compact Flow-Based Intrusion Detectors Under a Realistic Threat Model**

## Abstract

Robustness studies of network intrusion detectors usually perturb every flow feature within an L∞ ball, including fields an attacker cannot set. We evaluate compact detectors under a problem-space threat model: the attacker controls only its own flows (padding, extra packets, delays and, on UNSW-NB15, the initial TTL), derived features are recomputed and success counts only detected attack flows. On TON-IoT and UNSW-NB15 we attack a 1D-CNN, an MLP, a random forest and gradient-boosted trees (five seeds) with black-box, adaptive, white-box and transfer attacks. Even restricted to the volume and timing features, a feature-space attack at ε = 0.005 evaded more detected flows than the realistic attack on every model except the UNSW-NB15 trees, partly by ignoring dependencies between features. On TON-IoT the strongest realistic attack evaded 51.4% of the CNN's detections and 25.2% of the random forest's, mostly by padding small flows up to the budget's floors; on UNSW-NB15 the trees were harder to evade only while the TTL features were in the model. Every network defence raised the benign false-positive rate; at the undefended model's rate and decision rule, none significantly reduced the share of TON-IoT attack flows missed or evaded (adversarial training on the attacker's actions: 38.3% against 46.9%), and TRADES increased it, while on UNSW-NB15 the action-space defences cut it from 70.0% to 20.4%–22.2%. Retraining once on modified attack flows cut evasion of gradient-boosted trees on TON-IoT from 35.3% to 3.0% at no accuracy cost, but on UNSW-NB15 unseen TTL values still evaded 30.8%–40.3%.

**Keywords:** intrusion detection; Internet of Things; adversarial machine learning; evasion attacks; problem-space attacks; adversarial training; tree ensembles

## 1. Introduction

Machine-learning intrusion detectors for Internet of Things (IoT) and enterprise networks are often trained on flow records: statistics of each connection such as its duration, the bytes and packets sent in each direction, and protocol and state fields. An attacker who knows that such a detector is in place has an obvious move: change the attack traffic so that its flow record looks benign. Studies of this threat often borrow the methods of adversarial machine learning on images: they perturb every feature of the record within a small L∞ ball and report how often the prediction changes (e.g. Ibitoye et al., 2019).

For network traffic this model is unrealistic in two directions. Many features cannot be changed by the attacker at all: the destination's responses, the service and protocol of the victim, the connection state and the victim's port are fixed by the attack itself, and one-hot encoded categorical fields cannot take fractional values. Other features can be changed by far more than a small ε, at a cost: an attacker can pad its packets, send extra packets or slow down, and on some networks can choose the initial time-to-live (TTL) of its packets. Derived features (loads, mean packet sizes, header byte counts) must change consistently with these actions. An evaluation that ignores both directions can overstate vulnerability, by allowing impossible changes, and understate it, by forbidding easy large ones (Pierazzi et al., 2020; Apruzzese et al., 2022). The way success is counted matters as well: an attack flow that the detector misses anyway, or a benign flow that the attack makes look malicious, is not an evasion.

Several studies have restricted the attacker accordingly (Section 2). For compact detectors of the kind proposed for IoT gateways, three questions are still open. How much do the conventional and the realistic threat model disagree, in which direction, and does the answer hold when derived features are recomputed under physical constraints and both attacks are made strong? Are tree ensembles, often the most accurate models on these benchmarks, also the hardest to evade when they are attacked with the same gradient-free search as networks, and can they be hardened cheaply against attacks they were not trained on? And how much of the apparent vulnerability, and of the apparent effect of defences, comes from a known artefact of the dataset or from a shifted decision threshold rather than from the detectors?

Our contributions are:

1. A problem-space threat model for the flow records of TON-IoT, an IoT testbed, and UNSW-NB15, an enterprise-network testbed: the attacker adds payload bytes, packets and delays and, on UNSW-NB15, sets the TTL of its packets; the action-dependent features are recomputed (header bytes for each added packet, delays only on flows with at least two packets once added packets are counted, acknowledgement delay only after a handshake), and success means that a detected attack flow is classified as benign.
2. An evaluation of a compact CNN, an MLP, a random forest and gradient-boosted trees with a gradient-free search that applies to all four, an adaptive score-based search, strong white-box attacks and transfer, checked for saturation, against a feature-space reference that is itself strengthened (black-box search of the ball, the features the attacker can change and increases only, and gradients through a straight-through (BPDA) quantile transform; Athalye et al., 2018).
3. A comparison of feature-space adversarial training (on all classes, and class-conditional training that leaves rare classes unperturbed), adversarial training and TRADES on the attacker's actions, a control with the same training loop but no adversarial examples, and adversarial augmentation of tree ensembles, at each model's own operating point and at a matched benign false-positive rate, with a small primary family of tests and with candidates, TTLs and actions not used in training.
4. A separation of the UNSW-NB15 TTL artefact from properties of the detectors, including detectors trained without the TTL features.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Adversarial attacks on network intrusion detection

Early studies of adversarial examples against learned intrusion detectors applied image-domain attacks such as the fast gradient sign method (Goodfellow et al., 2015), projected gradient descent (PGD; Madry et al., 2018) and the Carlini–Wagner attack (Carlini & Wagner, 2017) to the feature vectors of flow records, without restricting which features change; Ibitoye et al. (2019), for example, attack feed-forward detectors on Bot-IoT in this way. Surveys of adversarial machine learning in security note that such feature-space attacks may not correspond to anything an attacker can do with real traffic (Rosenberg et al., 2021), and Apruzzese et al. (2022) propose a taxonomy of realistic attacker capabilities (knowledge, feedback and whether manipulation happens in the problem space or the feature space). Pierazzi et al. (2020) formalise problem-space attacks: transformations the attacker can actually perform, which must preserve semantics, be plausible and survive the pre-processing, and whose side effects on other features must be accounted for. From case studies and a review of security-conference papers, Apruzzese et al. (2023) argue that real attackers rarely compute gradients and that threat models should be precise and cost-driven.

Several studies restrict the attacker accordingly. Apruzzese et al. (2019) increase only the duration, bytes and packets of botnet flows, update the derived features and count evasion over malicious flows, against tree and neural classifiers. Debicha et al. (2023) modify only duration, bytes and packets in a black-box transfer attack, recompute dependent features and attack an MLP, a random forest and k-nearest neighbours. Chernikova and Oprea (2022) attack a botnet detector with gradients that respect feature dependencies. Alhajjar et al. (2021) restrict evolutionary and generative attacks to mutable features of NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15, many of them increase-only, and report the share of malicious records classified as normal for eleven models including tree ensembles. Han et al. (2021) go further and mutate real packets in the traffic space, measuring evasion over traffic that was detected before the attack. Sheatsley et al. (2022) derive constraints from TCP/IP rules and show that constraint-obeying attacks still reach high misclassification rates on NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15, and Teuffenbach et al. (2020) weight features by how hard they are to manipulate. Vitorino et al. (2022) generate realistic adversarial flows with class-specific perturbation patterns and use them to attack and adversarially train MLP and random-forest detectors on CIC-IDS2017 and IoT-23, and Vitorino et al. (2023) apply the method to IoT-23 and Bot-IoT with a random forest, two gradient-boosting ensembles and an isolation forest, finding the tree ensembles susceptible and adversarial training beneficial. Simonetto et al. (2022) propose a common framework for attacks and adversarial training under feature constraints.

These studies differ in how success is counted. Some count any misclassification, including an attack labelled as a different attack (Sheatsley et al., 2022); others count only attack records classified as benign (Apruzzese et al., 2019; Alhajjar et al., 2021; Han et al., 2021; Chernikova & Oprea, 2022). Only the latter corresponds to evasion of an alert. Finally, benchmark artefacts can make an attack look powerful: in UNSW-NB15, the TTL and protocol fields separate benign from malicious flows almost perfectly, and simply setting them to benign values matches a published constrained attack (Flood et al., 2024).

### 2.2 Defences and tree models

Adversarial training with PGD (Madry et al., 2018) and its trade-off-aware variant TRADES (Zhang et al., 2019) are the standard defences for neural networks. Under class imbalance, adversarial training widens the gap between frequent and rare classes (Wu et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2021); leaving the rare classes unperturbed during training is a simple imbalance-aware baseline, which we call class-conditional adversarial training (CCAT). Defences must be evaluated with attacks adapted to them (Carlini et al., 2019; Tramèr et al., 2020). In security domains, Dyrmishi et al. (2023) found that hardening with unrealistic, feature-space examples protected a botnet detector against a realistic attack, but not models in other domains; whether this holds for IoT flows, and whether adversarial training restricted to what the attacker can actually change does better, is open.

Tree ensembles, among the strongest models on many intrusion-detection benchmarks, have no gradients. They can be attacked exactly with mixed-integer programming (Kantchelian et al., 2016) or with decision-based black-box attacks (Brendel et al., 2018; Cheng et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2020), and hardened by adversarial boosting (Kantchelian et al., 2016), robust split criteria (Chen et al., 2019; Vos & Verwer, 2021) or, for flow-based detectors, by a dedicated hardening method for random forests (Apruzzese et al., 2020) and adversarial training with realistic examples (Vitorino et al., 2022, 2023). In intrusion detection they are often attacked only by transfer or by feature-space search. When the attacker's actions span only three or four dimensions, as in our threat model, a dense search over the action space evaluates every model on equal terms without gradients, and an adaptive search can refine it.

## 3. Threat Model

**Attacker.** The attacker is the source of a malicious flow and wants the detector to classify that flow as benign. It controls only its own sending behaviour. It can add bytes to the payload it sends (padding), send additional packets, delay its packets, which lengthens the flow, and delay its acknowledgements. On UNSW-NB15, whose features include the source TTL observed at the monitor, it can also choose the initial TTL of its packets, as any host with raw-socket access or a configurable network stack can; the observed value is the initial value minus the hops to the monitor, so any value from 1 to 255 can be reached. It cannot remove bytes or packets, because the attack payload must still be delivered, and it cannot change how the destination responds, the service, protocol or connection state of the flow, or the victim's port. A delay requires at least two packets once any added packets are counted, and an acknowledgement delay requires a TCP handshake (we apply it only to flows with a non-zero ackdat). These restrictions are conservative: extra packets may in practice draw extra acknowledgements from the destination, which we do not model.

**Knowledge.** We consider four levels. In the white-box setting the attacker knows the model, its pre-processing and gradients. In the decision-based black-box setting it can query the deployed detector with modified flows and observe the predicted class; in the score-based setting it also observes the class probabilities, which our adaptive search uses. In the transfer setting it computes actions on a surrogate. Our surrogate is a CNN trained on the same data, features and pre-processing, so the transfer setting is close to white-box knowledge of the training data and only withholds the target model.

**Budget.** For a flow with b source bytes, p source packets, duration d and ACK delay k, the attacker may add up to β(b + 1,000) payload bytes, β(p + 10) packets, β(d + 1 s) seconds of delay and β(k + 0.1 s) of ACK delay, for a relative budget β ∈ {0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2}. The additive floors allow flows with no payload, such as scans, to be padded at all; for small flows they dominate the budget, so we also report results with the floors removed, halved and doubled. Choosing the TTL has no budget. Because the TTL field almost separates benign from malicious flows in UNSW-NB15 (Flood et al., 2024), an evasion obtained by the TTL choice exploits a dataset artefact rather than a property of the detectors; we therefore report volume-and-timing actions and the TTL choice separately, treat the former as the main result on UNSW-NB15, and also train detectors without the TTL features.

**Feature consistency.** Every selected feature that depends on the actions is recomputed (Table 2). On TON-IoT, whose byte counts are payload bytes, the source bytes, packets and duration change directly, and the source IP bytes grow by the added payload plus, for each added packet, the flow's header bytes per packet, estimated as (source IP bytes − source bytes)/packets and clipped to 20–60 bytes (40 bytes for flows without packets). On UNSW-NB15, whose source byte count includes headers (at least 28 bytes per packet in the data), each added packet also adds 40 bytes, or the flow's mean packet size if that is smaller (but at least 28 bytes); the mean source packet size is recomputed from bytes and packets; the source load is rescaled by the change in bytes and duration and the destination load by the change in duration, because the destination sends the same bytes over a longer flow; the ACK delay changes directly; and the TTL-derived state feature (ct_state_ttl) is looked up from the training data for the new TTL, the destination TTL and the connection state, using the nearest training TTL for values not seen in training. The recomputed raw features then pass through the deployed min–max scaling and, for the networks, the quantile transform. With all actions zero, this mapping reproduces the pipeline's features to within 3.4e-04, a rounding effect. The main evaluation allows a fractional number of added packets and does not bound the payload per packet; Section 5.1 reports a check with integer packets and a maximum transmission unit (MTU).

**Success.** An attack succeeds on a flow if the flow is an attack, the model detects it without modification (predicts any attack class), and the modified flow is classified as benign. The attack success rate (ASR) is the share of detected attack flows that the attack turns into benign predictions, computed on a fixed, class-stratified sample of 5,000 attack test flows per dataset, the same for every model. Because the sample is stratified, the ASR is close to an average over attack classes; the supplementary material also gives it weighted by the prevalence of each class in the test set (Table S3). Because the ASR is taken over detected flows only, we also report the end-to-end evasion: the share of all sampled attack flows classified benign after the attack, missed or evaded.

## 4. Data, Models and Attacks

### 4.1 Data
TON-IoT (Alsaedi et al., 2020; Moustafa, 2021) and UNSW-NB15 (Moustafa & Slay, 2015) are pre-processed by the pipeline released with the code: identifier and free-text columns removed, categorical fields with at most 30 values one-hot encoded, duplicates removed after all column drops, a stratified 70/15/15 split with a cross-split duplicate guard, feature selection (mutual information and recursive feature elimination with cross-validation) and min–max scaling fitted on the training split, and SMOTE oversampling of the training split (Chawla et al., 2002). This leaves 25 features on TON-IoT and 10 on UNSW-NB15, the latter including both TTL features (sttl and ct_state_ttl). The feature set is selected on clean data before any attack and therefore also fixes the attack surface. Attacks start from the raw, unscaled test records, so that actions are applied in natural units (bytes, packets, seconds) before the deployed pre-processing. Classes with fewer than 2% of the training records before oversampling are treated as rare by CCAT: mitm on TON-IoT and analysis, backdoor, shellcode, worms on UNSW-NB15. To check that results do not depend on one split, the main models are also trained and attacked on two further stratified splits of each dataset, with the same selected features and the scaling and oversampling refitted. On UNSW-NB15 we also train detectors without sttl and ct_state_ttl. We do not use N-BaIoT here: its features are statistics over sliding time windows of a device's traffic, so the effect of changing one flow cannot be mapped onto them without the packet traces.

### 4.2 Models
We attack four compact detectors. The 1D-CNN has two convolutional blocks (32 and 64 filters, kernel 3, batch normalisation, max-pooling), global average pooling and dense layers of 128 and 64 units with dropout 0.3; the MLP has dense layers of 128 and 64 units with batch normalisation and a 64-unit layer with dropout 0.3. Both are trained on the selected features after a quantile transform to the uniform distribution (1,000 quantiles), with Adam (learning rate 10⁻³, halved after 5 epochs without improvement of the validation loss), batch size 256, at most 100 epochs and early stopping on the validation loss (patience 15), restoring the weights of the best epoch. The random forest (RF) has 100 trees of maximum depth 16 with √d features per split, and the gradient-boosted trees (GBDT; scikit-learn's histogram-based implementation) have at most 31 leaves per tree, learning rate 0.05 and up to 1,000 iterations, with scikit-learn's internal early stopping on 10% of the training data (at the default learning rate of 0.1 the same model collapsed to low macro-F1 on some seeds in preliminary runs on these data); both are trained on the min–max scaled features, on which the quantile transform would have no effect. Undefended models are trained with five seeds (0–4). Seeds vary initialisation and the randomness of the trees but not the data; the resplits above vary the data. Each model predicts the class of highest probability; Section 4.5 describes how we also set a benign threshold.

### 4.3 Attacks
All attacks are run against every trained model, including every defended model, so they are adapted to the defence (Tramèr et al., 2020). The random points of the evaluation grids are drawn with seeds disjoint from those used to build the tree augmentation; the corners of the budget box and the four TTL candidates are the same in both, so the TTL sweep and the adaptive search with any TTL are the tests with values the augmentation did not see.

- **Black-box grid search** (decision-based). For each flow and budget, the search evaluates all corners of the budget box (each action at zero or at its maximum; 8 on TON-IoT, 16 on UNSW-NB15) and 120 (TON-IoT) or 48 (UNSW-NB15) random points inside it, scaled to the flow's budget. On UNSW-NB15 each candidate is combined with the unchanged TTL and with each of the 4 most frequent non-zero TTLs of benign training flows (31, 254, 62, 1), giving 128 queries per flow and budget on TON-IoT and 320 on UNSW-NB15. The flow evades if any candidate is classified benign. We report volume-and-timing actions with the TTL unchanged (volume only), the TTL choice with all other actions at zero (TTL only), and both together; per-action variants (bytes only, packets only, delays only); and, at β = 0.5, the search with 1,000 further random points per flow. The grid of each budget is the same unit grid scaled to that budget, so the candidates of a larger budget do not contain those of a smaller one.
- **TTL sweep** (UNSW-NB15): the TTL-only attack with each value from 1 to 255.
- **Adaptive search** (score-based): from a random start in the budget box, 40 rounds of 16 proposals per flow (Gaussian steps with a step size decaying from 0.3 to 0.02 of the box, random snaps of one action to zero or its maximum), keeping the proposal with the highest benign probability; on UNSW-NB15 the TTL is either kept, drawn from the candidates or drawn from 1–255. It is run, at β = 0.5 and 2, on the flows that the grid search did not evade, and reported together with the grid (grid + adaptive). It is a score-based random search in the spirit of the Square Attack (Andriushchenko et al., 2020), used in AutoAttack (Croce & Hein, 2020), adapted to a box of three or four actions.
- **White-box action PGD** (networks): sign-gradient ascent on the actions, scaled to the budget box, through the feature map, the min–max scaling and a differentiable implementation of the quantile transform, for each TTL choice. A basic version (log-probability of the benign class, one random start, 50 steps of 5% of the box) is run at every budget; a strong version (margin between the benign class and the best other class, restarts from the zero and full corners and two random points, 100 steps with a step decaying from 10% to 1% of the box, success checked every 25 steps) at β = 0.5 and 2.
- **Transfer**: actions computed by the basic white-box PGD on the undefended CNN of the same seed (β = 0.5 and 1), applied to the undefended MLP, RF and GBDT.

**Strongest attack.** Our main measure at β = 0.5 counts a flow as evaded if any of the following evades it: the grid search (with and without the TTL candidates), the grid with 1,000 further points, the three adaptive searches and, for the networks, the strong white-box PGD. Adding the TTL sweep, a 24-point grid and the per-action grids raised the strongest ASR of any model by at most 0.8 pp; the basic PGD and transfer are also left out, and their ASRs were below those of the attacks included. For the networks we also ran the strong PGD with the gradient taken through the straight-through transform described below (Section 5.1).

**Saturation and feasibility checks.** On the trees, for which no white-box attack exists, we attack 500 detected flows per seed (seeds 0–2) with regular grids of increasing resolution (up to 17³ points on TON-IoT and 7⁴ on UNSW-NB15, each with every TTL candidate) and a longer adaptive search (150 rounds of 32 proposals, any TTL) on the flows nothing else evaded. We did not run an exact attack on the trees, so their ASRs remain lower bounds. To check physical feasibility, we repeat the grid search at β = 0.5 with the number of added packets rounded to an integer and with enough packets added for the payload to fit within 1,460 payload bytes per packet (TON-IoT) or 1,500 bytes per packet including headers (UNSW-NB15), discarding candidates that would need more packets than the budget allows.

**Conventional reference.** We compare with attacks in min–max feature space, which are not realisable and serve only for comparison. The basic reference is targeted L∞ PGD (ε ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.1}, 50 steps, through the differentiable quantile transform) on all selected features and on the mutable features only (the action-dependent volume and timing features). Because a black-box search of the same ball beat this attack by a wide margin (Section 5.1), we strengthened the reference for ε ∈ {0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1}: a black-box search over 128 random corners of the full ball; an exhaustive search over the patterns {−ε, 0, +ε} on the mutable features and over the increase-only patterns {0, +ε}; and, for the networks, a strong PGD (margin loss, restarts from the record, a random point and a random corner, step decaying from 0.25ε to 0.025ε) through a straight-through approximation of the quantile transform (backward-pass differentiable approximation, BPDA; Athalye et al., 2018), on the full ball and on the mutable features. The strongest feature-space attack on the mutable features counts a flow as evaded if the exhaustive search or the BPDA PGD on those features evades it.

### 4.4 Defences
The network defences train a fresh network with a custom loop: at most 30 epochs of 200 steps (Adam, learning rate 10⁻³), early stopping on the clean validation loss (patience 5) and the best weights restored. Each step combines a clean oversampled batch of 256 records and 128 further records with equal loss weight.

- **Clean loop** (control): the further records are real training attack records without modification. This separates the effect of the training loop and of the extra weight on attack records from that of the adversarial examples; because the extra records are attacks, the control also shifts the class prior towards attacks. Every network defence is compared with it as well as with the undefended model.
- **AT-all**: untargeted PGD adversarial training in min–max feature space on records of all classes (ε = 0.05, 7 steps of ε/4, random start), through the differentiable quantile transform. A variant (AT-all, BPDA) uses the straight-through transform of Section 4.3 for the inner maximisation, to check whether AT-all was trained against a weak inner attack.
- **CCAT**: as AT-all, but classes with fewer than 2% of the training records are trained without perturbation.
- **AT-threat**: adversarial training matched to the threat model: action-space PGD towards benign (β = 0.5, 7 sign steps of 0.05 of the box from a uniform random start) applied to real (not oversampled) training attack flows only. On UNSW-NB15 each flow keeps its TTL with probability 0.5 and otherwise takes one of the TTL candidates. AT-threat is applied to both the CNN and the MLP; the other network defences to the CNN only.
- **TRADES** (Zhang et al., 2019): cross-entropy on the clean batch plus 6 × the KL divergence between predictions on clean and action-perturbed attack flows, the perturbation found by 7 steps of action-space ascent on that divergence (with the same TTL choice as AT-threat). TRADES is therefore an action-space defence.
- **Tree augmentation**: for RF and GBDT, the most benign-looking black-box candidate (β = 0.5; box corners and 24 random points) of each of up to 20,000 real training attack flows is added to the training set with its true label, and the model is retrained once. On UNSW-NB15 each flow keeps its TTL with probability 0.5 and otherwise takes one of the TTL candidates. Three variants test generalisation: a second round, in which the search is repeated against the augmented model on a second random sample of training attack flows and the model is retrained with both sets; and augmentation without delay actions or without byte padding, attacked with all actions (held-out action).

The undefended models, the CNN defences and the augmented trees are trained with five seeds; the MLP with AT-threat, AT-all (BPDA), the further augmentation variants and the detectors without TTL features with three (0–2). To check that the comparison does not hinge on one configuration, AT-all was also trained with ε = 0.02 and 0.1 and AT-threat with β = 0.25 and 1 (seed 0; Table S10).

### 4.5 Evaluation
Clean performance is measured on the full test set: macro-F1, per-class F1 (in particular of the rare classes) and the benign false-positive rate (FPR). Robustness is measured by the ASR of Section 3. Numbers are means over seeds with their standard deviation; for the main attacks we give 95% bootstrap confidence intervals that resample seeds and then the attacked flows within each seed.

**Operating point.** A defence that makes a model flag more traffic makes it harder to evade into "benign" without making it more robust. We therefore also compare the models at a matched operating point under one decision rule: a flow is classified benign if its benign probability is at least a threshold set on the validation benign flows. For the undefended model the threshold gives its own validation FPR; for a defended model of the same family and seed it gives the undefended model's FPR (and, separately, 1%). The undefended reference is thus evaluated under the same rule as the defences rather than with the class of highest probability, a change that on its own alters evasion (Section 5.3). At each threshold we attack the flows the thresholded model detects with the grid search and, for the networks, the adaptive search (β = 0.5), and report the ASR and the share of all sampled attack flows classified benign after the attack (missed or evaded), which also counts attacks that a model detects less often at the matched threshold. On UNSW-NB15 the main comparison uses volume and timing actions only, because the TTL is a dataset artefact (Section 5.4); Table S11 gives all actions.

**Tests.** A defended model and the undefended model of the same seed share their initialisation, so defences are compared with paired t-tests over the common seeds. We specified a primary family of 12 tests per dataset, Holm-adjusted (Holm, 1979): each network defence against its undefended model and CNN AT-threat against each other CNN defence, on the share missed or evaded at the matched FPR (volume and timing only on UNSW-NB15); and each tree augmentation against its undefended tree, on the strongest-attack ASR (augmentation leaves the FPR unchanged). The family was first specified before the operating-point experiments, with the ASR as endpoint and the undefended model under the class of highest probability as reference; we revised the reference and the endpoint once, after those results, when we found that the comparison mixed two decision rules and ignored attacks that a model stops detecting at the matched threshold. The revised family is reported, and the ASR at the matched FPR is given beside it (Table 7). All other defence comparisons (118 on TON-IoT and 236 on UNSW-NB15: clean macro-F1 and further attacks against the undefended model, against the clean loop and between defences) form a secondary family per dataset with its own Holm adjustment, for which we also give unadjusted p-values. Network and tree models are compared pairwise with Welch's t-test (each network against each tree), Holm-adjusted within each dataset and attack (four tests). Experiments ran on one laptop; the networks were trained on its GPU (NVIDIA GTX 1650, 4 GB).

**The feature-space reference.** Table 5 compares the realistic volume-and-timing attack with the strengthened feature-space attack. The black-box search over corners of the full ball evaded every detected flow of every TON-IoT model at ε = 0.01, the trees included, far more than the white-box ε-PGD commonly used as the reference (for the CNN, 73.8% at ε = 0.01); a black-box attack that beats a white-box one points to gradients that are uninformative, not to robustness (Carlini et al., 2019). The full ball is unfair to the comparison, because it changes features the attacker cannot touch. Restricted to the mutable volume and timing features, the feature-space attack still evaded more detected flows than the realistic attack at the smallest ε we tried (0.005) on every TON-IoT model and on both UNSW-NB15 networks, and so it did even when only increases were allowed. Only on the UNSW-NB15 trees did the ball have to be larger before it overtook the realistic attack (smallest ε: 0.02 for the RF and 0.01 for the GBDT; increases only: > 0.1 and 0.02). The ball is not small: 0.005 of the min–max range of a heavy-tailed byte count can exceed a flow's whole volume, and after the networks' quantile transform it moves a feature across a large part of its distribution. The gap has two sources. One is magnitude: on TON-IoT the interval that a mutable feature can reach under the realistic actions had a median width of at most 4e-04 in min–max units, far below the smallest ball, while on UNSW-NB15 it reached 1e-01 (Table 5). The other is consistency: setting each mutable feature independently within its realistic interval, which keeps realistic magnitudes but breaks the dependencies between derived features, raised evasion above the realistic attack by +10.0 pp for the TON-IoT CNN and by up to 16.1 pp for the networks, but by only +0.5 pp and +1.3 pp for the RF. On UNSW-NB15 the realistic actions can also lower some mutable features (sload, smean and dload), so there the increases-only ball is not a relaxation of the realistic attack. In short, the feature-space attack overstated what volume and timing changes achieve for every model and every ε we tested, except the UNSW-NB15 trees at the smallest ε (and, for the RF with increases only, at every ε up to 0.1), and for the networks a sizeable part of the overstatement came from ignoring how features depend on each other.

**How strong are the realistic attacks?** The grid search was close to saturation on TON-IoT and less so on UNSW-NB15 (Table S1): 1,000 further random points per flow added at most 4.7 pp on TON-IoT and 9.8 pp on UNSW-NB15, and the adaptive search added 0.6–9.5 pp and 2.7–13.5 pp with the TTL kept. On the networks, the strong white-box action PGD improved on the basic version (by +12.6 pp for the CNN on TON-IoT) but stayed 0.4–5.8 percentage points below the grid search for both networks on both datasets, so gradients through the piecewise-linear quantile transform are weak here too; we therefore use the strongest attack, which includes the black-box searches, as the main measure. Taking the gradient through the straight-through (BPDA) transform did not change this: the strong action PGD with BPDA gradients evaded at most 0.1% of detected flows that the strongest attack had not, on any network, undefended or defended (Table S12). On the trees, finer regular grids and a longer adaptive search raised the ASR on a subsample by at most 3.8 pp on TON-IoT (Table S8), but by up to 10.3 pp on UNSW-NB15, where the strongest attack on the GBDT is therefore a loose lower bound. Integer packets and the MTU bound changed the grid-search ASR of every model by at most 1.1 pp (Table S9).

**Which actions matter?** Success grew with the budget for every model except the UNSW-NB15 RF, whose volume-only grid-search ASR peaked at β = 0.25 (12.9%, against 10.6% at β = 2; Figure 1); because the grids of different budgets are not nested, a falling curve signals an unsaturated search rather than robustness at larger budgets. No single action sufficed on TON-IoT: bytes alone and packets alone evaded the CNN on 12.7% and 11.1%, delays alone on 0.9%, and the three together on 47.9% (Table S2). On UNSW-NB15 delays alone evaded the networks (30.8% for the CNN), but hardly the trees (1.0% and 2.0%). The additive floors of the budget decide most of the TON-IoT result: with the budget purely proportional to the flow's own volume (floors × 0), the volume-only ASR at β = 0.5 fell to 3.0–8.5% of detected flows on TON-IoT, against 5.9–42.8% on UNSW-NB15. Most TON-IoT evasions therefore pad small flows by the absolute part of the budget (up to 500 bytes, 5 packets and 0.5 s at β = 0.5; Table S4 gives the cheapest successful actions); whether that overhead is acceptable depends on the attack.

**Which attack classes evade?** On TON-IoT the classes differed more than the models (Table S6). Ransomware and scanning flows, which are small, evaded every model on at least 75.5% and 76.8% of detections, while the RF was almost never evaded on backdoor, injection, password and XSS flows (0.2%–1.7%). On UNSW-NB15, with the TTL choice, every class was evaded on at least 74.0% of detections for the networks and the RF; the GBDT resisted more on reconnaissance (47.0%) and worms (48.4%).

**Queries and missed attacks.** Against the undefended models the median evading flow was evaded by the 4th query of the grid search on TON-IoT (CNN; the first query, all actions zero, is the unmodified flow and cannot succeed) and, on UNSW-NB15, by the 9th (CNN) and the 65th (RF and GBDT); for the trees this is the first candidate with a changed TTL. Each failed query is itself a detected attack flow, so query access is a strong assumption, and against a flow that the cheaper attacks miss the strongest attack uses up to 1,769 queries on TON-IoT and 7,243 on UNSW-NB15; transfer (Section 5.2) needs none. Because the ASR counts only attacks detected before modification, it understates the end result for models that miss many attacks: on UNSW-NB15, 72.4% of all sampled attack flows were classified benign after the volume-only attack on the CNN, against an ASR of 61.5% (Table S3).

### 5.2 Networks against trees
Under the realistic attacks the trees were harder to evade than the networks, with qualifications (Table 6). On TON-IoT each network was evaded more than each tree under every realistic attack in Table 6, by +16.2 pp (CNN against GBDT) to +39.7 pp (MLP against RF) for the strongest attack (Welch's t-test over seeds, Holm-adjusted p ≤ 0.005); with purely proportional budgets (floors × 0) the gap almost vanished (3.0–8.5% for all four models). On UNSW-NB15 the networks were evaded more by +27.2 pp to +46.8 pp with volume and timing only. With the TTL the gap narrowed: the CNN exceeded the RF by +9.6 pp (p = 0.026) and the MLP the RF by +5.7 pp (p = 0.103), while the GBDT stayed below both networks (+24.3 pp and +20.4 pp). Without the TTL features in the model, the gap on UNSW-NB15 largely closed (Section 5.4). The networks also received a white-box attack that the trees did not, which favours the trees slightly. The comparison uses each model's class of highest probability; with the benign probability thresholded at each model's own FPR, the trees became harder to evade still and the gap on TON-IoT widened (grid and adaptive search: CNN 46.2% and MLP 62.5%, against RF 11.8% and GBDT 16.7%; Table 7). On these data the trees were also the more accurate models (Table 3).

Seeds matter more for the networks. The standard deviation of the strongest volume-only ASR over five seeds on UNSW-NB15 was 12.7 percentage points for the CNN and 10.2 for the MLP, against 1.4 for the RF; a single-seed robustness result for a network can be off by tens of points. On two further data splits (seed 0, Table S5) the networks were evaded more than both trees by the strongest attack in 3 of 3 splits on TON-IoT and 3 of 3 on UNSW-NB15.

Actions computed by white-box PGD on the undefended CNN of the same seed transferred partly. At β = 0.5 they evaded the MLP on 33.9% of its detections on TON-IoT and 67.8% on UNSW-NB15, the RF on 16.6% and 59.2%, and the GBDT on 9.5% and 37.5%; on UNSW-NB15 the transferred actions include the TTL choice. The surrogate shares the target's training data and pre-processing, so this is close to the best case for an attacker without query access, and it still evaded fewer flows than the query attacks on every model.

### 5.3 Defences
Table 7 compares the defences at each model's own operating point and at the matched benign FPR, with the primary tests; Table 8 gives clean performance and the strongest-attack ASR of every defended model with paired changes against the undefended model of the same seeds and, for the networks, against the clean-loop control. Figures 2 and 3 show the CNN defences over all budgets and the tree defences under each attack.

**Networks: no gain on TON-IoT at a matched rule and false-positive rate.** Every network defence, the clean-loop control included, raised the benign FPR (TON-IoT CNN: 0.015 without defence, 0.035 with the clean loop, 0.044 with AT-threat and 0.084 with AT-all). At their own operating points the defences looked strong on TON-IoT: AT-threat left the CNN evaded on 5.5% of detected flows by the strongest attack, against 51.4% without defence, and even the clean loop halved the ASR (26.2%). Table 7 removes two effects at once: the defended model's shifted threshold and the change of decision rule. The rule matters on its own: thresholding the benign probability at the undefended model's own FPR changed the share of TON-IoT attack flows missed or evaded by −4.9 pp for the CNN, −12.4 pp for the RF and −7.7 pp for the GBDT, without any defence. Under the same rule and FPR, no network defence significantly reduced the share missed or evaded on TON-IoT: 46.9% for the undefended CNN, against 38.3% with AT-threat (−8.6 pp, Holm p = 0.584) and more with the clean loop (60.9%), AT-all (65.2%), CCAT (73.0%) and TRADES (92.7%; +45.8 pp, Holm p = 0.001), which at the matched threshold detected only 60.9% of the attack flows before modification. The MLP with AT-threat (35.3% against 65.2%) did not differ significantly from its undefended model either (three seeds, Holm p = 0.122). Among the CNN defences, AT-threat was missed or evaded less than with the clean loop (−22.6 pp, Holm p = 0.020) and TRADES (−54.4 pp, p = 0.020), but not significantly less than with AT-all (p = 0.053) or CCAT (p = 0.137). On UNSW-NB15, with volume and timing actions, the action-space defences held at the matched FPR: AT-threat cut the share of attack flows missed or evaded by the CNN from 70.0% to 22.2% (−47.7 pp, Holm p = 0.003) and TRADES to 20.4% (p < 0.001); the clean loop reached 47.6% (p = 0.002), and AT-all and CCAT 42.3% and 50.6% (p = 0.055 and 0.048). AT-threat was significantly better than the clean loop (p = 0.015) but not than AT-all (p = 0.235), CCAT (p = 0.055) or TRADES (p = 0.717); with the MLP it reached 42.6% against 75.3% (p = 0.017). With the TTL action included, AT-all and CCAT, which never trained on it, lost their advantage, while AT-threat and TRADES kept theirs (Table S11). Overall, 5 of the 12 primary tests on TON-IoT (one of them, TRADES, in the direction of more evasion) and 8 of 12 on UNSW-NB15 were significant after Holm correction. At a 1% benign FPR, which a deployed detector would need, every UNSW-NB15 network missed or was evaded on at least 93.4% of the attack flows (Table 7).

**Is AT-all a fair baseline?** AT-all and CCAT were trained with the white-box ε-PGD through the differentiable quantile transform. That attack evaded them on TON-IoT on only 27.5% and 27.0% of detected flows at ε = 0.05, but the BPDA PGD on 91.4% and 84.1%, and the black-box corner search evaded every detected flow of every TON-IoT CNN, defended or not (Table S7). The apparent robustness of the ε-ball defences in their own ball was therefore an artefact of a weak attack, and on TON-IoT a ranking of the defences by white-box ε-PGD would have been misleading. Training AT-all against the BPDA attack instead did not give a usable baseline: on TON-IoT the network lost most of its clean accuracy (macro-F1 0.585, benign FPR 0.280), it was still evaded on every detected flow by the corner search, and at the matched FPR the realistic attack evaded it on 71.9% of detected flows. We therefore cannot exclude that a better-trained ε-ball defence would do better, but we found none. On UNSW-NB15 the ranking does differ between threat models: under the corner search the ε-ball defences were the least evaded CNNs (AT-all 63.7%, CCAT 61.4%, against 97.1% for AT-threat), and under the realistic attack the most evaded. One further configuration of each defence (seed 0, Table S10) showed that the configuration matters: at the matched FPR on TON-IoT, AT-threat trained at β = 0.25 or 1 was evaded on 18.1% and 20.5% of detected flows, against 49.0% for the main configuration on the same seed, and AT-all with ε = 0.02, 0.05 and 0.1 on 83.0%, 67.4% and 41.1%; on UNSW-NB15 every AT-threat configuration was evaded less than every AT-all configuration. Because ε = 0.1 looked best for AT-all on seed 0, we retrained it on all five TON-IoT seeds: at the matched FPR it left 55.2% of attack flows missed or evaded (+8.3 pp against the undefended CNN, p = 0.234; AT-threat minus AT-all at ε = 0.1: −16.8 pp, p = 0.100; unadjusted, outside the primary family), at a clean macro-F1 of 0.889 and a benign FPR of 0.065. Its seed-0 advantage therefore did not hold across seeds. A wider sweep of both defences over all seeds may still find better settings.

**Feature-space hardening against realistic attacks.** Dyrmishi et al. (2023) found that unrealistic, feature-space hardening could protect a network detector against realistic attacks. At its own operating point AT-all changed the TON-IoT CNN's strongest-attack ASR by −30.5 pp against the undefended model, but by only −5.2 pp against the clean-loop control, and at the matched FPR it gave no protection on TON-IoT; on UNSW-NB15 it did not touch the TTL. On our data, the protection that feature-space hardening seemed to give came from the training loop and the shifted threshold.

**Trees: augmentation held at the same FPR.** Tree augmentation, a single retraining with the most benign-looking candidate of each of up to 20,000 training attack flows, lowered the strongest-attack ASR on TON-IoT of the GBDT from 35.3% to 3.0% and of the RF from 25.2% to 10.3% (five seeds, primary Holm p = 0.002 and < 0.001), with clean macro-F1 changes of +0.001 and −0.001 and FPR changes of 0.000 and 0.000; with the threshold matched and the undefended GBDT under the same rule, the grid search evaded the augmented GBDT on 1.5% of detected flows, against 16.7% without augmentation. Because thresholding alone also made the undefended trees harder to evade, part of what augmentation achieves at the class of highest probability a benign threshold gives for free, but augmentation still lowered evasion under the same rule. The strongest attack includes the denser grid and the adaptive search, neither used to build the augmentation, and the saturation check on a subsample left the augmented trees' ASR within 3.8 pp of it. A second round of augmentation changed little (5.5% for the GBDT, 8.6% for the RF). Held-out actions showed the limit: trees augmented without delays were evaded on 9.9% (GBDT) and 19.5% (RF), and trees augmented without byte padding (which alone evaded 11.8% and 7.9% of the undefended trees' detections) on 16.0% and 13.0%, between the undefended and the fully augmented trees. At β = 2, four times the augmentation budget, the grid plus adaptive search evaded the augmented GBDT on 23.9% and the augmented RF on 19.1% of detected flows, against 60.0% and 28.7% without augmentation. The augmented GBDT was as accurate as the most accurate undefended model on TON-IoT (0.961 against 0.960).

**UNSW-NB15, trees.** Against the TTL candidates it was built with, tree augmentation almost removed the attack: the grid search evaded the augmented RF and GBDT on 0.6% and 1.7% of detected flows, and the strongest volume-only attack on 1.1% and 2.2%. TTL values outside the candidates broke this: the TTL sweep over 1–255 alone evaded 17.8% and 20.8%, and the adaptive search with any TTL, combined with volume and timing, 30.6% and 39.9% (Figure 3). The strongest attack therefore evaded the augmented trees on 30.8% and 40.3% of detected flows, still well below the undefended trees (89.7% and 75.0%; primary Holm p < 0.001 and 0.002), at no cost in macro-F1 (−0.003 and +0.002) or FPR (+0.005 and 0.000). For the augmented GBDT this is a loose lower bound: a longer adaptive search with any TTL evaded a further 10.2% of a 500-flow subsample (Table S8). A second round lowered the strongest ASR to 24.9% (RF) and 37.7% (GBDT). Trees augmented without delays and attacked with them were evaded by volume and timing on 10.4% and 5.2% of detected flows, more than with delays in training. Evaluated only against its own TTL candidates, tree augmentation would have looked like nearly complete protection on this dataset; it is not.

### 5.4 The TTL artefact
Table 9 separates the TTL from the detectors on UNSW-NB15. The TTL-only attack did not depend much on the candidate list: TTL values outside the four candidates evaded the undefended CNN, MLP and GBDT about as often as the candidates (CNN 84.5% against 83.8%), and the RF less often (36.6% against 53.5%; Figure 4 shows every value from 1 to 255). Detectors trained without sttl and ct_state_ttl (three seeds) reached a clean macro-F1 of 0.533 (CNN), 0.532 (MLP), 0.581 (RF) and 0.588 (GBDT), changes of −0.021, −0.020, −0.021 and −0.017 against the same seeds with the TTL features, at benign FPRs of 0.203, 0.192, 0.176 and 0.138. Without the TTL the attacker loses its free action, and the strongest attack, now volume and timing only, evaded 36.3% (CNN), 42.1% (MLP), 28.4% (RF) and 36.7% (GBDT) of detected flows. Against the volume-only attack on the same seeds of the detectors with TTL features (54.8%, 70.7%, 20.4% and 33.9%), removing the TTL features made the networks harder to evade with volume and timing, the RF easier and the GBDT about as easy, so the gap between networks and trees largely closed. The detection sets differ, however: the CNN without TTL features detected 99.6% of the sampled attacks before modification, against 71.2% with them, and over all sampled attack flows 36.6% (CNN), 42.7% (MLP), 28.8% (RF) and 37.1% (GBDT) ended up classified benign. The network–tree comparison on UNSW-NB15 is therefore partly a statement about how each family uses the TTL features.

## 6. Discussion
### 6.1 Implications
Five lessons follow for evaluating the adversarial robustness of flow-based detectors.

First, the threat model has to be stated in the problem space, and the conventional ε-ball is not a conservative substitute for it. Made strong, the feature-space attack evaded more detected flows than the realistic one for every model and ε we tested, even restricted to the features the attacker can change and to increases, except the UNSW-NB15 trees at the smallest balls (Section 5.1); for the networks, much of the difference came from changing features independently that the attacker can only change together. A weak feature-space attack is no better a guide: white-box ε-PGD through the quantile transform made the ε-ball defences look robust in their own ball, which a black-box search showed they were not. A robustness figure measured in feature space says little about the problem space.

Second, defences must be compared at the same operating point and under the same decision rule. Every network defence we tested, including a control without adversarial examples, made the detector flag more traffic, and a detector that flags more is harder to evade into "benign" without being more robust. Thresholding the benign probability, rather than taking the class of highest probability, also lowered evasion of the undefended models, the trees most. With both effects removed, no network defence significantly lowered the share of TON-IoT attack flows missed or evaded, and TRADES raised it; adversarial training on the attacker's actions was the best of them, significantly better than the clean loop and TRADES. On UNSW-NB15 the action-space defences and, to a lesser degree, the clean loop and CCAT kept a significant gain against volume and timing changes. Reporting ASR at the defended model's own threshold, as is common, would have credited every defence with a large gain; a robustness evaluation should also report the share of attacks missed or evaded, because a defence can lower the ASR by detecting fewer attacks.

Third, when the attacker controls only a few quantities, a gradient-free search evaluates networks and trees on equal terms, but it should be checked for saturation and complemented by an adaptive search; white-box attacks through piecewise-linear pre-processing were weaker than black-box search, in action space and in feature space. Retraining trees once on attack flows modified within the threat model lowered evasion without changing accuracy or the false-positive rate, the only defence in our study that did so, but it carried over only partly to TTL values and actions it was not built with, and an evaluation with the augmentation's own candidates would have overstated it greatly (Section 5.3).

Fourth, the budget model matters. Most TON-IoT evasions relied on the absolute floors of the budget, which let the attacker pad small flows such as scans; with purely proportional budgets the ASR fell to 3.0–8.5% and the gap between networks and trees almost vanished (Section 5.1). Robustness results should report how they depend on the budget, not a single setting.

Fifth, benchmark artefacts shape robustness results as they shape accuracy results (Arp et al., 2022). On UNSW-NB15 the TTL, a field the attacker sets for free and that separates the classes because of how the testbed was built (Flood et al., 2024), decided whether the networks could be evaded, defences trained in the ε-ball did not touch it, and without the TTL features the gap between networks and trees largely closed (Section 5.4). Robustness results on UNSW-NB15 should report the TTL separately, and the detectors themselves, with benign false-positive rates of 0.128 to 0.198, are not deployable as they stand. Finally, robustness estimates for the networks varied by tens of percentage points between seeds (Section 5.2), so single-seed robustness results for networks are unreliable.

### 6.2 Limitations
The actions are modelled on flow features: derived features are recomputed, but modified packets are not replayed, so retransmissions, fragmentation and any change in the destination's behaviour, such as acknowledgements of extra packets, are not represented (Section 3); the main evaluation also allows fractional packets and no MTU bound, although a check with both changed the grid-search results by at most 1.1 pp; the strongest attack, the adversarial-training inner maximisers and the augmentation candidates were not re-run under these constraints. The attacker cannot split an attack over several flows, vary its source ports or imitate the destination; such attackers may do better. One budget family was used, proportional to the flow's own volume plus additive floors, and the attacker's costs were not modelled beyond it; the floors decide much of the TON-IoT result. The query attacks assume that the attacker observes the detector's verdict, and each failed query raises an alert. The transfer surrogate shares the target's training data, which favours the attacker. All ASRs are lower bounds from searches, not exact attacks: on the trees an exact mixed-integer attack (Kantchelian et al., 2016) is feasible in principle in our three- or four-dimensional action space and was not run, and the search on the UNSW-NB15 GBDT was clearly not saturated. Both datasets are testbed captures with known artefacts, the UNSW-NB15 detectors are weak, and N-BaIoT, whose window features cannot be recomputed from one modified flow, was excluded. Only compact models of fixed architecture were tested, on a feature set selected on clean data before any attack, which fixes the attack surface. The network defences were not tested against an action held out of their training, as the tree augmentation was. Each defence was trained with one main configuration, with one further configuration per defence on a single seed. The MLP defence, the BPDA variant of AT-all, the further augmentation variants and the detectors without TTL features have three seeds and the further data splits one, and with five seeds some large effects remain non-significant after correction; the ASR is estimated on a fixed sample of 5,000 attack flows per dataset.

## 7. Conclusion
We evaluated four compact intrusion detectors on an IoT and an enterprise testbed against an attacker who can only pad, add packets to and delay its own flows and, on UNSW-NB15, set their TTL, with derived features recomputed and success counted only on detected attacks. A strong feature-space attack, even restricted to the features the attacker can change, overstated what volume and timing changes achieve on every model except the UNSW-NB15 trees at small ε, and a weak one misjudged the defences. Tree ensembles were more accurate and harder to evade than the networks, but on TON-IoT the gap rested on the absolute floors of the budget and on UNSW-NB15 on the TTL features. Defences for networks mostly moved the decision threshold: compared with the undefended model under the same rule and false-positive rate, none significantly reduced the share of TON-IoT attack flows missed or evaded, while on UNSW-NB15 adversarial training on the attacker's actions and TRADES cut it clearly. One round of adversarial augmentation within the threat model cut the strongest-attack success against gradient-boosted trees on TON-IoT to 3.0% without loss of accuracy or a change in the false-positive rate; on UNSW-NB15 TTL values not used in the augmentation still evaded 30.8%–40.3% of detected flows. Robustness evaluations of flow-based detectors should state the threat model in the problem space, make both the realistic and the reference attack strong, compare defences at a matched operating point, test them against actions and values they were not trained on, and report dataset artefacts separately.
