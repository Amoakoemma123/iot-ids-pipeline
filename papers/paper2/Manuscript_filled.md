## Title

**What Can an Attacker Actually Change? Evasion and Hardening of Compact Flow-Based Intrusion Detectors Under a Realistic Threat Model**

## Abstract

Robustness studies of network intrusion detectors usually perturb every flow feature within an L∞ ball, including fields an attacker cannot set. We evaluate compact detectors under a problem-space threat model: the attacker controls only its own flows (padding, extra packets, delays and, on UNSW-NB15, the initial TTL), derived features are recomputed and only detected attack flows count. On TON-IoT and UNSW-NB15 we attack a 1D-CNN, an MLP, a random forest and gradient-boosted trees with black-box, adaptive, white-box and transfer attacks. A feature-space attack restricted to the volume and timing features evaded more detected flows than the realistic attack on every model except the UNSW-NB15 trees, partly by ignoring dependencies between features, and a weak one ranked the defences wrongly. On TON-IoT the strongest realistic attack evaded 51.4% of the CNN's detections and 25.2% of the random forest's, mostly by padding small flows up to the budget's floors. Every network defence raised the false-positive rate; at the undefended model's rule and rate none significantly reduced the share of TON-IoT attack flows missed or evaded, and the gains seen over a range of rates depended on the decision rule. Retraining the gradient-boosted trees once on modified attack flows cut that share on TON-IoT from 16.9% to 1.6% under the same rule and rate, a lower bound from our searches, but unseen TTL values and destination replies still evaded augmented trees. Defences should be compared over several operating points and decision rules and tested against unseen actions and values.

**Keywords:** intrusion detection; Internet of Things; adversarial machine learning; evasion attacks; problem-space attacks; adversarial training; tree ensembles

## 1. Introduction

Machine-learning intrusion detectors for Internet of Things (IoT) and enterprise networks are often trained on flow records: statistics of each connection such as its duration, the bytes and packets sent in each direction, and protocol and state fields. An attacker who knows that such a detector is in place has an obvious move: change the attack traffic so that its flow record looks benign. Studies of this threat often borrow the methods of adversarial machine learning on images: they perturb every feature of the record within a small L∞ ball and report how often the prediction changes (e.g. Ibitoye et al., 2019).

For network traffic this model is unrealistic in two directions. Many features cannot be changed by the attacker at all: the destination's responses, the service and protocol of the victim, the connection state and the victim's port are fixed by the attack itself, and one-hot encoded categorical fields cannot take fractional values. Other features can be changed by far more than a small ε, at a cost: an attacker can pad its packets, send extra packets or slow down, and on some networks can choose the initial time-to-live (TTL) of its packets. Derived features (loads, mean packet sizes, header byte counts) must change consistently with these actions. An evaluation that ignores both directions can overstate vulnerability, by allowing impossible changes, and understate it, by forbidding easy large ones (Pierazzi et al., 2020; Apruzzese et al., 2022). The way success is counted matters as well: an attack flow that the detector misses anyway, or a benign flow that the attack makes look malicious, is not an evasion.

Several studies have restricted the attacker accordingly (Section 2). For compact detectors of the kind proposed for IoT gateways, three questions are still open. How much do the conventional and the realistic threat model disagree, in which direction, and does the answer hold when derived features are recomputed under physical constraints and both attacks are made strong? Are tree ensembles, often the most accurate models on these benchmarks, also the hardest to evade when they are attacked with the same gradient-free search as networks, and can they be hardened cheaply against attacks they were not trained on? And how much of the apparent vulnerability, and of the apparent effect of defences, comes from a known artefact of the dataset or from a shifted decision threshold rather than from the detectors?

Our contributions are:

1. A problem-space threat model for the flow records of TON-IoT, an IoT testbed, and UNSW-NB15, an enterprise-network testbed: the attacker adds payload bytes, packets and delays and, on UNSW-NB15, sets the TTL; derived features are recomputed, and success means that a detected attack flow is classified as benign.
2. An evaluation of a compact CNN, an MLP, a random forest and gradient-boosted trees with a gradient-free search that applies to all four, adaptive, white-box and transfer attacks, checked for saturation, against a strengthened feature-space reference (black-box search of the ball, mutable features and increases only, and BPDA gradients; Athalye et al., 2018).
3. A comparison of feature-space and action-space adversarial training, TRADES, a clean-loop control and adversarial augmentation of tree ensembles at matched decision rules and false-positive rates, with a small primary family of tests, held-out candidates, TTLs and actions, and sensitivity checks for the inner search and for destination replies.
4. A separation of the UNSW-NB15 TTL artefact from properties of the detectors, including detectors trained without the TTL features.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Adversarial attacks on network intrusion detection

Early studies of adversarial examples against learned intrusion detectors applied image-domain attacks such as the fast gradient sign method (Goodfellow et al., 2015), projected gradient descent (PGD; Madry et al., 2018) and the Carlini–Wagner attack (Carlini & Wagner, 2017) to the feature vectors of flow records, without restricting which features change; Ibitoye et al. (2019), for example, attack feed-forward detectors on Bot-IoT in this way. Surveys of adversarial machine learning in security note that such feature-space attacks may not correspond to anything an attacker can do with real traffic (Rosenberg et al., 2021), and Apruzzese et al. (2022) propose a taxonomy of realistic attacker capabilities (knowledge, feedback and whether manipulation happens in the problem space or the feature space). Pierazzi et al. (2020) formalise problem-space attacks: transformations the attacker can actually perform, which must preserve semantics, be plausible and survive the pre-processing, and whose side effects on other features must be accounted for. From case studies and a review of security-conference papers, Apruzzese et al. (2023) argue that real attackers rarely compute gradients and that threat models should be precise and cost-driven.

Several studies restrict the attacker accordingly. Apruzzese et al. (2019) increase only the duration, bytes and packets of botnet flows, update the derived features and count evasion over malicious flows, against tree and neural classifiers. Debicha et al. (2023) modify only duration, bytes and packets in a black-box transfer attack, recompute dependent features and attack an MLP, a random forest and k-nearest neighbours. Chernikova and Oprea (2022) attack a botnet detector with gradients that respect feature dependencies. Alhajjar et al. (2021) restrict evolutionary and generative attacks to mutable features of NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15, many of them increase-only, and report the share of malicious records classified as normal for eleven models including tree ensembles. Han et al. (2021) go further and mutate real packets in the traffic space, measuring evasion over traffic that was detected before the attack. Sheatsley et al. (2022) derive constraints from TCP/IP rules and show that constraint-obeying attacks still reach high misclassification rates on NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15, and Teuffenbach et al. (2020) weight features by how hard they are to manipulate. Vitorino et al. (2022) generate realistic adversarial flows with class-specific perturbation patterns and use them to attack and adversarially train MLP and random-forest detectors on CIC-IDS2017 and IoT-23, and Vitorino et al. (2023) apply the method to IoT-23 and Bot-IoT with a random forest, two gradient-boosting ensembles and an isolation forest, finding the tree ensembles susceptible and adversarial training beneficial. Simonetto et al. (2022) propose a common framework for attacks and adversarial training under feature constraints.

These studies differ in how success is counted. Some count any misclassification, including an attack labelled as a different attack (Sheatsley et al., 2022); others count only attack records classified as benign (Apruzzese et al., 2019; Alhajjar et al., 2021; Han et al., 2021; Chernikova & Oprea, 2022). Only the latter corresponds to evasion of an alert. Finally, benchmark artefacts can make an attack look powerful: in UNSW-NB15, the TTL and protocol fields separate benign from malicious flows almost perfectly, and simply setting them to benign values matches a published constrained attack (Flood et al., 2024).

### 2.2 Defences and tree models

Adversarial training with PGD (Madry et al., 2018) and its trade-off-aware variant TRADES (Zhang et al., 2019) are the standard defences for neural networks. Under class imbalance, adversarial training widens the gap between frequent and rare classes (Wu et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2021); leaving the rare classes unperturbed during training is a simple imbalance-aware baseline, which we call class-conditional adversarial training (CCAT). Defences must be evaluated with attacks adapted to them (Carlini et al., 2019; Tramèr et al., 2020). In security domains, Dyrmishi et al. (2023) found that hardening with unrealistic, feature-space examples protected a botnet detector against a realistic attack, but not models in other domains; whether this holds for IoT flows, and whether adversarial training restricted to what the attacker can actually change does better, is open.

Tree ensembles, among the strongest models on many intrusion-detection benchmarks, have no gradients. They can be attacked exactly with mixed-integer programming (Kantchelian et al., 2016) or with decision-based black-box attacks (Brendel et al., 2018; Cheng et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2020), and hardened by adversarial boosting (Kantchelian et al., 2016), robust split criteria (Chen et al., 2019; Vos & Verwer, 2021) or, for flow-based detectors, by a dedicated hardening method for random forests (Apruzzese et al., 2020) and adversarial training with realistic examples (Vitorino et al., 2022, 2023). In intrusion detection they are often attacked only by transfer or by feature-space search. When the attacker's actions span only three or four dimensions, as in our threat model, a dense search over the action space evaluates every model on equal terms without gradients, and an adaptive search can refine it.

## 3. Threat Model

**Attacker.** The attacker is the source of a malicious flow and wants the detector to classify that flow as benign. It controls only its own sending behaviour. It can add bytes to the payload it sends (padding), send additional packets, delay its packets, which lengthens the flow, and delay its acknowledgements. On UNSW-NB15, whose features include the source TTL observed at the monitor, it can also choose the initial TTL of its packets, as any host with raw-socket access or a configurable network stack can; the observed value is the initial value minus the hops to the monitor, so any value from 1 to 255 can be reached. It cannot remove bytes or packets, because the attack payload must still be delivered, and it cannot change how the destination responds, the service, protocol or connection state of the flow, or the victim's port. A delay requires at least two packets once any added packets are counted, and an acknowledgement delay requires a TCP handshake (we apply it only to flows with a non-zero ackdat). Extra packets may in practice draw replies from the destination (acknowledgements, resets or ICMP messages), which we do not model; such replies would change destination-side counters, in a direction that depends on the target, so this simplification can favour either the attacker or the detector (Table S15 marks the features concerned; Sections 5.3 and 6.2).

**Knowledge.** We consider four levels. In the white-box setting the attacker knows the model, its pre-processing and gradients. In the decision-based black-box setting it can query the deployed detector with modified flows and observe the predicted class; in the score-based setting it also observes the class probabilities, which our adaptive search uses. In the transfer setting it computes actions on a surrogate. Our surrogate is a CNN trained on the same data, features and pre-processing, so the transfer setting is close to white-box knowledge of the training data and only withholds the target model.

**Budget.** For a flow with b source bytes, p source packets, duration d and ACK delay k, the attacker may add up to β(b + 1,000) payload bytes, β(p + 10) packets, β(d + 1 s) seconds of delay and β(k + 0.1 s) of ACK delay, for a relative budget β ∈ {0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2}. The additive floors allow flows with no payload, such as scans, to be padded at all; for small flows they dominate the budget, so we also report results with the floors removed, halved and doubled. At β = 0.5 the floors allow 500 bytes, 5 packets and 0.5 s per flow: for a single-packet SYN probe this multiplies its traffic several times and slows a scan of many ports noticeably, an overhead an attacker can accept for targeted probes but not for fast wide scans. Choosing the TTL has no budget. Because the TTL field almost separates benign from malicious flows in UNSW-NB15 (Flood et al., 2024), an evasion obtained by the TTL choice exploits a dataset artefact rather than a property of the detectors; we therefore report volume-and-timing actions and the TTL choice separately, treat the former as the main result on UNSW-NB15, and also train detectors without the TTL features.

**Feature consistency.** Every selected feature that depends on the actions is recomputed from the modified raw flow (Table 2): bytes, packets, duration and ACK delay change directly; IP-level bytes, mean packet size and the source and destination load follow from them; and on UNSW-NB15 the TTL-derived state feature is looked up from the training data. The recomputed raw features pass through the deployed min–max scaling and, for the networks, the quantile transform; with all actions zero the mapping reproduces the pipeline's features to within 3.4e-04. The main evaluation allows fractional packets and no MTU bound (checked in Section 5.1). Supplementary Section S18 gives the full mapping.

| Dataset | Action (attacker) | Budget at β | Applies to | Selected features recomputed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TON-IoT | add payload bytes | β(bytes + 1,000) | all flows | src_bytes; src_ip_bytes |
| TON-IoT | add packets | β(packets + 10) | all flows | src_pkts; src_ip_bytes (+ header bytes per packet) |

**Success.** An attack succeeds on a flow if the flow is an attack, the model detects it without modification (predicts any attack class), and the modified flow is classified as benign. The attack success rate (ASR) is the share of detected attack flows that the attack turns into benign predictions, computed on a fixed, class-stratified sample of 5,000 attack test flows per dataset, the same for every model. Because the sample is stratified, the ASR is close to an average over attack classes; the supplementary material also gives it weighted by the prevalence of each class in the test set (Table S3). Because the ASR is taken over detected flows only, we also report the end-to-end evasion: the share of all sampled attack flows classified benign after the attack, missed or evaded.

## 4. Data, Models and Attacks

### 4.1 Data
TON-IoT (Alsaedi et al., 2020; Moustafa, 2021) and UNSW-NB15 (Moustafa & Slay, 2015) are pre-processed by the pipeline released with the code: identifier and free-text columns removed, categorical fields with at most 30 values one-hot encoded, duplicates removed after all column drops, a stratified 70/15/15 split with a cross-split duplicate guard, feature selection (mutual information and recursive feature elimination with cross-validation) and min–max scaling fitted on the training split, and SMOTE oversampling of the training split (Chawla et al., 2002). This leaves 25 features on TON-IoT and 10 on UNSW-NB15, the latter including both TTL features (sttl and ct_state_ttl). The feature set is selected on clean data before any attack and therefore also fixes the attack surface. Attacks start from the raw, unscaled test records, so that actions are applied in natural units (bytes, packets, seconds) before the deployed pre-processing. Classes with fewer than 2% of the training records before oversampling are treated as rare by CCAT: mitm on TON-IoT and analysis, backdoor, shellcode, worms on UNSW-NB15. To check that results do not depend on one split, the main models are also trained and attacked on two further stratified splits of each dataset, with the same selected features and the scaling and oversampling refitted. On UNSW-NB15 we also train detectors without sttl and ct_state_ttl. We do not use N-BaIoT here: its features are statistics over sliding time windows of a device's traffic, so the effect of changing one flow cannot be mapped onto them without the packet traces.

### 4.2 Models
We attack four compact detectors. The 1D-CNN has two convolutional blocks (32 and 64 filters, kernel 3, batch normalisation, max-pooling), global average pooling and dense layers of 128 and 64 units with dropout 0.3; the MLP has dense layers of 128 and 64 units with batch normalisation and a 64-unit layer with dropout 0.3. Both are trained on the selected features after a quantile transform to the uniform distribution (1,000 quantiles), with Adam (learning rate 10⁻³, halved after 5 epochs without improvement of the validation loss), batch size 256, at most 100 epochs and early stopping on the validation loss (patience 15), restoring the weights of the best epoch. The random forest (RF) has 100 trees of maximum depth 16 with √d features per split, and the gradient-boosted trees (GBDT; scikit-learn's histogram-based implementation) have at most 31 leaves per tree, learning rate 0.05 and up to 1,000 iterations, with scikit-learn's internal early stopping on 10% of the training data (at the default learning rate of 0.1 the same model collapsed to low macro-F1 on some seeds in preliminary runs on these data); both are trained on the min–max scaled features, on which the quantile transform would have no effect. Early stopping left the GBDT with 111–480 iterations on TON-IoT, while on UNSW-NB15 it reached the cap of 1,000 on 4 of five seeds, so the UNSW-NB15 GBDT is limited by the iteration budget; the RF trees have on average 1,424 (TON-IoT) and 4,364 (UNSW-NB15) leaves. Undefended models are trained with five seeds (0–4). Seeds vary initialisation and the randomness of the trees but not the data; the resplits above vary the data. Each model predicts the class of highest probability; Section 4.5 describes how we also set a benign threshold.

### 4.3 Attacks
All attacks are run against every trained model, including every defended model, so they are adapted to the defence (Tramèr et al., 2020). The random points of the evaluation grids are drawn with seeds disjoint from those used to build the tree augmentation; the corners of the budget box and the four TTL candidates are the same in both, so the TTL sweep and the adaptive search with any TTL are the tests with values the augmentation did not see.

- **Black-box grid search** (decision-based): all corners of the budget box (8 on TON-IoT, 16 on UNSW-NB15) and 120 or 48 random points inside it, scaled to each flow's budget and, on UNSW-NB15, combined with the unchanged TTL and each of the 4 most frequent benign TTLs (31, 254, 62, 1); we report volume and timing, TTL only and both, per-action variants and, at β = 0.5, a further 1,000 random points.
- **TTL sweep** (UNSW-NB15): every TTL from 1 to 255.
- **Adaptive search** (score-based): 40 rounds of 16 proposals per flow, keeping the most benign-scoring one, run on the flows the grid did not evade, in the spirit of the Square Attack (Andriushchenko et al., 2020) used in AutoAttack (Croce & Hein, 2020).
- **White-box action PGD** (networks): sign-gradient ascent on the actions through the feature map, the scaling and a differentiable quantile transform, in a basic and a strong version (margin loss, corner restarts, 100 steps).
- **Transfer**: actions computed on the undefended CNN of the same seed, applied to the other undefended models.

Parameters of every attack are in Section S18.

**Strongest attack.** Our main measure at β = 0.5 counts a flow as evaded if any of the following evades it: the grid search (with and without the TTL candidates), the grid with 1,000 further points, the three adaptive searches and, for the networks, the strong white-box PGD. Adding the TTL sweep, a 24-point grid and the per-action grids raised the strongest ASR of any model by at most 0.8 pp; the basic PGD and transfer are also left out, and their ASRs were below those of the attacks included. For the networks we also ran the strong PGD with the gradient taken through the straight-through transform described below (Section 5.1).

**Saturation and feasibility checks.** On the trees, for which no white-box attack exists, denser regular grids and a longer adaptive search on 500 detected flows per seed check whether the search is saturated; no exact attack was run, so tree ASRs are lower bounds. A second grid search uses integer packets and an MTU bound.

**Conventional reference.** Attacks in min–max feature space, not realisable, serve for comparison: targeted L∞ PGD (ε ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.1}) and a strengthened reference (ε from 0.005 to 0.1) combining a black-box search over random corners of the ball, an exhaustive search of {−ε, 0, +ε} patterns on the mutable features and, for the networks, a strong PGD through a straight-through approximation of the quantile transform (BPDA; Athalye et al., 2018). Details are in Section S18.

### 4.4 Defences
The network defences train a fresh network with a custom loop: at most 30 epochs of 200 steps (Adam, learning rate 10⁻³), early stopping on the clean validation loss (patience 5) and the best weights restored. Each step combines a clean oversampled batch of 256 records and 128 further records with equal loss weight.

- **Clean loop** (control): the further records are real training attack records without modification. This separates the effect of the training loop and of the extra weight on attack records from that of the adversarial examples; because the extra records are attacks, the control also shifts the class prior towards attacks. Every network defence is compared with it as well as with the undefended model.
- **AT-all**: untargeted PGD adversarial training in min–max feature space on records of all classes (ε = 0.05, 7 steps of ε/4, random start), through the differentiable quantile transform. A variant (AT-all, BPDA) uses the straight-through transform of Section 4.3 for the inner maximisation, to check whether AT-all was trained against a weak inner attack.
- **CCAT**: as AT-all, but classes with fewer than 2% of the training records are trained without perturbation.
- **AT-threat**: adversarial training matched to the threat model: action-space PGD towards benign (β = 0.5, 7 sign steps of 0.05 of the box from a uniform random start) applied to real (not oversampled) training attack flows only. On UNSW-NB15 each flow keeps its TTL with probability 0.5 and otherwise takes one of the TTL candidates. AT-threat is applied to both the CNN and the MLP; the other network defences to the CNN only. Seven steps of 0.05 reach at most 0.35 of the box from the start, whereas tree augmentation searches the corners, where most evasions lie (Section 5.1); a variant (**AT-threat, corner start**) therefore starts the inner PGD of each flow at the most benign-scoring of all box corners and eight random points, a candidate set as strong as the augmentation's, and was trained with five seeds.
- **TRADES** (Zhang et al., 2019): cross-entropy on the clean batch plus 6 × the KL divergence between predictions on clean and action-perturbed attack flows, the perturbation found by 7 steps of action-space ascent on that divergence (with the same TTL choice as AT-threat). TRADES is therefore an action-space defence.
- **Tree augmentation**: for RF and GBDT, the most benign-looking black-box candidate (β = 0.5; box corners and 24 random points) of each of up to 20,000 real training attack flows is added to the training set with its true label, and the model is retrained once. On UNSW-NB15 each flow keeps its TTL with probability 0.5 and otherwise takes one of the TTL candidates. Three variants test generalisation: a second round, in which the search is repeated against the augmented model on a second random sample of training attack flows and the model is retrained with both sets; and augmentation without delay actions or without byte padding, attacked with all actions (held-out action).

The undefended models, the CNN defences and the augmented trees are trained with five seeds; the MLP with AT-threat, AT-all (BPDA), the further augmentation variants and the detectors without TTL features with three (0–2). To check that the comparison does not hinge on one configuration, AT-all was also trained with ε = 0.02 and 0.1 and AT-threat with β = 0.25 and 1 (seed 0; Table S10).

### 4.5 Evaluation
Clean performance is measured on the full test set: macro-F1, per-class F1 (in particular of the rare classes) and the benign false-positive rate (FPR). Robustness is measured by the ASR of Section 3. Numbers are means over seeds with their standard deviation; for the main attacks we give 95% bootstrap confidence intervals that resample seeds and then the attacked flows within each seed; with five seeds these understate between-seed uncertainty, so defence differences are given with t-based intervals over seeds.

**Operating point.** A defence that makes a model flag more traffic makes it harder to evade into "benign" without making it more robust. We therefore also compare the models at a matched operating point under one decision rule: a flow is classified benign if its benign probability is at least a threshold set on the validation benign flows. For the undefended model the threshold gives its own validation FPR; for a defended model of the same family and seed it gives the undefended model's FPR (and, separately, 1%). The undefended reference is thus evaluated under the same rule as the defences rather than with the class of highest probability, a change that on its own alters evasion (Section 5.3). At each threshold we attack the flows the thresholded model detects with the grid search and, for the networks, the adaptive search (β = 0.5), and report the ASR and the share of all sampled attack flows classified benign after the attack (missed or evaded), which also counts attacks that a model detects less often at the matched threshold. On UNSW-NB15 the main comparison uses volume and timing actions only, because the TTL is a dataset artefact (Section 5.4); Table S11 gives all actions. Because one threshold sits in the steep tail of the benign-probability distribution, we also trace the attack-aware ROC: the share missed or evaded by the grid search (β = 0.5) as the validation FPR runs from 0.5% to 5%, summarised by its mean over that range (partial area), under the rule above and under a second rule that thresholds one minus the highest attack-class probability (Table S20). These comparisons were added in revision and are secondary.

**Tests.** A defended model and the undefended model of the same seed share their initialisation, so defences are compared with paired t-tests over the common seeds, with Welch tests as a check because the defended networks are trained with a different loop. Seeds vary training randomness but not the data, so all tests are conditional on one training set. We specified a primary family of 12 tests per dataset, Holm-adjusted (Holm, 1979): each network defence against its undefended model and CNN AT-threat against each other CNN defence, on the share missed or evaded at the matched FPR (volume and timing only on UNSW-NB15); and each tree augmentation against its undefended tree, on the strongest-attack ASR (augmentation leaves the FPR unchanged). The family was first specified before the operating-point experiments, with the ASR as endpoint and the undefended model under the class of highest probability as reference; we revised the reference and the endpoint once, after those results, when we found that the comparison mixed two decision rules and ignored attacks that a model stops detecting at the matched threshold. The revised family is reported, with the missed and the evaded share given separately (Table 7). Table S21 gives the family as first specified; on TON-IoT it leads to the same conclusion (no network defence significantly below its undefended model, TRADES significantly above), and on UNSW-NB15 to similar ones. All other defence comparisons (118 on TON-IoT and 236 on UNSW-NB15: clean macro-F1 and further attacks against the undefended model, against the clean loop and between defences) form a secondary family per dataset with its own Holm adjustment, for which we also give unadjusted p-values. Network and tree models are compared pairwise with Welch's t-test (each network against each tree), Holm-adjusted within each dataset and attack (four tests). Experiments ran on one laptop; the networks were trained on its GPU (NVIDIA GTX 1650, 4 GB).

## 5. Results
### 5.1 How much does the threat model change the picture?
Table 3 gives the clean performance of the undefended models. **The feature-space reference.** Table 5 compares the realistic volume-and-timing attack with the strengthened feature-space attack. A black-box search over corners of the full ball evaded every detected flow of every TON-IoT model at ε = 0.01, the trees included, far more than the white-box ε-PGD commonly used as the reference (CNN: 73.8%); a black-box attack that beats a white-box one points to uninformative gradients, not to robustness (Carlini et al., 2019). Restricted to the mutable volume and timing features, and even to increases only, the feature-space attack still evaded more detected flows than the realistic attack at the smallest ε we tried (0.005) on every model except the UNSW-NB15 trees, where the ball had to reach 0.02 (RF) and 0.01 (GBDT) before it overtook the realistic attack (increases only: > 0.1 and 0.02). The gap has two sources. One is magnitude: 0.005 of the min–max range of a heavy-tailed byte count can exceed a flow's whole volume, and on TON-IoT the realistic interval of a mutable feature had a median width of at most 4e-04 in min–max units. The other is consistency: setting each mutable feature independently within its realistic interval, which keeps realistic magnitudes but breaks the dependencies between derived features, raised evasion of the networks by up to 16.1 pp (+10.0 pp for the TON-IoT CNN) but of the RF by only +0.5 pp and +1.3 pp. The feature-space attack therefore overstated what volume and timing changes achieve for every model except the UNSW-NB15 trees at small ε, for the networks partly by ignoring how features depend on each other.

**How strong are the realistic attacks?** The grid search was close to saturation on TON-IoT and less so on UNSW-NB15 (Table S1): 1,000 further random points per flow added at most 4.7 pp on TON-IoT and 9.8 pp on UNSW-NB15, and the adaptive search added 0.6–9.5 pp and 2.7–13.5 pp with the TTL kept. On the networks, the strong white-box action PGD improved on the basic version (by +12.6 pp for the CNN on TON-IoT) but stayed 0.4–5.8 percentage points below the grid search for both networks on both datasets, so gradients through the piecewise-linear quantile transform are weak here too; we therefore use the strongest attack, which includes the black-box searches, as the main measure. Taking the gradient through the straight-through (BPDA) transform did not change this: the strong action PGD with BPDA gradients evaded at most 0.1% of detected flows that the strongest attack had not, on any network, undefended or defended (Table S12). On the trees, finer regular grids and a longer adaptive search raised the ASR on a subsample by at most 3.8 pp on TON-IoT (Table S8), but by up to 10.3 pp on UNSW-NB15, where the strongest attack on the GBDT is therefore a loose lower bound. Integer packets and the MTU bound changed the grid-search ASR of every model by at most 1.1 pp (Table S9).

**Which actions matter?** Success grew with the budget for every model except the UNSW-NB15 RF, whose volume-only grid-search ASR peaked at β = 0.25 (12.9%, against 10.6% at β = 2; Figure 1); because the grids of different budgets are not nested, a falling curve signals an unsaturated search rather than robustness at larger budgets. No single action sufficed on TON-IoT: bytes alone and packets alone evaded the CNN on 12.7% and 11.1%, delays alone on 0.9%, and the three together on 47.9% (Table S2). On UNSW-NB15 delays alone evaded the networks (30.8% for the CNN), but hardly the trees (1.0% and 2.0%). The additive floors of the budget decide most of the TON-IoT result: with the budget purely proportional to the flow's own volume (floors × 0), the volume-only ASR at β = 0.5 fell to 3.0–8.5% of detected flows on TON-IoT, against 5.9–42.8% on UNSW-NB15. Most TON-IoT evasions therefore pad small flows by the absolute part of the budget (up to 500 bytes, 5 packets and 0.5 s at β = 0.5; Table S4 gives the cheapest successful actions); whether that overhead is acceptable depends on the attack.

**Which attack classes evade?** On TON-IoT the classes differed more than the models (Table S6): small ransomware and scanning flows evaded every model on at least 75.5% and 76.8% of detections, while the RF was almost never evaded on backdoor, injection, password and XSS flows.

**Queries and missed attacks.** Against the undefended models the median evading flow was evaded by the 4th grid query on TON-IoT (CNN) and the 9th on UNSW-NB15; each failed query is itself a detected attack, so query access is a strong assumption, while transfer (Section 5.2) needs none. The worst-case query count of the strongest attack (1,769 on TON-IoT and 7,243 on UNSW-NB15) is an upper bound, reached only on flows that every cheaper attack misses. Because the ASR counts only attacks detected before modification, it understates the end result for models that miss many attacks: on UNSW-NB15, 72.4% of all sampled attack flows were classified benign after the volume-only attack on the CNN, against an ASR of 61.5% (Table S3).

### 5.2 Networks against trees
Under the realistic attacks the trees were harder to evade than the networks, with qualifications (Table 6). On TON-IoT each network was evaded more than each tree under every realistic attack in Table 6, by +16.2 pp (CNN against GBDT) to +39.7 pp (MLP against RF) for the strongest attack (Welch's t-test over seeds, Holm-adjusted p ≤ 0.005); with purely proportional budgets (floors × 0) the gap almost vanished (3.0–8.5% for all four models). On UNSW-NB15 the networks were evaded more by +27.2 pp to +46.8 pp with volume and timing only. With the TTL the gap narrowed: the CNN exceeded the RF by +9.6 pp (p = 0.026) and the MLP the RF by +5.7 pp (p = 0.103), while the GBDT stayed below both networks (+24.3 pp and +20.4 pp). Without the TTL features in the model, the gap on UNSW-NB15 largely closed (Section 5.4). The networks also received a white-box attack that the trees did not, which favours the trees slightly. With the benign probability thresholded at each model's own FPR the gap on TON-IoT widened (Table 7), and over false-positive rates of 0.5–5% the trees stayed far harder to evade (Table S20).

Seeds matter more for the networks. The standard deviation of the strongest volume-only ASR over five seeds on UNSW-NB15 was 12.7 percentage points for the CNN and 10.2 for the MLP, against 1.4 for the RF; a single-seed robustness result for a network can be off by tens of points. On two further data splits (seed 0, Table S5) the networks were evaded more than both trees by the strongest attack in 3 of 3 splits on TON-IoT and 3 of 3 on UNSW-NB15.

Actions computed by white-box PGD on the undefended CNN of the same seed transferred partly. At β = 0.5 they evaded the MLP on 33.9% of its detections on TON-IoT and the trees on 16.6% (RF) and 9.5% (GBDT); Table 4 gives UNSW-NB15, where the transferred actions include the TTL choice. The surrogate shares the target's training data and pre-processing, so this is close to the best case for an attacker without query access, and it still evaded fewer flows than the query attacks on every model.

### 5.3 Defences
Table 7 compares the defences at each model's own operating point and at the matched benign FPR, with the primary tests; Table S19 gives clean performance and the strongest-attack ASR of every defended model with paired changes against the undefended model of the same seeds and, for the networks, against the clean-loop control. Figures 2 and 3 show the CNN defences over all budgets and the tree defences under each attack.

**Networks at a matched rule and false-positive rate: no detectable gain on TON-IoT.** Every network defence, the clean-loop control included, raised the benign FPR (TON-IoT CNN: 0.015 without defence, 0.035 with the clean loop, 0.044 with AT-threat). At their own operating points the defences therefore looked strong: AT-threat left the TON-IoT CNN evaded on 5.5% of detected flows, against 51.4% without defence, and even the clean loop halved the ASR (26.2%). Table 7 removes the shifted threshold and the change of decision rule; the rule alone changed the share of TON-IoT attack flows missed or evaded by −4.9 pp (CNN), −12.4 pp (RF) and −7.7 pp (GBDT) without any defence. Under the same rule and FPR, no network defence significantly reduced the share missed or evaded on TON-IoT: 46.9% for the undefended CNN against 38.3% with AT-threat (−8.6 pp, 95% CI [−34.4 pp, +17.2 pp], Holm p = 0.584) and more with the other defences; TRADES raised it (+45.8 pp, Holm p = 0.001), largely because at the matched threshold it detected only 60.9% of the attack flows before modification. AT-threat was missed or evaded less than the clean loop and TRADES (Holm p = 0.020 and 0.020), but not significantly less than AT-all or CCAT. On UNSW-NB15, with volume and timing actions, the action-space defences held at the matched FPR: AT-threat cut the share missed or evaded by the CNN from 70.0% to 22.2% (Holm p = 0.003) and TRADES to 20.4% (p < 0.001), and with the TTL action included AT-all and CCAT lost their advantage while AT-threat and TRADES kept theirs (Table S11). Overall, 5 of the 12 primary tests on TON-IoT (one of them, TRADES, towards more evasion) and 8 of 12 on UNSW-NB15 were significant after Holm correction. With five seeds and per-seed values that vary by tens of percentage points, these tests detect only large differences: the TON-IoT interval for AT-threat is [−34.4 pp, +17.2 pp], so the absence of a significant gain there is not evidence that the gain is zero.

**A stronger inner search for AT-threat.** AT-threat's inner attack starts at a random point and moves at most 0.35 of the box, while tree augmentation trains on the corners. With the corner start (Section 4.4; Table S16), AT-threat on TON-IoT left 40.9% of the attack flows missed or evaded at the matched FPR (per seed 36.1%, 45.6%, 31.1%, 49.0%, 42.5%), against 46.9% for the undefended CNN (−6.0 pp, 95% CI [−19.9 pp, +7.8 pp], p = 0.294) and 38.3% for AT-threat with a random start (+2.6 pp, [−19.3 pp, +24.4 pp]), at a clean macro-F1 of 0.914. On UNSW-NB15 it cut the share missed or evaded by volume and timing to 25.1% (−44.9 pp, [−56.1 pp, −33.7 pp]), about as much as AT-threat with a random start (+2.8 pp, [−18.8 pp, +24.5 pp]). These tests are unadjusted and outside the primary family. Giving the network's inner search the trees' candidates therefore did not change the network result: the difference between the two families is not explained by a weaker inner search for the networks, although the seed variation leaves effects of about ±20 pp undetectable.

**Over a range of false-positive rates.** A single threshold in the tail of the benign-probability distribution is fragile, so Figure 4 and Table S20 trace the share missed or evaded by the grid search as the validation FPR runs from 0.5% to 5%. On TON-IoT the picture differs from the single operating point: averaged over that range, AT-threat lowered the share missed or evaded from 34.3% to 13.8% (−20.5 pp, 95% CI [−37.2 pp, −3.8 pp]), its corner-start variant to 12.8% ([−32.1 pp, −11.0 pp]) and the MLP with AT-threat from 52.5% to 15.5%, mostly at FPRs of 2% and above; TRADES raised it (+14.1 pp, [+3.2 pp, +25.0 pp]), and AT-all and CCAT did not lower it. Under the second rule, which thresholds one minus the highest attack-class probability, the gain of AT-threat disappeared (+1.0 pp, [−19.6 pp, +21.6 pp]) and TRADES, AT-all and CCAT were evaded far more than the undefended CNN (+44.0 pp, +37.7 pp and +41.7 pp), so the TRADES result does not hinge on the rule but the AT-threat result does. On UNSW-NB15 the matched operating point lies at the undefended CNN's own FPR of about 0.198; at FPRs of 5% and below every network was missed or evaded on most attack flows (partial area 76.9%–95.2% for the CNNs), and no network defence lowered it significantly (AT-threat −4.5 pp, [−19.6 pp, +10.6 pp]). The trees were far harder to evade over the whole range, and augmentation lowered the partial area further, to 0.6% (GBDT) and 1.2% (RF) on TON-IoT and to 1.3% and 22.6% on UNSW-NB15. These comparisons are grid-search only, secondary and unadjusted.

**Is AT-all a fair baseline?** The white-box ε-PGD that AT-all and CCAT were trained with evaded them on TON-IoT on only 27.5% and 27.0% of detected flows at ε = 0.05, but the BPDA PGD on 91.4% and 84.1%, and the black-box corner search evaded every detected flow (Table S7): their robustness in their own ball was an artefact of a weak attack. Training AT-all against the BPDA attack collapsed the network (macro-F1 0.585), and AT-all at ε = 0.1, the best configuration on seed 0 (Table S10), did not keep its advantage over five seeds (+8.3 pp against the undefended CNN, p = 0.234). On UNSW-NB15 the ε-ball defences were the least evaded CNNs under the corner search and the most evaded under the realistic attack. A wider sweep of both defences may still find better settings; Section S18.4 gives the full comparison.

**Feature-space hardening against realistic attacks.** Dyrmishi et al. (2023) found that unrealistic, feature-space hardening could protect a network detector against realistic attacks. At its own operating point AT-all changed the TON-IoT CNN's strongest-attack ASR by −30.5 pp against the undefended model, but by only −5.2 pp against the clean-loop control, and at the matched FPR it gave no protection on TON-IoT; on UNSW-NB15 it did not touch the TTL. On our data, the protection that feature-space hardening seemed to give came from the training loop and the shifted threshold.

**Trees: augmentation held at the same FPR.** Tree augmentation, a single retraining with the most benign-looking candidate of each of up to 20,000 training attack flows, lowered the strongest-attack ASR on TON-IoT of the GBDT from 35.3% to 3.0% and of the RF from 25.2% to 10.3% (five seeds, primary Holm p = 0.002 and < 0.001), with clean macro-F1 changes of +0.001 and −0.001 and FPR changes of 0.000 and 0.000. Under the same threshold rule and FPR, the share of TON-IoT attack flows missed or evaded fell from 16.9% to 1.6% for the GBDT and from 11.9% to 8.5% for the RF (Table 7). These are lower bounds from our searches, not exact attacks: the strongest attack includes the denser grid and the adaptive search, neither used to build the augmentation, and the saturation check left the augmented trees' ASR within 3.8 pp of it. A second round changed little (5.5% for the GBDT, 8.6% for the RF). Held-out actions showed the limit: trees augmented without delays were evaded on 9.9% (GBDT) and 19.5% (RF), and trees augmented without byte padding on 16.0% and 13.0%, between the undefended and the fully augmented trees. At β = 2, four times the augmentation budget, the grid plus adaptive search evaded the augmented GBDT on 23.9% of detected flows, against 60.0% without augmentation.

**UNSW-NB15, trees.** Against the TTL candidates it was built with, tree augmentation almost removed the attack: the grid search evaded the augmented RF and GBDT on 0.6% and 1.7% of detected flows, and the strongest volume-only attack on 1.1% and 2.2%. TTL values outside the candidates broke this: the TTL sweep over 1–255 alone evaded 17.8% and 20.8%, and the adaptive search with any TTL, combined with volume and timing, 30.6% and 39.9% (Figure 3). The strongest attack therefore evaded the augmented trees on 30.8% and 40.3% of detected flows, still well below the undefended trees (89.7% and 75.0%; primary Holm p < 0.001 and 0.002), at no cost in macro-F1 (−0.003 and +0.002) or FPR (+0.005 and 0.000). For the augmented GBDT this is a loose lower bound: a longer adaptive search with any TTL evaded a further 10.2% of a 500-flow subsample (Table S8). A second round lowered the strongest ASR to 24.9% (RF) and 37.7% (GBDT). Trees augmented without delays and attacked with them were evaded by volume and timing on 10.4% and 5.2% of detected flows, more than with delays in training. Evaluated only against its own TTL candidates, tree augmentation would have looked like nearly complete protection on this dataset; it is not.

**Destination replies.** The attack does not model the destination's replies to extra packets (Section 3). As a sensitivity check (Table S17), each added packet drew one reply of 40 header bytes, which raised the destination-side packet and byte counters on TON-IoT and the destination bytes and load on UNSW-NB15, with the connection state unchanged, and the β = 0.5 grid was repeated with the same candidates. For the undefended models the grid ASR changed by −3.1 pp to +4.6 pp on TON-IoT and −0.8 pp to +12.2 pp on UNSW-NB15 (volume and timing), with evasions both lost and gained, so the direction depends on the model. The defences, all trained without replies, were evaded more (+3.6 pp to +9.9 pp and +3.4 pp to +25.0 pp): on TON-IoT the grid evaded AT-threat on 14.3% instead of 4.4% of detected flows and the augmented GBDT on 10.0% instead of 1.6%. The largest effect was on the UNSW-NB15 network defences: with volume and timing actions, the grid evaded AT-threat on 28.8% instead of 8.8% and its corner-start variant on 36.0% instead of 11.0%, against 57.8% for the undefended CNN with replies, so most of their advantage at their own threshold depends on the destination not replying. These grid ASRs are at each model's own threshold, not at the matched FPR of Table 7. An attacker who can provoke replies therefore gains against defences built without them, and a defence should be trained with the destination's reaction modelled; under this reply model the defended trees on TON-IoT remain well below the undefended ones (28.6% for the GBDT).

**Costs and rare classes.** Every network defence, the clean-loop control included, lowered rare-class F1 (paired, −0.459 to −0.061 on TON-IoT and −0.120 to −0.040 on UNSW-NB15) and raised the benign FPR (+0.020 to +0.264 and +0.011 to +0.043; the extremes on TON-IoT are the collapsed BPDA variant of AT-all). CCAT, which leaves the rare classes unperturbed, kept rare-class F1 above AT-all (0.459 against 0.349 on TON-IoT, 0.211 against 0.192 on UNSW-NB15), but below the undefended CNN (0.620 and 0.307) and below AT-threat (0.521 and 0.267). ## 6. Discussion
### 6.1 Implications
Five lessons follow for evaluating the adversarial robustness of flow-based detectors.

First, the threat model has to be stated in the problem space, because the ε-ball can rank defences wrongly. A weak ε-PGD made the ε-ball defences look robust in their own ball, and on UNSW-NB15 they were the least evaded CNNs under the corner search but the most evaded under the realistic attack. A strong ε-ball attack also misstated the size of the threat, mostly upwards and, for the networks, partly by changing independently features that the attacker can only change together; as an upper bound on evasion it is usually safe, as a guide to which defence to deploy it is not.

Second, defences must be compared at the same operating point and decision rule, preferably over a range of false-positive rates, and with the share of attacks missed or evaded. Every network defence made the detector flag more traffic, which lowers the ASR at its own threshold without making it more robust; with rule and FPR matched at one threshold, the network defences gave no detectable gain on TON-IoT. Over a range of FPRs, adversarial training on the attacker's actions did help there under one decision rule but not under another, and on UNSW-NB15 the action-space defences helped only at the undefended model's high FPR. A defence evaluation should therefore report a range of operating points and more than one decision rule.

Third, a gradient-free search evaluates networks and trees on equal terms when the attacker controls only a few quantities, provided it is checked for saturation and complemented by an adaptive search; white-box attacks through piecewise-linear pre-processing were weaker than black-box search. Retraining trees once on attack flows modified within the threat model lowered evasion at no cost in accuracy or false-positive rate, whereas adversarial training of the networks with an inner search started from the same candidates did not lower it detectably on TON-IoT. Neither defence generalised fully: tree augmentation covered TTL values and actions it was not built with only partly, an evaluation with its own candidates would have overstated it greatly, and every defence lost part of its gain when the destination replied to the added packets.

Fourth, the budget model matters. Most TON-IoT evasions relied on the absolute floors of the budget, and with purely proportional budgets the gap between networks and trees almost vanished (Section 5.1); robustness results should report this dependence.

Fifth, benchmark artefacts shape robustness results as they shape accuracy results (Arp et al., 2022). On UNSW-NB15 the TTL, a field the attacker sets for free and that separates the classes because of how the testbed was built (Flood et al., 2024), decided whether the networks could be evaded, and the detectors there are not deployable as they stand. Robustness estimates for the networks also varied by tens of percentage points between seeds, so single-seed results for networks are unreliable.

### 6.2 Limitations
- **Flow-level actions.** Derived features are recomputed, but modified packets are not replayed: retransmissions, fragmentation and the destination's reactions are not represented (Section 3). One reply per added packet changed the undefended models' grid ASR in both directions but raised the evasion of every defence, by up to +25.0 pp; integer packets and an MTU bound changed the grid results by at most 1.1 pp, and the strongest attack, the inner maximisers and the augmentation candidates were not re-run under these constraints.
- **Attacker.** It cannot split an attack over several flows, vary its source ports or imitate the destination, and its costs are modelled only through one budget family, whose additive floors decide much of the TON-IoT result. The query attacks assume the attacker sees the verdict, and each failed query raises an alert; the transfer surrogate shares the target's training data.
- **Lower bounds.** All ASRs come from searches. An exact mixed-integer attack on the trees (Kantchelian et al., 2016) would need, per flow, a program with a binary variable for every leaf, about 142,400 (TON-IoT) to 436,439 (UNSW-NB15) for the RF and up to 31 per tree for up to 1,000 boosted trees (GBDT), for hundreds of flows and several seeds; we did not attempt it, so the tree results are lower bounds, loose for the UNSW-NB15 GBDT.
- **Data and models.** Both datasets are testbed captures with known artefacts, the UNSW-NB15 detectors are weak, and N-BaIoT was excluded because its window features cannot be recomputed from one modified flow. Only compact models of fixed architecture were tested, on features selected on clean data before any attack.
- **Defences.** Each defence has one main configuration and one further configuration on a single seed. The network defences were not tested against a held-out action, and their inner search matched the trees' candidates only in the corner-start variant. The operating-point comparison rests on one threshold per model; over a range of FPRs (Section 5.3) the conclusions for the network defences depend on the operating point and the decision rule.
- **Statistics.** Seeds vary training randomness but not the data, so the tests are conditional on one training set; the further data splits have one seed. With five seeds and large between-seed variation, differences of about 20 pp at a single operating point cannot be detected. The MLP defence, the BPDA variant of AT-all, the further augmentation variants and the detectors without TTL features have three seeds, and the ASR is estimated on a fixed sample of 5,000 attack flows per dataset.

## 7. Conclusion
We evaluated four compact intrusion detectors on an IoT and an enterprise testbed against an attacker who can only pad, add packets to and delay its own flows and, on UNSW-NB15, set their TTL, with derived features recomputed and success counted only on detected attacks. A strong feature-space attack, even restricted to the features the attacker can change, overstated what volume and timing changes achieve on every model except the UNSW-NB15 trees at small ε, and a weak one misjudged the defences. Tree ensembles were more accurate and harder to evade than the networks, but on TON-IoT the gap rested on the absolute floors of the budget and on UNSW-NB15 on the TTL features. Defences for networks mostly moved the decision threshold. At the undefended model's rule and false-positive rate, none significantly reduced the share of TON-IoT attack flows missed or evaded, also with an inner search as strong as the tree augmentation's, although five seeds leave differences of about 20 pp undetectable; over false-positive rates of 0.5–5%, adversarial training on the attacker's actions did lower it, but only under one of two decision rules. On UNSW-NB15 its gain held only at the undefended model's false-positive rate of about 20%, and destination replies to the added packets, which no defence was trained with, removed most of it. One round of adversarial augmentation within the threat model cut the strongest-attack success against gradient-boosted trees on TON-IoT to at least 3.0% under our strongest search, without loss of accuracy or a change in the false-positive rate; if every added packet draws a destination reply, which the augmentation did not model, the grid evaded it on 10.0% of detected flows, and on UNSW-NB15 TTL values not used in the augmentation still evaded 30.8%–40.3% of detected flows. Robustness evaluations of flow-based detectors should state the threat model in the problem space, make both the realistic and the reference attack strong, compare defences at a matched operating point, test them against actions and values they were not trained on, and report dataset artefacts separately.
