## Title

**Fusing Feature Groups of One Flow Record for IoT Intrusion Detection: A Controlled Study on TON-IoT**

## Abstract

Intrusion detection studies increasingly encode semantic groups of flow features with separate neural branches and fuse them, reporting gains for this "multimodal" design. Such gains can also come from more features, extra parameters, an untuned baseline or a weak reference model. We test multi-stream fusion with controls for each on TON-IoT, the only non-saturated IoT benchmark in the study, on the enterprise UNSW-NB15 and on the saturated N-BaIoT: two semantic streams, compact 1D-CNN encoders, early, late and attention fusion, five seeds, same-size random feature splits, capacity-matched networks, a validation grid for the fusion networks and tree ensembles. One CNN on all features (early fusion) exceeded the better single stream by {{TON_IoT_d_early_best1}} macro-F1 on TON-IoT and {{UNSW_NB15_d_early_best1}} on UNSW-NB15; a random forest gained {{TON_IoT_d_rf_rf_best1}} and {{UNSW_NB15_d_rf_rf_best1}} from the same features. With a fixed configuration late fusion added {{TON_IoT_d_late_early}} on TON-IoT (significant after Holm correction), but once both were tuned, early and late fusion tied ({{TON_IoT_d_late_tuned_early_tuned}}); on UNSW-NB15 no difference between fusion architectures was significant after Holm correction. Attention fusion added nothing significant. Semantic instead of random streams of the same sizes changed late-fusion macro-F1 by {{TON_IoT_semrand_late}} on TON-IoT and {{UNSW_NB15_semrand_late}} on UNSW-NB15. Tree ensembles beat the tuned networks except on the saturated N-BaIoT; the most accurate, a gradient-boosted model, needs {{TON_IoT_gb_size_mb}} MB on TON-IoT against {{TON_IoT_late_size_kb}} kB for late fusion. Attention weights reflected routing, not stream usefulness. With min–max inputs, the volume-stream CNN failed silently on TON-IoT (macro-F1 {{TON_IoT_s1_mm}}); a tree reference exposed the failure.

**Keywords:** intrusion detection; Internet of Things; multimodal fusion; feature groups; deep learning; random forest; evaluation methodology

## 1. Introduction

Machine-learning intrusion detection systems (IDS) for the Internet of Things (IoT) are usually trained on flow records, vectors of a few dozen statistics computed per connection. A growing line of work treats subsets of these statistics as separate "modalities" or "views": byte and packet counts in one branch, protocol, port and connection-state fields in another, each encoded by its own network and fused by concatenation or attention. The motivation is borrowed from multimodal learning on images, audio and text (Baltrušaitis et al., 2019), where separate encoders are needed because the inputs differ in structure. Reported results are usually favourable: the fused model beats each single branch and often a single network on all features.

Such comparisons are hard to interpret. A fused model sees more features than either branch, so beating a branch says little about fusion. Fusion heads add parameters, so a gain over a single network may come from capacity rather than from the grouping. If the grouping matters, a semantic split should outperform an arbitrary one of the same sizes, a control we have not seen reported. And tree ensembles remain strong on tabular data (Grinsztajn et al., 2022; Shwartz-Ziv & Armon, 2022), so a fusion network that is not compared with one on the same features may be solving a problem that does not exist. These are instances of the evaluation pitfalls documented for machine learning in security (Arp et al., 2022).

We carried out this comparison with these controls in place. The study replaces an earlier, unpublished version of this work, which reported a dual-stream framework evaluated on a single seed; on re-running it with a corrected data pipeline we found that its central observation, a flow-feature branch that "fails" on TON-IoT, was a training failure caused by input scaling (Section 5.5). Our contributions are:

1. A controlled case study of early, late and attention fusion of two semantic feature streams on TON-IoT, with five seeds, paired comparisons, a seeds × test-records bootstrap and a Holm-corrected family of tests. UNSW-NB15 (an enterprise benchmark) serves as a non-IoT robustness check and N-BaIoT as a ceiling check; neither is a second IoT case.
2. Controls that, to our knowledge, have not been combined in fusion-IDS work: random feature splits of the same sizes, single networks matched to late fusion in parameters and head, late fusion with both encoders on all features, a validation grid for the fusion networks, tuned tree ensembles on all features and on each stream, an order-independent encoder, and repeated data splits.
3. An analysis of attention weights as explanations: whether they follow which stream classifies a class better, and whether they are stable across seeds.
4. A worked example of a known pitfall: with min–max scaled, heavy-tailed flow counts the batch-normalised CNN learned almost nothing from the volume stream, and the earlier version of this study read the result as a property of the stream; a tree reference on the same features exposed it.

Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 describes the data and streams, Section 4 the models and protocol, Section 5 the results, and Section 6 discusses implications and limitations.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Fusion of feature groups in intrusion detection

Learning-based IDS for IoT have moved from single networks on flow features (Diro & Chilamkurti, 2018; Imrana et al., 2021; see the surveys of Chaabouni et al., 2019, and Lansky et al., 2021) to designs with several input branches. Some fuse inputs that differ in kind. Kiflay et al. (2024) combine six flow features with the first payload bytes of each UNSW-NB15 flow, using two random forests and decision-level fusion; Xu et al. (2025) fuse a byte-level image of the first packets of a flow with the flow's statistics; Ullah et al. (2024) fuse text-like and image-like encodings of packet contents; and Singh et al. (2026) combine network and host audit data. Others derive their branches from one flow table, which is the setting we test. Attota et al. (2021) train an ensemble of networks on multiple views of IoT network data in a federated multi-view IDS; Ayantayo et al. (2023) split UNSW-NB15 and NSL-KDD features by data type and compare early and late fusion; Dinh et al. (2024a) give each feature group its own sub-encoder in a multi-input autoencoder; Sanjalawe et al. (2025) use a dual-stream attention network on selected IIoT features; and AboulEla and Kashef (2026) fuse numeric, text, graph and quantum-inspired representations of the same NetFlow records. Attention over features is common (Al Qathrady et al., 2024; Ishtiaq et al., 2025), and some "dual-path" designs feed the same full feature vector to both paths (Alabbadi & Bajaber, 2025). Reported accuracies on IoT benchmarks are often near 100% (Ishtiaq et al., 2025; Afraji et al., 2025, for binary classification), where differences between architectures cannot be resolved.

The comparisons offered for fusion are usually against the model's own single branches. Of 14 recent fusion, multi-view and attention-based IDS papers (Appendix A, Table A.1), eight read in full text, none reported a random grouping of features, a single network with a matched parameter count, or results over several seeds, and two trained a tree ensemble on the same data; the abstracts of the other six mention none of these except one tree ensemble. Where trees were compared, they were competitive. In Singh et al. (2026) tree ensembles on the fused features were more accurate than the fused CNN; in Dinh et al. (2024b) a random forest on the original features matched the multi-input autoencoder followed by a random forest on UNSW-NB15 (F-score 0.727 against 0.728); and Kiflay et al. (2024) found decision-level fusion better than one forest on the concatenated features, a positive result for fusing inputs that differ in kind. AboulEla and Kashef (2026) report modest, dataset-dependent gains, and Li et al. (2025) found multi-view models on TON-IoT and UNSW-NB15 generally, but not always, better than single-view ones.

The closest studies to ours are Ayantayo et al. (2023), who compare early and late fusion of data-type groups of UNSW-NB15 features, and Li et al. (2025). We differ from the first in holding the parameter count and the head fixed and in replacing the semantic grouping by random ones, and from the second in that our streams come from one flow record rather than from different sources. Our question is narrower than theirs: given one set of flow features, does grouping and fusing them help?

### 2.2 Multimodal learning

Multimodal learning combines inputs of different structure, such as images, audio and text (Baltrušaitis et al., 2019; Ramachandram & Taylor, 2017). Separate encoders are needed there because a convolution suited to images does not suit text, and fusion may be early (inputs), late (representations or decisions) or attention-based (Bahdanau et al., 2015; Vaswani et al., 2017; Nagrani et al., 2021). Even with true modalities, joint training is not automatically better than the best single modality (Wang et al., 2020), and one modality can dominate optimisation and leave the other under-used (Peng et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2022). Du et al. (2023) show that jointly trained multimodal networks can learn weaker uni-modal features than separately trained ones, and that ensembles of uni-modal models can match joint training; Liang et al. (2024) survey these questions. Attention weights, often presented as explanations, need not agree with other measures of importance (Jain & Wallace, 2019), although whether they can serve as explanations depends on how they are tested (Wiegreffe & Pinter, 2019).

### 2.3 Baselines and evaluation in ML-based security

On tabular data, tree ensembles remain strong competitors of deep networks (Grinsztajn et al., 2022; Shwartz-Ziv & Armon, 2022; McElfresh et al., 2023). Security-specific pitfalls of machine-learning evaluation, including inappropriate baselines and spurious correlations, have been documented repeatedly (Sommer & Paxson, 2010; Arp et al., 2022; Apruzzese et al., 2023), and benchmark datasets themselves contain errors (Engelen et al., 2021). Our study applies these lessons to fusion architectures: it asks what the fusion adds once the features, the parameter count and a strong tabular baseline are controlled.

## 3. Data and Feature Streams

### 3.1 Datasets and pre-processing

TON-IoT contains network flows from an IoT/IIoT testbed with nine attack classes (backdoor, DDoS, DoS, injection, MITM, password, ransomware, scanning, XSS) and benign traffic (Alsaedi et al., 2020; Moustafa, 2021). UNSW-NB15 contains flows from an enterprise-style testbed with nine attack categories (Moustafa & Slay, 2015); it is not an IoT dataset, and we keep it as a second, independent benchmark. Both pass through the corrected pre-processing pipeline of our companion studies, released with this paper, in this order. Identifier and free-text columns are removed; categorical fields with at most 30 distinct values (on TON-IoT protocol, service, connection state and DNS flags) are one-hot encoded (the category sets are fixed on the full data before the split, without labels, and the first category is dropped) and those with more are dropped; exact duplicates are removed after all column drops. After the split described next, numeric columns with more than 40% missing values in the training split are dropped, remaining gaps are filled with the training median, infinite values are replaced by the training 99th percentile, and constant features are removed. The data are then split into stratified training, validation and test sets (70/15/15), and validation and test records identical to a training record (all columns and the label) are removed. Feature selection is fitted on the training split: a mutual-information filter (threshold 0.05) followed by recursive feature elimination with three-fold cross-validation (RFECV), which ranks features with a 50-tree random forest, scores subsets by macro-F1 on a 50,000-record subsample and keeps at least ten features. The selected features are min–max scaled (fitted on the training split), and the training split is oversampled with SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002); validation and test sets are never resampled. SMOTE interpolates between records, so synthetic records can carry fractional values in one-hot columns. On UNSW-NB15 the cross-validated macro-F1 of RFECV was highest at its floor ({{UNSW_NB15_rfecv_best}} with ten features against {{UNSW_NB15_rfecv_all}} with all {{UNSW_NB15_rfecv_n_in}}), so only ten features remain and the streams there are small.

N-BaIoT contains benign, BASHLITE and Mirai traffic of nine commercial IoT devices, described by {{N_BaIoT_n_feat}} stream statistics computed by the Kitsune feature extractor (Meidan et al., 2018; Mirsky et al., 2018). We use the preparation of our federated-learning study: a uniform 3% sample of every device–class file, duplicates removed at 32-bit precision, the two BASHLITE flood classes dropped (their records collapse to fewer than 100 distinct vectors), a 70/15/15 split by device and class with the same cross-split guard, min–max scaling fitted on the training split, all features kept and no oversampling, because the classes are nearly balanced. Kitsune features are statistics over sliding time windows, so neighbouring windows of one device can fall into both the training and the test set; together with its saturated scores (Section 5.1), this makes N-BaIoT a ceiling check rather than a test of the architectures. ### 3.2 Semantic streams

Each dataset's selected features are split into two streams by a fixed rule (Table 1). The rule was fixed before any model of this study was trained, but it is the split of the earlier version of this work, whose results we had seen. On TON-IoT and UNSW-NB15, stream 1 holds the volume and timing of the flow (duration, bytes and packets per direction, load, mean packet size, ACK delay) and stream 2 everything else: on TON-IoT ports, protocol, service, connection state and DNS fields, on UNSW-NB15 the time-to-live and connection-count fields. Grouping the features of one flow table into separately encoded subsets is common in the fusion literature, although with other rules (Ayantayo et al., 2023, group by data type; Dinh et al., 2024a, give each feature group its own sub-encoder); we know of no study that uses exactly this volume-versus-behaviour split. On N-BaIoT, stream 1 holds the host-level statistics (Kitsune's MI_dir and H groups, aggregated over all traffic from the source) and stream 2 the channel and socket statistics (HH, HH_jit and HpHp groups), a split defined by the feature extractor itself. The streams are unequal in size: {{TON_IoT_n_s1}} and {{TON_IoT_n_s2}} features on TON-IoT, {{UNSW_NB15_n_s1}} and {{UNSW_NB15_n_s2}} on UNSW-NB15, and {{N_BaIoT_n_s1}} and {{N_BaIoT_n_s2}} on N-BaIoT.

## 4. Models and Protocol

### 4.1 Encoders and fusion

Every stream is encoded by the same compact 1D-CNN, which treats the feature vector as a one-channel sequence: a convolution with 64 filters (kernel 3) and batch normalisation, max-pooling, a convolution with 128 filters and batch normalisation, global average pooling and a 64-unit dense embedding. Five architectures are compared (Figure 1):

- **Stream 1 only** and **stream 2 only**: one encoder on one stream, dropout 0.3 and a softmax layer.
- **Early fusion**: the same single encoder on the concatenated features of both streams ({{TON_IoT_params_single}} parameters on TON-IoT). This is simply one CNN on all features; we keep the name used in the fusion literature, where concatenating the inputs is called early fusion.
- **Late fusion**: one encoder per stream, the two embeddings concatenated and passed through a 128-unit dense layer, dropout and softmax ({{TON_IoT_params_late}} parameters).
- **Attention fusion**: one encoder per stream; a dense layer with a two-way softmax on the concatenated embeddings produces one weight per stream and record, the embeddings are summed with these weights and passed through a 64-unit dense layer, dropout and softmax ({{TON_IoT_params_attn}} parameters, fewer than late fusion because of the smaller head). The weight of stream 1 is computed for every test record; class means and within-class spreads are saved.

All networks are trained with Adam (learning rate 10⁻³, halved after 4 epochs without improvement of the validation loss, minimum 10⁻⁵), batch size 512, on the oversampled training set without class weights, for at most 120 epochs with early stopping on validation loss (patience 10). The weights of the epoch with the lowest validation loss are restored in every run, including runs that reach the budget; {{n_capped}} of the {{n_cnn_runs_main}} new network runs of the main and capacity experiments used the full budget (N-BaIoT stream 2, seed 4, best epoch {{best_epoch_max}}, so it was still improving at the cap; N-BaIoT is saturated and outside the confirmatory family). Network inputs are the selected features after a quantile transform to the uniform distribution (1,000 quantiles, fitted on a 200,000-record subsample of the oversampled training set, or on the whole training set of N-BaIoT, which is smaller and not oversampled); Section 5.5 explains why. Fitting the transform on the oversampled set lets synthetic SMOTE records shape the quantiles; fitting it on real records only would have been an equally valid choice.

### 4.2 Controls

**Tree ensembles.** A random forest (RF; scikit-learn, Pedregosa et al., 2011; 200 trees) and a histogram gradient-boosting classifier (GBDT; up to 1,000 iterations with early stopping after 20 iterations without improvement on an internal 10% split, which scikit-learn draws from the oversampled training set, so synthetic records take part in choosing the iteration) are trained on all features and on each stream, on the min–max scaled, oversampled training set. Their hyperparameters (RF: maximum depth 20 or unlimited, minimum leaf size 1 or 2; GBDT: learning rate 0.05 or 0.1, 31 or 127 leaves) are chosen on the validation macro-F1 with all features and then fixed. {{gb_rollback_note}} On UNSW-NB15 the GBDT ran to {{UNSW_NB15_gb_n_iter_range}} iterations, at or near the cap, so its result is limited by the iteration budget and may understate the GBDT there. Tree splits depend only on the order of values within a feature, so a monotone transform such as the quantile transform would change the trees little (split thresholds and the histogram bins of the GBDT can still move); we therefore train them on the min–max features. To give the networks a comparable chance, early and late fusion were also tuned on a small grid (learning rate 10⁻³ or 3 × 10⁻⁴, dropout 0.1 or 0.3, encoder width ×1 or ×2; eight configurations that include the fixed one), chosen like the trees on validation macro-F1 with seed 0 and then trained with five seeds (Section 5.2). All other networks use the fixed configuration of Section 4.1. Because the RFECV selector itself uses a random forest, the selected features may suit tree models; we return to this in Section 6.3.

**Capacity.** Late fusion has about 2.5 times as many parameters as the single network, and a different head. Three further networks separate these factors: early fusion with every layer widened by a factor of 1.6 (**wide early fusion**, {{TON_IoT_early_wide_params}} parameters on TON-IoT, against {{TON_IoT_params_late}} for late fusion); early fusion with the same 128-unit head as late fusion (**early fusion with head**); and late fusion in which both encoders see all features (**late fusion on all features**), which keeps the late-fusion architecture but removes the grouping.

**Random feature splits.** To test whether the semantic grouping matters, the features are also split at random into two streams of the same sizes as the semantic ones (10 splits per dataset), in the spirit of the random subspace method (Ho, 1998). The single-stream networks, late and attention fusion and the per-stream trees are trained on every random split; split r uses seed r mod 5, so each seed occurs with exactly two random splits. Every run on TON-IoT and UNSW-NB15 was made with the present code and saved its test predictions. On N-BaIoT, which is saturated and outside the confirmatory family, {{n_reused_rand}} random-split runs and {{n_reused_main}} semantic-split runs are taken from an earlier run of the same experiment that differed only in a 60-epoch cap and in restoring the best weights only when early stopping fired; these runs stopped before the cap, so they were scored at their best epoch.

**Column order and encoder type.** A 1D convolution depends on the order of its inputs, so a random split changes not only the grouping but also which features are neighbours. Early fusion is therefore repeated on five random orders of all features (order k trained with seed k, so order and seed are confounded; we compare the spread over orders with the spread over seeds). On TON-IoT and UNSW-NB15, all five architectures and the random-split experiment are also repeated with a multilayer-perceptron encoder (two 128-unit layers with batch normalisation and a 64-unit embedding), which does not depend on feature order.

**Data splits.** Seeds vary initialisation, batch order and dropout, but not the data. On TON-IoT and UNSW-NB15 the experiment is therefore repeated on five further stratified 70/15/15 splits (single-stream, early, late and attention networks, the RF on all features and on each stream, and the GBDT on all features; one seed per split). The selected features are kept from the original split, and scaling, oversampling and the quantile transform are refitted on each new training split. Feature selection used labels of records that now fall into the new test sets; this affects all models alike but makes the replicates slightly optimistic.

### 4.3 Evaluation

The primary metric is macro-F1 on the test set; we also report the benign false-positive rate (FPR, the share of benign test records classified as an attack), the attack detection rate and the macro-averaged area under the precision–recall curve (one-vs-rest). On the original split, each configuration is trained with five seeds (0–4), and the same seed is used for every architecture. GPU training is not bit-wise deterministic: of {{rerun_n}} runs repeated with the same seed, {{rerun_same_epoch}} reached their best epoch at the same point, and macro-F1 changed by {{rerun_dF_mean}} on average (at most {{rerun_dF_max}}; at most {{TON_IoT_rerun_dF_max}} on TON-IoT). Networks are compared by paired differences over seeds, reported as the mean with a t-based 95% confidence interval and the number of seeds in which the difference is positive; differences below 10⁻⁶ count as ties, and an interval bound printed as 0.000 has the sign given at full precision in Table B.1. Tree ensembles and networks share no initialisation, so they are compared with Welch's t-test. The confirmatory family was fixed before the experiments reported here were run. It consists of four paired t-tests on each of TON-IoT and UNSW-NB15: late against early fusion, attention against early fusion, late fusion against wide early fusion, and late fusion against late fusion on all features. The first two were planned from the start; the two capacity comparisons were added after review of an earlier draft, before their runs were made. Holm correction is applied over these {{holm_family_size}} tests. Five seeds on one fixed test set describe training noise but not test-sampling noise, so for each test of the family we also report a hierarchical bootstrap (2,000 resamples: seeds drawn with replacement, then test records drawn with replacement, and the paired difference in macro-F1 recomputed from the saved predictions). N-BaIoT is at the ceiling and is not part of the family, and all other comparisons are descriptive. In particular, the direct comparison of semantic and random splits (Section 5.3), which addresses the central grouping question, is descriptive; late fusion against late fusion on all features is its confirmatory proxy. Appendix B (Table B.1) lists the whole family with unadjusted and Holm-adjusted p-values; the confidence intervals reported in the text and tables are unadjusted. A non-significant test is not evidence of no effect. We therefore also read each difference against a margin of ±0.01 macro-F1: a difference whose confidence interval lies inside the margin is called negligible, and one whose interval crosses it is called inconclusive. The margin was chosen after the first results were seen, so it is a reading aid, not a pre-registered equivalence test, and it is not negligible for every class: with ten classes on TON-IoT, 0.01 macro-F1 is a change of 0.1 in the F1 of one class, so we also report which class a difference comes from. The five further data splits give split-level replicates: the original split (mean over seeds) and the five new splits form six paired observations per comparison. Semantic and random splits are compared within seeds: for each seed, the semantic run minus the mean of the two random splits trained with that seed, giving five paired differences. Experiments ran on one laptop (NVIDIA GPU for network training); inference latency was measured separately on its CPU with no other jobs running.

### 5.4 What do the attention weights say?

Attention fusion assigns each record a weight for each stream, and such weights are often presented as an explanation of which "modality" drives a decision. Averaged over test records, stream 1 receives {{TON_IoT_attn_w_mean}} of the weight on TON-IoT (range over seeds {{TON_IoT_attn_w_range}}), {{UNSW_NB15_attn_w_mean}} on UNSW-NB15 and {{N_BaIoT_attn_w_mean}} on N-BaIoT ({{N_BaIoT_attn_w_range}}). The weights vary between classes (on TON-IoT from {{TON_IoT_attn_class_min_w}} for {{TON_IoT_attn_class_min}} to {{TON_IoT_attn_class_max_w}} for {{TON_IoT_attn_class_max}}) and also within a class (mean within-class standard deviation {{TON_IoT_attn_sd_within}}).

If the weights explained the model, a class should receive more weight on the stream that classifies it better. We correlated each class's mean stream-1 weight with its stream advantage (Figure 3), the per-class F1 of the stream-1 CNN minus that of the stream-2 CNN (Spearman's ρ over classes, computed for each seed's weights against the seed-averaged stream advantage). The correlation is weak on TON-IoT (mean ρ = {{TON_IoT_attn_rho_adv}}, range over seeds {{TON_IoT_attn_rho_adv_range}}) and moderate on UNSW-NB15 ({{UNSW_NB15_attn_rho_adv}}, {{UNSW_NB15_attn_rho_adv_range}}); with ten classes, a correlation of this size is imprecise (permutation p for the seed-averaged weights {{TON_IoT_attn_rho_adv_p}} and {{UNSW_NB15_attn_rho_adv_p}}; all p-values in this section are exploratory). An in-model ablation separates what the weights describe from what they are taken to mean. Replacing one stream's embedding by its mean over the test set, with the attention weights kept, lowered macro-F1 by {{TON_IoT_abl_drop_s1}} for stream 1 and {{TON_IoT_abl_drop_s2}} for stream 2 on TON-IoT ({{UNSW_NB15_abl_drop_s1}} and {{UNSW_NB15_abl_drop_s2}} on UNSW-NB15, {{N_BaIoT_abl_drop_s1}} and {{N_BaIoT_abl_drop_s2}} on N-BaIoT). We define a class's reliance on stream 1 as the F1 lost when stream 1 is removed minus the F1 lost when stream 2 is removed. Reliance correlates strongly with the class's mean stream-1 weight (ρ = {{TON_IoT_attn_rho_rel}}, {{UNSW_NB15_attn_rho_rel}} and {{N_BaIoT_attn_rho_rel}}; permutation p < 0.01 on each dataset), and similarly with the weight-scaled contribution of each stream's embedding. This agreement is largely built in, because the weight multiplies the embedding that the ablation removes: the weights describe how the trained model routes information. The substantive question is whether that routing follows stream usefulness. It does not: reliance and stream advantage correlate at ρ = {{TON_IoT_rel_rho_adv}} on TON-IoT (p = {{TON_IoT_rel_rho_adv_p}}) and {{UNSW_NB15_rel_rho_adv}} on UNSW-NB15 (p = {{UNSW_NB15_rel_rho_adv_p}}). On TON-IoT, for example, the DoS class gets the lowest mean stream-1 weight (the lowest in {{TON_IoT_attn_class_min_seeds}} of five seeds) although the volume stream classifies it slightly better than the protocol stream ({{TON_IoT_s1_pc_dos}} against {{TON_IoT_s2_pc_dos}}). On N-BaIoT, where both streams classify every class almost perfectly, the weights still range from {{N_BaIoT_attn_class_min_w}} ({{N_BaIoT_attn_class_min}}) to {{N_BaIoT_attn_class_max_w}} ({{N_BaIoT_attn_class_max}}), so they reflect how the model happened to route information, not which stream is needed. On TON-IoT and N-BaIoT the ranking of classes by weight also changes between seeds: the mean pairwise Spearman correlation between seeds is {{TON_IoT_attn_rho_seeds}} on TON-IoT (lowest pair {{TON_IoT_attn_rho_seeds_min}}) and {{N_BaIoT_attn_rho_seeds}} on N-BaIoT (lowest {{N_BaIoT_attn_rho_seeds_min}}), against {{UNSW_NB15_attn_rho_seeds}} on UNSW-NB15. Stream weights from a single trained model should therefore not be read as evidence of which feature group matters for an attack; this agrees with earlier findings that attention weights are not, by themselves, explanations (Jain & Wallace, 2019).

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 What the results mean for multi-stream IDS

Four conclusions follow for flow-feature IDS. First, the benefit attributed to fusion is mostly the benefit of using all features: combining streams helps relative to each stream, but one network on all features captures nearly all of it, and a random forest captures it without any neural network. Second, the architecture of fusion matters little. With one fixed configuration late fusion gained a small, consistent amount over early fusion on TON-IoT, but late fusion on all features largely reproduced it, it comes mainly from the extra 128-unit dense layer, and tuning early fusion on a small validation grid matched it entirely (tuned late minus tuned early {{TON_IoT_d_late_tuned_early_tuned}}); attention fusion gave no significant gain. Third, the semantic grouping itself contributes little: random groupings of the same sizes change the single streams considerably but the fused models by {{TON_IoT_semrand_late}} on TON-IoT and {{UNSW_NB15_semrand_late}} {{UNSW_NB15_semrand_late_ci}} on UNSW-NB15, a small benefit below our margin. The larger fusion gain of the semantic split mostly reflects its weaker individual streams. Fourth, tree ensembles on all features remain the stronger models after the networks are tuned, by {{TON_IoT_d_rf_bestnet}}–{{TON_IoT_d_gb_bestnet}} on TON-IoT and {{UNSW_NB15_d_rf_bestnet}}–{{UNSW_NB15_d_gb_bestnet}} on UNSW-NB15. The most accurate model, the GBDT, needs {{TON_IoT_gb_size_mb}} MB on TON-IoT; the tuned forests occupy hundreds of megabytes, and a forest of {{TON_IoT_rf_t25_d12_mb}} MB scored below late fusion (Section 5.6). We therefore see no evidence that the features of one flow record behave like modalities in the multimodal-learning sense. They come from the same observation and share a time base and, after transformation, a scale, and on these benchmarks they did not need different encoders.

These conclusions rest mainly on TON-IoT, the only non-saturated IoT dataset in the study, and on UNSW-NB15, an enterprise benchmark with ten selected features. They concern two small encoder families and one semantic split per dataset. Multi-stream designs may still be useful where the streams really are different sources: packet payloads or byte sequences alongside flow statistics, host logs alongside network flows, or features arriving from different sensors at different times, where one stream can be missing. Our results do not test these settings. Within a flow record, the gains they found are no larger than those of one network, or a tree ensemble, on all features.

### 6.2 Recommendations

For studies that propose fusion of feature groups for intrusion detection we recommend: (i) compare against one network on all features with matched parameters, not only against single branches; (ii) include random groupings of the same sizes; (iii) include a tree ensemble on the same features and on each group, which also detects training failures such as the one in Section 5.5; (iv) report several seeds with paired differences; and (v) do not interpret fusion weights as explanations without checking them against per-group performance and across seeds.

### 6.3 Limitations

The selected features come from a selector that ranks features with a random forest, which may favour the tree references; SMOTE produces fractional values in one-hot columns, which affects stream 2 on TON-IoT most. N-BaIoT shares time windows between training and test records and is saturated, and UNSW-NB15 has only ten selected features. The streams are one semantic split per dataset; other groupings (for example by protocol layer) were not tested, and ten random splits sample only a small part of the possible groupings. The encoders are small 1D-CNNs and MLPs, chosen for IoT deployment. Only early and late CNN fusion were tuned, on eight configurations with the choice made on one seed, and the other networks keep the fixed configuration; larger encoders or tabular transformers might use separate streams differently and might narrow the gap to the trees, as tuned deep tabular models do on general benchmarks (Gorishniy et al., 2021). UNSW-NB15 is not IoT traffic and N-BaIoT is saturated, so TON-IoT is the only non-saturated IoT benchmark in the study and the IoT evidence rests on it; a second non-saturated IoT benchmark such as CICIoT2023 (Neto et al., 2023) or Edge-IIoTset (Ferrag et al., 2022) would strengthen it. Five seeds give 95% intervals of about ±{{TON_IoT_ci_halfwidth}} on TON-IoT but about ±{{UNSW_NB15_ci_halfwidth}} on UNSW-NB15, so UNSW-NB15 can resolve only effects of about 0.01 and larger. Run-to-run variation on the GPU (Section 4.3) is of the same order as the smaller effects: on UNSW-NB15, retraining late fusion with the same configuration and seeds moved its five-seed mean by {{UNSW_NB15_d_late_tuned_late}} (Section 5.2). On UNSW-NB15 the column order of the CNN input matters about as much as the seed. The training curves of the failed min–max run were not saved. The data-split replicates keep the features selected on the original split. The CNN inputs use a quantile transform; with other remedies for heavy-tailed inputs (for example a log transform) the absolute numbers would differ slightly.

## 7. Conclusion

On the flow-feature benchmarks we studied, splitting the features of a record into semantic streams and fusing them added little beyond what one network, or a tree ensemble, achieves on all features: late fusion gained a few thousandths of macro-F1 on TON-IoT with a fixed configuration, a gain that did not depend on the grouping and that tuning early fusion matched, and nothing significant after Holm correction on UNSW-NB15, and attention fusion gave no significant gain. The gains such designs report over their own branches are real but come mostly from the features, not from the fusion. A random grouping of the same features served the fused model almost as well as the semantic one: within 0.002 on TON-IoT and by {{UNSW_NB15_semrand_late}} on UNSW-NB15. Tree ensembles beat the networks in macro-F1 also after the fusion networks were tuned; the most accurate, a gradient-boosted model, needs {{TON_IoT_gb_size_mb}} MB on TON-IoT, and forests of a few megabytes scored at or below late fusion. Attention weights over the streams describe how the model routes information, but are only loosely tied to stream usefulness and, on two of the three datasets, change between seeds. A misconfigured input scaling can silently disable one branch and reverse conclusions about which stream matters, and a tree-ensemble reference catches it. We release the code, the result files and the stream definitions so that these controls can be applied to other fusion designs.
