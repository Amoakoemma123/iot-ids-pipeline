## Title

**Supplementary Material: From Explanation Audit to Hardening: Leakage, Attribution Reliability and Transferable Counterfactual Training for Network Intrusion Detection**

This supplement gives the details behind the main text. Section, table and figure numbers without an "S" refer to the main article, where the references are listed.

## S1. Pre-processing and models

Both datasets pass through a shared pre-processing pipeline, released with our code. Identifier-like and free-text columns (row identifiers, IP addresses, DNS queries, HTTP and SSL strings) are removed, strings are normalised, numeric columns are typed, categorical fields with more than 30 values are dropped and the rest are one-hot encoded (dropping the first level). Exact duplicate records are then removed, so that duplicates are judged on the final feature columns, and the data are split into stratified training, validation and test sets (70/15/15, seed 42). Missing-value filtering, median imputation, the replacement of infinite values and the removal of constant columns are fitted on the training rows only, and any validation or test record identical to a training record is removed. Features are selected on the training set by a mutual-information filter (features with mutual information below {{mi_threshold}} are dropped) followed by recursive feature elimination with {{rfecv_cv}}-fold stratified cross-validation (RFECV; a 50-tree Random Forest scored by macro-F1 on a {{rfecv_subsample}}-record subsample, with a floor of {{rfecv_floor}} features), scaled to [0, 1], and the training set is oversampled with SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002), which raises every class to the size of the largest training class (the configured cap of 50,000 records per class does not bind on either dataset); validation and test sets are never resampled. On TON-IoT, RFECV keeps {{TON_nfeat}} of the {{TON_nfeat_mi}} features that pass the filter, an interior optimum. On UNSW-NB15 it keeps {{UNSW_nfeat}} of {{UNSW_nfeat_mi}}, exactly the floor; its cross-validated score is highest there and lower for every larger feature set, so the floor and the optimum coincide, but a lower floor could have selected fewer features. Deduplication and the cross-split check operate on the cleaned columns before feature selection; Section 3.3 reports how many test records become identical to a training record once only the selected features are kept.

The models come from a companion study by the same authors, a separate manuscript that benchmarks five detectors on the same corrected pipeline; the present paper reuses its seed-42 RF and CNN and their test performance (Table 2), and its own contribution is the analysis of their explanations and the counterfactual and hardening experiments, which the companion study does not address. We explain the RF, the best model of that study on both datasets (results in the released repository), and the CNN, the most compact competitive model (on UNSW-NB15 an autoencoder + RF scores slightly higher than the CNN, macro-F1 {{UNSW_v2_aerf_f1}} against {{UNSW_v2_cnn_f1}}). The RF (Breiman, 2001) has 200 trees (maximum depth 20, minimum leaf size 2, √p features per split). The CNN has two convolutional blocks (32 and 64 filters, kernel 3, batch normalisation, max-pooling), global average pooling and two dense layers (128 and 64 units, dropout 0.3), {{TON_cnn_params}} parameters in total; it is trained with Adam (learning rate halved after 7 epochs without improvement), class weights computed on the oversampled training labels (in which all classes have the same size, so the weights are uniform and have no effect), and early stopping on validation loss (patience 15). The convolutions run over the fixed order of the feature vector, in which neighbouring features need not be related; we use the CNN as a compact neural detector, not because the order carries locality. Both models are trained with three seeds (42, 43, 44) on the same data, split and selected features. For the explanation and counterfactual stages the seed also sets the random draws of those procedures (SHAP backgrounds, LIME and GradientExplainer sampling, deletion donors, counterfactual and hardening search), while the explained test records are fixed; ranges over seeds therefore combine retraining with explainer and search randomness. The seed-42 models are those of the companion study; the seed-42 CNN was trained without a fixed TensorFlow seed, so its exact values cannot be reproduced by retraining (the saved model is released). Seed 42 provides the record-level confidence intervals in the tables, and the other seeds the range over retrained models (Section 4.9). After feature selection TON-IoT has {{TON_nfeat}} features and UNSW-NB15 {{UNSW_nfeat}} (the original UNSW-NB15 set had {{UNSW_v1_nfeat}}, including `id`).

## S2. Audit of the id column: further comparisons and feature-space twins

We also compared the original model of the earlier pipeline with a model retrained without `id` and with feature selection run again, and a re-selected model with `id` against the corrected model on the deduplicated data; these comparisons also change other features (and, for the first, the earlier version of the pipeline), and give macro-F1 differences of {{dec_reselect_kept}} and {{dec_reselect_removed}}. The copied test records were nevertheless not easy cases for the original model: they were classified *less* accurately than the others (accuracy {{dec_Aseen_acc}} against {{dec_Aunseen_acc}}; macro-F1 {{dec_Aseen_f1}} against {{dec_Aunseen_f1}}), because duplicates are concentrated in classes with conflicting labels.

A weaker form of the same effect remains in the UNSW-NB15 data. Records that differ in the cleaned columns can coincide once only the selected features are kept: {{UNSW_fcopy_pct}}% of the UNSW-NB15 test records (including {{UNSW_fcopy_top}} of the test records of those two classes) are identical, label included, to a training record in the ten selected features, and {{UNSW_fcopy_feat_pct}}% match a training record's features with any label. These records are harder than the rest for the RF: excluding all {{UNSW_fcopy_feat_pct}}% raises its macro-F1 from {{UNSW_rf_f1}} to {{UNSW_rf_f1_nocopy}}. For the CNN the opposite holds ({{UNSW_cnn_f1}} to {{UNSW_cnn_f1_nocopy}}, in every seed), so the copies inflate the CNN's score slightly and deflate the RF's. On TON-IoT, with more features, exact coincidences are rare ({{TON_fcopy_pct}}% of test records; exact float equality is the right definition here, since rounding the scaled values would merge distinct small byte counts). Because the explanation samples and counterfactual query sets are drawn from this test set, Sections 5.2–5.4 repeat the key UNSW-NB15 measures without the records that have a feature-space twin in training, and Table S4 marks the classes in which most test records have one.

## S3. Method details

**Explainers.** For the RF we use interventional TreeSHAP (Lundberg et al., 2020) with probability output and a background of 200 random training records; for the CNN, SHAP's GradientExplainer (expected gradients; Erion et al., 2021) with the same background and its default of 200 samples per record (`nsamples` = 200). Models and explainers use scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011), TensorFlow (Abadi et al., 2016), SHAP 0.49 and LIME 0.2.0.1. LIME uses the tabular explainer without discretisation (continuous mode), which samples perturbations from independent Gaussians with the training mean and standard deviation of each feature (LIME's default sampling; its default representation would discretise the features into quartiles), weights them with LIME's default exponential kernel on the Euclidean distance in standardised feature space with the default kernel width 0.75√p ({{TON_kw}} on TON-IoT and {{UNSW_kw}} on UNSW-NB15), and fits a weighted ridge surrogate on all features; it draws 1,000 perturbation samples unless stated otherwise. S5 reports how the results change with half and twice the kernel width and with discretisation. Without discretisation the surrogate is fitted on standardised features, so a LIME weight is a slope per standard deviation of the feature. We therefore rank LIME's features by their contribution, the weight multiplied by the record's standardised value (x − μ)/σ, which, like a SHAP value, measures how far the feature moves this prediction from the average; ranking by the raw weights, which ignores where the record lies, is reported in S5 as a robustness check. Every explanation refers to the class the model predicts for that record.

**Agreement.** The earlier version of this analysis had compared class-averaged global SHAP with LIME weights pooled over 12 records, a comparison of two different objects, which these measures replace.

**Stability.** LIME samples its perturbations at random, so two runs on the same record can return different explanations. For {{N_STAB}} test records per class (seed-42 models), we run LIME {{N_RUNS}} times with 1,000 samples and 5 times with 5,000 samples, and report the mean pairwise Jaccard index of the top-5 sets (self-agreement). SHAP's values also depend on its random background and, for GradientExplainer, on random sampling along the integration path; we therefore recompute SHAP with three independent backgrounds for the RF and five background–seed combinations for the CNN and report its self-agreement in the same way. To separate the two sources of variation, we also recompute the CNN attributions with a fixed background and new random seeds at 200 and 1,000 samples, and with new backgrounds at 1,000 samples, and the RF attributions with backgrounds of 200 and of 1,000 records. The comparisons involve different numbers of runs (20 and 5 for LIME, 3 and 5 for SHAP); mean pairwise agreement does not depend on the number of runs, but the share of records above a threshold does somewhat. We also compare a consensus LIME explanation (the mean absolute weights of the 20 runs) with SHAP. We use 0.75 as a pragmatic threshold for an explanation an analyst could rely on, and report the share of records that reach it.

**Faithfulness.** Agreement and stability do not show that an explanation names the features the model actually uses. We therefore apply a deletion test (Warnecke et al., 2020; Samek et al., 2021): for each record in the agreement sample we replace the top-k features of an explanation (k = 1, 3, 5; ranked by absolute attribution, as for agreement) and measure the drop in the predicted-class probability. Because the ranking is by absolute value, the top-k set can contain features that push against the predicted class, whose replacement should raise confidence; S5 therefore also reports a ranking by signed attribution, which replaces only the features that push towards the predicted class. How a feature is 'removed' matters, and each common choice favours one explainer: values of random training records are exactly the background distribution of interventional SHAP, independent Gaussian draws are LIME's sampling distribution, and with LIME ranked by contribution, replacing a feature by its training mean removes exactly that feature's contribution w·(x − μ)/σ from the surrogate, so mean replacement is, if anything, favourable to LIME. We therefore use four strategies: the training mean; the values of random training records (averaged over 10 records drawn from 5,000); Gaussian draws with the training mean and standard deviation (10 draws); and a conditional strategy, which is the sampling distribution of neither explainer: the values of the 10 training records (of the same 5,000, excluding exact copies of the record) that are closest to the record on the features that are *not* replaced, which keeps the replaced values consistent with the rest of the record. Excluding the copies changes no mean confidence drop by more than {{UNSW_rf_condx_vs_cond_max}}. The baseline replaces k randomly chosen features (10 draws per record). A faithful explanation produces a larger drop than the random baseline, and we compare SHAP with LIME by paired bootstrap intervals of the per-record difference. Two robustness runs use the seed-42 models and the first {{N_SENS}} records per class of the agreement sample, with the same SHAP background and donors: LIME at 0.5, 1 and 2 times its default kernel width and in its default discretised mode (in which a LIME weight is already a contribution relative to the record's quartile bin), for agreement, surrogate fit and deletion; and deletion with the top-k features ranked by signed attribution, for SHAP and for continuous LIME at the default width.

**Counterfactual search.** We compare three search strategies: *random* (any k groups), *SHAP-guided* (always the k groups with the largest SHAP attribution for that record) and *attacker-controllable* (random groups among those an attacker can set from the attacking host: {{TON_attacker_groups}} on TON-IoT; the source TTL `sttl` alone and source volume on UNSW-NB15, where the TTL-derived `ct_state_ttl`, which also depends on the responder, is left unchanged; changing `sttl` alone can therefore produce combinations of `sttl` and `ct_state_ttl` that the dataset's derivation of `ct_state_ttl` would not generate, an inconsistency the support check guards against only partly). With only three and two such groups, the attacker-controllable search changes at most three groups on TON-IoT and two on UNSW-NB15. On UNSW-NB15 the five dependency groups cover all ten features, so a counterfactual that copies every group is a benign record and full evasion within four groups is expected by construction. For each attack class we query up to {{N_CF}} correctly detected test records per model and seed, and report the share that can be evaded, the number of groups changed, the groups most often involved and the number of model queries to the first evasion (counting every candidate up to the first accepted one, including candidates the support check rejects, which an attacker who applied the check first would not need to submit). Because candidates are sampled, the reported k is an upper bound on the true minimum.

**Hardening.** If counterfactuals point to real weaknesses, training on them should reduce evasion. For up to {{N_HARD_GEN}} correctly detected training records per attack class we generate counterfactuals against the RF, label them with their original attack class, add them to the (SMOTE-oversampled) training set and retrain the RF with the same settings. As a control we add the same number of synthetic attacks per class, each a random convex combination of two training attacks of that class, which tests whether any extra attack data would have the same effect. A second control applies the same benign-donor copying (the same set of groups, the same number of groups and the same support check) to random training attacks of each class, but without requiring the model to call the result benign (when none of 50 candidates passes the support check, the last one is kept, which happened for {{TON_copy_unsupported}} of the {{TON_hard_att_naug}} copies per seed on TON-IoT and {{UNSW_copy_unsupported}} on UNSW-NB15); it tests whether the gain comes from the evasion criterion rather than from benign-looking attack data. Benign donors are split into two disjoint pools, one for generating training counterfactuals and one for evaluating evasion. Evasion is evaluated on one fixed query set per seed: the test attacks (up to 60 per class) that all four models detect correctly. This set and its donor pool differ from those of Section 4.6, so the evasion rates of the original model differ somewhat between the two sections. We run the experiment twice, once with counterfactuals that may change any group and once with attacker-controllable groups only (in both generation and evaluation), for three seeds, and report evasion, macro-F1 and the benign false-positive rate. Because all models are evaluated on the same query records, we compare the counterfactual model with each other model by exact McNemar tests on the per-record evasion outcomes, per seed and pooled over seeds (exact test on the summed discordant pairs, a stratified McNemar test). Because aggregate false positives over thousands of benign test flows cannot reveal a local cost of adding attack-labelled records close to benign traffic, we also report the false-positive rate on the benign test records nearest to the added records (the five nearest to each added record).

**Statistics and computation.** Tables report means with percentile bootstrap 95% confidence intervals over records ({{N_BOOT}} resamples) for the seed-42 models, and the range of the means for seeds 42, 43 and 44. The bootstrap resamples records with replacement without stratifying by class; the samples themselves are class-stratified (up to {{N_LOCAL}} records per class), so their means weight classes equally rather than by traffic volume. For the headline agreement, counterfactual and hardening results we also give the mean over the three seeds with a two-level bootstrap interval (seeds resampled, then records within each seed); in the text, evasion rates are means over seeds unless marked as seed 42. No correction for multiple comparisons is applied; counts of significant combinations are descriptive, because the combinations share records, and we lead with effect sizes. Computation used a laptop with an Intel Core i5-10200H CPU, 16 GB RAM and an NVIDIA GTX 1650 (4 GB); SHAP and LIME ran on the CPU.

## S7. Counterfactual hardening: per-class differences, paired tests and support

The two augmentations also differ by class: for MITM the counterfactuals help more than the benign-donor copies ({{TON_hard_att_counterfactual_cls_mitm}}% against {{TON_hard_att_control_copy_cls_mitm}}%), for ransomware less ({{TON_hard_att_counterfactual_cls_ransomware}}% against {{TON_hard_att_control_copy_cls_ransomware}}%).

Judged by interval separation, the per-seed evidence looks weaker: the counterfactual model's 95% interval lies below those of the original model and the interpolation control in {{TON_hard_att_sep_ctl}} of 3 seeds on TON-IoT and {{UNSW_hard_att_sep_ctl}} of 3 on UNSW-NB15, and below that of the benign-donor control in {{TON_hard_att_sep_copy}} and {{UNSW_hard_att_sep_copy}} of 3 (seed 42 on UNSW-NB15: {{UNSW_hard_att_counterfactual_ev42}} against {{UNSW_hard_att_control_copy_ev42}}). Interval separation is a conservative criterion for outcomes that are paired by record. Exact McNemar tests on the shared query records give, for counterfactuals against benign-donor copies, {{UNSW_mcn_copy_p_byseed}} in seeds 42, 43 and 44 on UNSW-NB15 (pooled over seeds {{UNSW_mcn_copy_pooled_p}}; mean difference in evasion {{UNSW_mcn_copy_diff_3s}} points, seed × record interval) and {{TON_mcn_copy_p_byseed}} on TON-IoT (pooled {{TON_mcn_copy_pooled_p}}; {{TON_mcn_copy_diff_3s}} points). Against the original model they give {{UNSW_mcn_orig_p_byseed}} on UNSW-NB15 and {{TON_mcn_orig_p_byseed}} on TON-IoT. {{UNSW_mcn_conclusion}} McNemar's test treats each trained model as fixed and so reflects variation between records only, whereas the seed × record interval also includes variation between retrained models; on TON-IoT the counterfactuals' advantage over benign-donor copies ({{TON_mcn_copy_diff_3s}} points) is significant in {{TON_mcn_copy_nsig}} of 3 seeds and therefore not robust to retraining.{{hp_note}}

What these evasions mean, and what the hardening costs, depends on the support check. An attacker-controllable evasion is only possible if the features the attacker cannot change already lie within the support radius of a benign training record. This holds for {{TON_prox_evaded}}% of the attacks the original RF lets evade on TON-IoT and {{UNSW_prox_evaded}}% on UNSW-NB15, as it must, since an evading counterfactual passes the support check on all features and keeps the uncontrollable ones; the informative figures are those for the attacks it does not let evade, {{TON_prox_notevaded}}% and {{UNSW_prox_notevaded}}% (pooled over seeds). On TON-IoT evadable attacks are therefore largely those whose uncontrollable part already resembles benign traffic, whereas on UNSW-NB15 the condition hardly separates evadable from other attacks. Adding hundreds of attack-labelled records close to benign traffic could raise false positives locally even when the aggregate rate is unchanged. On the benign test records nearest to the added counterfactuals (the five nearest to each; {{TON_lfpr_n_cf_knn5}} records per seed on TON-IoT and {{UNSW_lfpr_n_cf_knn5}} on UNSW-NB15), the false-positive rate is {{TON_lfpr_counterfactual_cf_knn5}}% after counterfactual retraining against {{TON_lfpr_original_cf_knn5}}% for the original RF on TON-IoT, and {{UNSW_lfpr_counterfactual_cf_knn5}}% against {{UNSW_lfpr_original_cf_knn5}}% on UNSW-NB15; near the benign-donor copies it is {{TON_lfpr_control_copy_copy_knn5}}% against {{TON_lfpr_original_copy_knn5}}% (TON-IoT) and {{UNSW_lfpr_control_copy_copy_knn5}}% against {{UNSW_lfpr_original_copy_knn5}}% (UNSW-NB15; means over seeds). The added counterfactuals thus barely change false positives near them on TON-IoT; on UNSW-NB15 most of the nearby benign records are already misclassified by the original RF, and the rate rises by about two further points. Without the UNSW-NB15 query records that have a feature-space twin in training ({{UNSW_hard_notwin_nquery}} records per seed remain), evasion is {{UNSW_hard_notwin_original_ev}}% for the original RF, {{UNSW_hard_notwin_counterfactual_ev}}% after counterfactual retraining and {{UNSW_hard_notwin_control_copy_ev}}% with benign-donor copies.
