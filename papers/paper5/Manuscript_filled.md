## Title

**From Explanation Audit to Hardening: Leakage, Attribution Reliability and Transferable Counterfactual Training for Network Intrusion Detection**

## Abstract

Explanations are increasingly offered as the trust layer of machine-learning intrusion detection systems (IDS), but are rarely measured. We audit the explanations of a Random Forest (RF) and a compact 1D-CNN on TON-IoT and UNSW-NB15 over three seeds, and test whether counterfactual analysis can harden the detector. In an earlier pipeline version, SHAP ranked the UNSW-NB15 row identifier first: it encoded file order and had disabled duplicate removal (38.6% of records are redundant copies); with features held fixed, the identifier raised RF accuracy with and without exact duplicates. The corrected models lean on a second shortcut, the source TTL. SHAP and LIME share 1.86 of their top-5 features per RF alert on TON-IoT; default LIME is unstable on TON-IoT and for the CNN, whose default SHAP is less stable still, and SHAP's top features lowered confidence more than LIME's in every RF and most CNN deletion tests. Counterfactuals that copy dependent sender-controlled fields from benign flows evade 13–42% of detected attacks. Retraining on them cut RF evasion from 41% to 16% on TON-IoT and from 43% to 32% on UNSW-NB15 without loss of macro-F1; the gain transferred to a score-guided search and to partial moves with new benign donors: against an attacker using any of the three searches, evasion fell from 44% to 18% and from 45% to 38%. A control copying benign values without the evasion criterion matched most of this on TON-IoT, but not against the combined attacker. Single local explanations do not reliably explain individual alerts.

**Keywords:** explainable AI; intrusion detection; Internet of Things; SHAP; LIME; counterfactual explanations; data leakage

## 1. Introduction

Machine-learning intrusion detection systems (IDS) for the Internet of Things (IoT) report high detection rates on public benchmarks, yet operators are slow to deploy them because the models do not say why an alert was raised. Post-hoc explanation methods, mainly SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) and LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016), are therefore increasingly attached to IDS models as a transparency layer. Most IDS studies that use them show a global importance chart and a few local examples, and then conclude that the model has become interpretable. Whether these explanations are stable, whether different methods agree for the same alert, and whether the features they name actually drive the prediction are rarely measured, although work outside the IDS literature shows that each of these can fail (Krishna et al., 2024; Nauta et al., 2023).

This paper measures these properties for two IDS models, a Random Forest (RF) and a compact one-dimensional convolutional network (CNN), on TON-IoT and UNSW-NB15. The study began as a case of explanations at work. In an earlier, unpublished version of our pipeline, SHAP ranked `id`, the sequential row number of the UNSW-NB15 file, as the most important feature of both models. A row number carries no information about network traffic, so its top rank meant that the models had learned an artefact of the file. Following the trail showed a second problem: because every row had a different `id`, the duplicate-removal step removed nothing from UNSW-NB15, although 67,601 of its records (38.6%) are redundant copies of another record once `id` is set aside. We report this audit as a case study, separate the effects of the identifier and the duplicates experimentally, correct the data, and evaluate the explanations of the corrected models with three training seeds, class-stratified samples and confidence intervals.

The paper makes four contributions:

1. An explanation-led data audit, reported as a case study, with a controlled decomposition (Table 1) showing that the leaking identifier inflated UNSW-NB15 accuracy through the file order it encoded whether or not exact duplicates were present, and macro-F1 clearly only on the test set that kept them (on records common to both test sets neither effect was detectable), and that the corrected models turn to a second shortcut the data offer, the source time-to-live (TTL).
2. A per-instance evaluation of explanation quality for both models on both datasets and three seeds: SHAP–LIME agreement against its chance level, the self-agreement of both LIME and SHAP and its dependence on their sampling budgets, and faithfulness by deletion with four replacement strategies, one of which matches neither explainer's sampling distribution, and paired confidence intervals; LIME is ranked by each feature's contribution, and its raw weights are reported as a robustness check.
3. Feasibility-constrained counterfactuals that copy dependent fields jointly from real benign flows and stay within the support of benign traffic, compared under three search strategies, including one restricted to fields an attacker controls.
4. A controlled hardening experiment showing that counterfactual retraining reduces evasion under the attacker-controllable threat model but not when any field may change, and, through two controls of the same size, where the gain comes from: mainly from benign values in the attacker-controllable fields on TON-IoT, whereas on UNSW-NB15 the counterfactuals helped more than either control on average, a difference we test per seed with paired tests on the shared query records; and a transfer test in which the same models are attacked with two searches not used to generate the training data, showing that the gain persists under both and that, against an attacker who uses any of the three searches, the counterfactuals beat the benign-copy control in every seed on both datasets.

Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 describes the data, the audit and the corrected models. Section 4 defines the explanation measures. Section 5 reports the results, Section 6 discusses what they mean for practice and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Explanations for intrusion detection

SHAP and LIME have become the default explainers for machine-learning IDS. Moustafa et al. (2023) survey explainable intrusion detection for the IoT, and many studies apply SHAP or LIME to a trained detector and report the features it relies on; for example, Zebin et al. (2022) explain a detector of DNS-over-HTTPS attacks with SHAP, Gaspar et al. (2024) apply both methods to a multi-layer perceptron IDS, and Mohale and Obagbuwa (2025) compare LIME, SHAP and ELI5 for seven classifiers on UNSW-NB15. Most of this work treats an explanation as a finding about the attack. Explanations can also be read as findings about the data: Layeghy and Portmann (2023) use SHAP to show that detectors fail across datasets because they learn dataset-specific feature–label correspondences, and Arp et al. (2022) recommend explanations as a check for spurious correlations, one of ten pitfalls they find common in security research. Our `id` audit is an instance of this second use.

### 2.2 Evaluating explanation quality

Outside security, explanation methods are known to disagree with one another (Krishna et al., 2024) and to need quantitative evaluation rather than anecdotal examples (Nauta et al., 2023; Bodria et al., 2023); Visani et al. (2022) propose statistical indices for the stability of LIME in particular. Warnecke et al. (2020) proposed security-specific criteria, among them descriptive accuracy, which removes the features an explanation ranks highest and measures the effect on the prediction, the idea behind our deletion test (see also Samek et al., 2021). For network IDS, Arreche et al. (2024) evaluate SHAP and LIME on several metrics including stability and robustness across datasets and models; Kalakoti et al. (2024) score LIME and SHAP explanations of IoT botnet detectors for faithfulness, monotonicity, complexity and sensitivity, and Kalakoti et al. (2025) evaluate several explainers for faithfulness, complexity and robustness in the classification of alerts of a security operations centre; Kumar and Thing (2026) find that different explanation tools conflict for most deep-learning IDS they examine; and Maseno et al. (2026) show on TON-IoT that SHAP explanations of recurrent detectors become unstable under adversarial perturbation. Each of the measures we use therefore has precedent; our study applies them within one protocol, for a tree ensemble and a compact CNN, on two datasets and three seeds (Section 2.5).

### 2.3 Counterfactuals and explanation-guided evasion

Counterfactual explanations (Wachter et al., 2017), often generated with DiCE (Mothilal et al., 2020), describe the change that would alter a prediction. In intrusion detection they double as evasion recipes. Pawlicki et al. (2024) show that DiCE counterfactuals can be used to craft samples that flip a detector's label, and Okada et al. (2025) use the features that explanations rank highest to perturb real traffic and evade several deep-learning detectors while keeping the attacks functional. Galwaduge and Samarabandu (2025) compare counterfactual algorithms on three intrusion datasets and propose a diffusion-based generator for actionable counterfactuals, and Ben Kacem et al. (2025) combine a Random Forest IDS with SHAP, LIME and DiCE. Our counterfactuals differ in three respects: dependent fields are copied jointly from one real benign flow and candidates must lie close to benign traffic, so that each counterfactual is a plausible record; we compare unconstrained and attacker-controllable threat models; and we test, against a control, whether the counterfactuals can be turned into hardening data.

Our attacker-controllable threat model follows the literature on realistic adversarial attacks against network detectors. Apruzzese et al. (2022) model attackers who can alter only features they control, such as the duration and the bytes and packets they send, and Apruzzese et al. (2020) harden random-forest detectors against evasion of this kind; Sheatsley et al. (2022) and Chernikova and Oprea (2022) enforce domain constraints so that adversarial flows remain valid, Simonetto et al. (2022) unify constrained attack and defence, and Vitorino et al. (2022) train detectors on realistic adversarial perturbations; Vitorino et al. (2023) survey such attacks and defences. Al-Essa et al. (2026) compare counterfactual augmentation with adversarial training for intrusion detection. Restricting changes to sender-controlled fields and retraining on evasive flows are therefore established. What we add is a generator that copies dependent fields jointly from real benign flows under a support check, controls that separate the evasion criterion from the benign values it introduces, and a test of the hardened models against searches other than the one that generated their training data.

### 2.4 Data quality in intrusion detection benchmarks

Benchmark IDS datasets contain construction errors that inflate results. Engelen et al. (2021) document labelling errors and duplicated or misordered traffic in CICIDS2017 and release a corrected version, and Lanvin et al. (2023) show that such errors significantly change detection performance; Flood et al. (2024) identify recurring design flaws across seven highly cited benchmark datasets, and Goldschmidt and Chudá (2025) survey the limitations of network intrusion datasets more broadly; D'hooge et al. (2022) show that metadata fields such as addresses and ports contaminate detectors trained on ten benchmark datasets including UNSW-NB15; Bouke and Abdullah (2023) measure how leakage during pre-processing inflates intrusion detection results; Zoghi and Serpen (2024) address the overlap of records in UNSW-NB15; and Cantone et al. (2024) show that detectors that are near perfect within a dataset fall to near chance across datasets. Jacobs et al. (2022) show that network-security models frequently learn shortcuts and spurious correlations, and Beltiukov et al. (2023) report detectors that separate benign from malicious flows almost entirely by the TTL. On UNSW-NB15 itself, `sttl` is repeatedly found to be the dominant feature: it receives by far the highest XGBoost importance in the feature-selection study of Kasongo and Sun (2020), and it is the most influential feature of several explained classifiers in Mohale and Obagbuwa (2025), who read it as a sign that attackers manipulate TTL values. Duplicated records, file-order effects and host-identifying TTL values in UNSW-NB15 are part of this picture. Our TTL result confirms the dominance of `sttl` and interprets it differently, as a property of the testbed hosts; our contribution here is to show how an explanation exposed these artefacts, and to measure how much each changed the results of a pipeline that believed it had removed them.

### 2.5 What is new

Each ingredient of our protocol has precedent: `sttl` dominance in UNSW-NB15 (above), disagreement between explainers (Krishna et al., 2024; Kumar & Thing, 2026), deletion-based faithfulness (Warnecke et al., 2020) and explanation-guided evasion (Pawlicki et al., 2024; Okada et al., 2025). What is new is their combination and three findings it makes possible: (i) a controlled decomposition separating a leaking row identifier from disabled deduplication, the identifier raising accuracy on its own and macro-F1 clearly only on the test set with duplicates, a difference that test composition may explain; (ii) a per-alert comparison of SHAP and LIME that measures agreement against chance and against each explainer's own self-agreement, and faithfulness under four replacement strategies, one of which matches neither explainer's sampling distribution; and (iii) a contrast of two threat models for feasibility-constrained counterfactuals, with hardening experiments whose two controls separate the effect of the evasion criterion from that of benign-looking attack data, and which are re-attacked with searches the training data were not generated with.

## 3. Data, Audit and Models

### 3.1 Datasets

TON-IoT (Alsaedi et al., 2020) contains network flows from an IoT/IIoT testbed with nine attack classes (backdoor, DDoS, DoS, injection, MITM, password, ransomware, scanning, XSS) and benign traffic. We use the train/test network file of 211,043 records. UNSW-NB15 (Moustafa & Slay, 2015) contains flows from an enterprise-style testbed with nine attack categories (analysis, backdoor, DoS, exploits, fuzzers, generic, reconnaissance, shellcode, worms) and benign traffic; we use its training file of 175,341 records. UNSW-NB15 is not an IoT dataset, and we retain it as a second, independent benchmark rather than as evidence about IoT traffic.

### 3.2 Pre-processing

Both datasets pass through a shared pre-processing pipeline, released with our code: identifier-like and free-text columns are removed, categorical fields with at most 30 values are one-hot encoded, exact duplicates are removed on the final feature columns, the data are split into stratified training, validation and test sets (70/15/15, seed 42) with a guard that removes validation and test records identical to a training record, features are selected on the training set by a mutual-information filter and recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV), scaled to [0, 1], and the training set is oversampled with SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002). This leaves 25 features on TON-IoT and 10 on UNSW-NB15 (details, including why RFECV stops at its floor on UNSW-NB15, in Supplementary Material, S1).

### 3.3 Case study: auditing the id column

In the earlier version of the pipeline the selected UNSW-NB15 feature set contained `id`, the record's position in the file, and SHAP ranked it first for both models. The file is ordered in blocks (Figure 1A): its first 47,911 records are all benign, and *generic* attacks appear only after the first 66.8% of the file. The position of a record therefore predicts its label, and the models used it. The identifier had a second effect. With `id` present every record was unique, so the deduplication step removed nothing; without it, 67,601 of the 175,341 records (38.6%) are redundant copies of another record (45.2% of all records belong to a group of identical records), leaving 107,740; after the pipeline has also removed identifier and high-cardinality columns, 105,955 distinct records remain. The duplicates are unevenly spread (Figure 1B): the *generic* class shrinks from 40,000 to 4,181 records. Among the remaining records, 8,472 share identical features with a record of a different attack category (458 even with a record of the other binary label), which no classifier can separate. Re-creating the original split shows that 44.0% of the original test records had an identical copy, label included, in the training set.

To separate the two problems, we retrained the RF with the feature set held fixed: the 10 features selected by the corrected pipeline, with and without `id`, on the data with and without duplicates (Table 1). The two conditions of each pair are built from the same load of the data and share their training and test records, so `id` is the only difference within a pair. With duplicates kept, adding `id` raises macro-F1 by 0.031 [0.016, 0.046] (seed 42, paired bootstrap 95% interval over test records; 0.030–0.031 over three RF seeds); with duplicates removed, by only 0.007 [-0.006, 0.020] (0.006–0.011), an interval that includes zero in every seed. The difference between the two effects, the interaction of `id` with the duplicates, is 0.024 [0.004, 0.044] (seed 42; independent bootstraps of the two test sets). The two pairs are evaluated on different test sets (26,302 and 15,894 records) with different class compositions, and macro-F1 weights classes equally, so part of the change could come from the test composition rather than from the duplicates. On the 2,370 records that occur in both test sets, the macro-F1 effect of `id` is 0.003 [-0.059, 0.066] with duplicates in training and 0.020 [-0.012, 0.052] without them: neither is detectable, and the order is reversed, so the larger macro-F1 effect with duplicates may reflect the composition of the test set rather than an interaction. Accuracy tells a different story: `id` raises it by 0.053 [0.049, 0.056] with duplicates and by 0.050 [0.045, 0.055] without them (seed 42, paired bootstrap; 0.053 and 0.050–0.052 over three RF seeds). The identifier is therefore a leak in its own right, through the file order it encodes, and it helps mainly the large classes, which dominate accuracy but not macro-F1; its effect on macro-F1, and so on the smaller classes, was clear only while the duplicates were present. "Without duplicates" means without exact duplicates on the cleaned columns; feature-space coincidences remain (below). On TON-IoT the corrected pipeline produces exactly the same data as before (identical split, features and values). All results that follow use the corrected data.

## 4. Explanation Measures

### 4.1 Explainers

For the RF we use interventional TreeSHAP (Lundberg et al., 2020) with probability output and a background of 200 random training records; for the CNN, SHAP's GradientExplainer (expected gradients; Erion et al., 2021) with the same background and its default of 200 samples per record. Models and explainers use scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011), TensorFlow (Abadi et al., 2016), SHAP 0.49 and LIME 0.2.0.1. LIME uses the tabular explainer in continuous mode with its default Gaussian sampling, kernel and kernel width, a weighted ridge surrogate on all features and 1,000 perturbation samples unless stated otherwise (Supplementary Material, S3). Because the surrogate is then fitted on standardised features, we rank LIME's features by their contribution, the weight multiplied by the record's standardised value (x − μ)/σ, which, like a SHAP value, measures how far the feature moves this prediction from the average; ranking by the raw weights is reported as a robustness check. Every explanation refers to the class the model predicts for that record.

### 4.2 Global importance

Global importance is the mean absolute SHAP value over 1,000 random test records of the seed-42 models, averaged over the class-specific attributions of all classes (not only the predicted class). To show how firm the ranking is, we bootstrap these records 500 times and report the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of each feature's rank, rounded down; these intervals reflect the sampling of records only, not the randomness of the explainer, which is considerable for the CNN (Section 5.2). For the RF we also compute TreeSHAP interaction values on 100 records; these use the path-dependent algorithm, as the interventional one does not provide interactions.

### 4.3 Per-instance agreement

For a class-stratified sample of up to 30 test records per class, we compare the SHAP and LIME attributions of the same record and the same predicted class. We report the number of top-5 features the two share, the Jaccard index of the two top-5 sets, |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|, and Spearman's rank correlation of the absolute attributions over all features. Because two random top-5 sets already overlap, we also report the chance-corrected Jaccard index (J − J₀)/(1 − J₀), where J₀ is the expected Jaccard index of two random top-5 sets under the hypergeometric distribution (0.12 for the 25 TON-IoT features). Random top-5 sets are only a floor: two explainers of the same model will both favour globally important features. As a stronger baseline we therefore also report the Jaccard index between each local top-5 set and the model's global top-5 features (Section 4.2), and we relate SHAP–LIME agreement to the two explainers' self-agreement (Section 4.4) by the ratio J / √(J_SHAP,self · J_LIME,self). As one-hot columns of the same source variable can split one piece of evidence across several features, we repeat the comparison on fields, summing absolute attributions within each one-hot group (on UNSW-NB15 no selected feature is one-hot, so fields and features coincide).

### 4.4 Stability

LIME samples its perturbations at random, so two runs on the same record can return different explanations. For 10 test records per class (seed-42 models), we run LIME 20 times with 1,000 samples and 5 times with 5,000 samples, and report the mean pairwise Jaccard index of the top-5 sets (self-agreement). SHAP's values also depend on its random background and, for GradientExplainer, on random sampling along the integration path; we recompute SHAP with independent backgrounds and seeds and report its self-agreement in the same way (Supplementary Material, S3). We use 0.75 as a pragmatic threshold for an explanation an analyst could rely on, and report the share of records that reach it.

### 4.5 Faithfulness

Agreement and stability do not show that an explanation names the features the model actually uses. We therefore apply a deletion test (Warnecke et al., 2020; Samek et al., 2021): for each record in the agreement sample we replace the top-k features of an explanation (k = 1, 3, 5; ranked by absolute attribution, as for agreement) and measure the drop in the predicted-class probability. How a feature is 'removed' matters, and each common choice favours one explainer: values of random training records are the background distribution of interventional SHAP, independent Gaussian draws are LIME's sampling distribution, and mean replacement removes exactly a feature's LIME contribution. We therefore use four strategies: the training mean; the values of random training records; Gaussian draws with the training mean and standard deviation; and a conditional strategy, which is the sampling distribution of neither explainer: the values of the 10 training records closest to the record on the features that are *not* replaced. The baseline replaces k randomly chosen features. A faithful explanation produces a larger drop than the random baseline, and we compare SHAP with LIME by paired bootstrap intervals of the per-record difference. Two robustness runs, with the top-k features ranked by signed attribution and with LIME in three other configurations, are described in Supplementary Material, S3.

### 4.6 Feasible counterfactuals

A counterfactual explanation is a minimal change that alters the prediction (Wachter et al., 2017). For an IDS it describes an evasion: the smallest change that makes a detected attack look benign. Generic counterfactual generators move features to arbitrary values, including fractional values of one-hot flags and combinations of byte and packet counts that no flow can have; the counterfactuals of our earlier analysis had this problem. We instead build counterfactuals from real benign flows. Features are grouped into dependency groups whose values must change together: on TON-IoT, the protocol state (protocol, service, connection state and DNS fields), source volume (bytes, packets and IP bytes) and destination volume; on UNSW-NB15, the TTL fields, source and destination volume, TCP timing and connection history; remaining one-hot fields form one group each and other features are single groups (TON-IoT has 6 groups, UNSW-NB15 5). A candidate copies k groups jointly from one randomly chosen benign training record, and is accepted only if it is classified as benign *and* lies within the support of benign traffic, that is, if its Euclidean distance (on the scaled features) to the nearest of 20,000 benign training records does not exceed the 95th percentile of their nearest-neighbour distances (estimated on 5,000 of them). This radius is small, so accepted counterfactuals lie close to real benign flows. It also means that, in the attacker-controllable search, an attack can only evade if the features the attacker cannot change already lie close to some benign flow; such evasion partly measures the overlap of attack and benign traffic, and we report how often it applies (Section 5.4). For k = 1, …, 4 in turn we draw 200 candidates and stop at the smallest k with an accepted candidate.

We compare three search strategies: *random* (any k groups), *SHAP-guided* (always the k groups with the largest SHAP attribution for that record) and *attacker-controllable* (random groups among those an attacker can set from the attacking host: src port, src volume and duration on TON-IoT; the source TTL `sttl` alone and source volume on UNSW-NB15). On UNSW-NB15 the five dependency groups cover all ten features, so a counterfactual that copies every group is a benign record and full evasion within four groups is expected by construction. For each attack class we query up to 30 correctly detected test records per model and seed, and report the share that can be evaded, the number of groups changed, the groups most often involved and the number of model queries to the first evasion. Because candidates are sampled, the reported k is an upper bound on the true minimum (further details in Supplementary Material, S3).

### 4.7 Counterfactual hardening

If counterfactuals point to real weaknesses, training on them should reduce evasion. For up to 200 correctly detected training records per attack class we generate counterfactuals against the RF, label them with their original attack class, add them to the (SMOTE-oversampled) training set and retrain the RF with the same settings. Two controls of the same size test other explanations of a gain: interpolated attacks, each a random convex combination of two training attacks of the same class, test whether any extra attack data would have the same effect; benign-donor copies, which apply the same copying of the same groups to random training attacks but without requiring the model to call the result benign, test whether the gain comes from the evasion criterion rather than from benign-looking attack data. Benign donors are split into disjoint pools for generation and evaluation, and evasion is evaluated on one fixed query set per seed, the test attacks (up to 60 per class) that all four models detect correctly, so the evasion rates of the original model differ somewhat from those of Section 4.6. We run the experiment with counterfactuals that may change any group and with attacker-controllable groups only, for three seeds, and report evasion, macro-F1 and the benign false-positive rate; models are compared by exact McNemar tests on the per-record outcomes, and we also report the false-positive rate near the added records (Supplementary Material, S3).

Generation and evaluation above use the same random search, so a gain might only mean that the model has learnt that search. To test whether the hardening transfers, we attack the same four models (rebuilt with identical random streams, which reproduce the per-record outcomes of the original search exactly) on the same query records with two further searches. Both keep the attacker-controllable threat model and the support check, and both take as donors 100 benign test records per query, a population used neither for generation nor for the evaluation above. The greedy search is score-guided: for each donor it copies one attacker-controllable group at a time, each step taking the group that lowers the model's attack probability most. The partial-move search moves the controllable groups only part of the way towards the donor, x' = x + λ(d − x) with λ equal to 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75, on every non-empty subset of the groups, and so produces values that no benign record has. An attack counts as evaded if any donor yields a benign prediction within support. We also report the union of the three searches, the evasion rate of an attacker who tries all of them, and compare models by exact McNemar tests per seed and pooled over seeds.

### 4.8 Shortcut checks

Fixed testbed services make port numbers a possible shortcut to the label, and in testbeds the TTL of a packet often identifies the sending host rather than its behaviour. On TON-IoT, where `src_port` and `dst_port` are selected, we retrain the RF without the port features. On UNSW-NB15 we remove the TTL fields (`sttl`, `dttl` and `ct_state_ttl`) from the raw data *before* feature selection, so that the pipeline can select replacements, and retrain both models. We also measure how far `sttl` alone predicts the label: for each `sttl` value seen in training we take the majority label of the training records with that value, and apply this rule to the test set.

### 4.9 Statistics

Tables report means with percentile bootstrap 95% confidence intervals over records (2,000 resamples) for the seed-42 models, and the range of the means for seeds 42, 43 and 44; the samples are class-stratified, so their means weight classes equally rather than by traffic volume. For the headline agreement, counterfactual and hardening results we also give the mean over the three seeds with a two-level bootstrap interval (seeds resampled, then records within each seed); in the text, evasion rates are means over seeds unless marked as seed 42. No correction for multiple comparisons is applied; counts of significant combinations are descriptive, because the combinations share records, and we lead with effect sizes (computing environment in Supplementary Material, S3).

**Agreement.** For the same alert, SHAP and LIME agree only partly. For the RF on TON-IoT they share on average 1.86 of their top-5 features (top-5 Jaccard 0.24 [0.23, 0.26]; range over seeds 0.22–0.24), and for the CNN 2.27 (Jaccard 0.31 [0.30, 0.33]). Agreement is well above chance (0.12) but far from complete: the chance-corrected Jaccard index is 0.14 [0.12, 0.15] for the RF and 0.22 [0.20, 0.24] for the CNN. Merging one-hot columns into fields raises agreement, more for the CNN (0.47 [0.45, 0.49]) than for the RF (0.31 [0.29, 0.32]; chance 0.17), so part of the disagreement concerns which column of a one-hot field carries the evidence. For the RF the two methods also order the remaining features very differently (Spearman ρ 0.12 [0.10, 0.14], against 0.58 [0.57, 0.60] for the CNN). On UNSW-NB15, with 10 features, the two share 2.70 (RF) and 3.15 (CNN) of their top-5 features, against a chance Jaccard index of 0.35 (chance-corrected 0.05 [0.03, 0.08] and 0.20 [0.16, 0.23]). With so few features the raw overlap looks moderate, but for the RF it is barely above what two random rankings would give. A stronger baseline gives a similar picture: a record's SHAP top-5 overlaps the model's global top-5 more than it overlaps LIME's top-5 (Jaccard 0.45 for the RF and 0.26 for the CNN on TON-IoT; 0.49 and 0.57 on UNSW-NB15), except for the CNN on TON-IoT. Agreement varies strongly by class (Figure 2), from 0.13 (injection) to 0.41 (dos) for the RF on TON-IoT, and it is essentially unchanged without misclassified records or UNSW-NB15 records with a feature-space twin (Supplementary Material, S5). Cross-method agreement should also be read against each method's agreement with itself (below). Relative to the ceiling set by the two self-agreements, √(J_SHAP,self · J_LIME,self), SHAP–LIME agreement is 0.30 (RF) and 0.59 (CNN) on TON-IoT and 0.45 and 0.70 on UNSW-NB15 (self-agreements from the stability subsample). For the CNN, cross-method agreement thus reaches more than half of what the explainers' own noise allows, so part of that disagreement is noise within each method; for the RF, whose explanations are more reproducible, most of the disagreement is a conflict between the methods.

**Stability.** At its default of 1,000 samples, LIME does not reliably agree with itself on TON-IoT or for the CNN. Over 20 runs, its top-5 self-agreement on TON-IoT is 0.70 [0.68, 0.72] for the RF and 0.58 [0.56, 0.61] for the CNN, and only 29% and 9% of records reach 0.75; on UNSW-NB15, with fewer features, it is 0.82 [0.80, 0.84] (RF) and 0.73 [0.71, 0.75] (CNN), so LIME is reasonably stable for the UNSW-NB15 RF (75% of records at or above 0.75). With 5,000 samples the RF explanations stabilise (0.81 [0.79, 0.83] on TON-IoT; 66% of records at or above 0.75) but the CNN explanations improve less (0.62 [0.59, 0.65]; UNSW-NB15 0.77 [0.74, 0.79]). TreeSHAP is stable for the RF (0.90 [0.87, 0.92] on TON-IoT, 0.88 [0.86, 0.91] on UNSW-NB15), and background size does not matter (backgrounds of 1,000 records: 0.91 [0.89, 0.94] and 0.88 [0.86, 0.91]). GradientExplainer at its default settings is not: recomputed with new backgrounds and random seeds, its CNN attributions have a top-5 self-agreement (Jaccard) of only 0.48 [0.46, 0.50] on TON-IoT and 0.63 [0.61, 0.65] on UNSW-NB15, less than LIME at its default budget (the TON-IoT value corresponds to about 3.2 of 5 features shared between two runs). Part of this is sampling noise, but even with five times the default budget the TON-IoT CNN attributions reach a top-5 Jaccard of only 0.60 (Supplementary Material, S5). Averaging 20 LIME runs does not bring LIME closer to SHAP (S5), so the disagreement between the methods is systematic and not only sampling noise. The LIME surrogate also fits the CNN poorly (R² 0.06 [0.05, 0.07] on TON-IoT, against 0.29 [0.28, 0.31] for the RF).

**Faithfulness.** SHAP's top features are the more influential under every replacement strategy in the mean (Figure 3; Tables 3 and 4). Counting combinations of seed, strategy and k (36 per model and dataset), the SHAP − LIME difference is positive in all of them for the RF, and for the CNN in 35 of 36 on each dataset; it is significant (paired 95% interval above zero) in all of them for the RF, and for the CNN in 28 on TON-IoT and 31 on UNSW-NB15. In no combination is LIME significantly more faithful; for example, for the UNSW-NB15 CNN under mean replacement the difference at k = 3 is 0.02 [-0.01, 0.05]. On TON-IoT, replacing the top three SHAP features of the RF by training means lowers its confidence in its prediction by 0.76 [0.73, 0.78], the top three LIME features by 0.59 [0.55, 0.63] and three random features by 0.29 [0.28, 0.30]. The ordering does not depend on the replacement distribution: SHAP leads LIME with values from random training records, which is SHAP's own background distribution, but also with Gaussian draws, which is LIME's, and with conditional replacement by the nearest training records, which is neither. SHAP's top features beat random ones significantly in all 36 combinations for every model and dataset. LIME's do so in 36 (TON-IoT RF), 30 (TON-IoT CNN), 18 (UNSW-NB15 RF) and 30 (UNSW-NB15 CNN). LIME is thus weakest for the UNSW-NB15 RF, where its top features are no more influential than random ones under mean replacement (0.40 against 0.40), consistent with its near-chance agreement with SHAP there. Conditional replacement produces much smaller drops, because values consistent with the rest of the record disturb the model less, and the comparison also holds without the UNSW-NB15 records that have a feature-space twin (Supplementary Material, S5).

**Robustness.** We also ranked the top-k features by signed attribution, ran LIME at half and twice its default kernel width and in its discretised mode, and ranked LIME by its raw weights, as LIME lists them (Supplementary Material, S5). Ranking by sign does not reverse the comparison in any combination. SHAP's lead in the deletion test therefore does not depend on LIME's kernel width or representation. Raw-weight ranking changes how much the two methods agree, raising agreement for the RF and lowering it for the CNN, but not the faithfulness comparison: under neither LIME ranking is LIME significantly more faithful than SHAP in any combination.

### 5.3 Feasible counterfactuals

Supplementary Tables S3 and S4 report, per attack class, the share of detected attacks that can be evaded on TON-IoT and UNSW-NB15. When any group may change, every detected attack of both models can be evaded on TON-IoT, with 1.56 [1.49, 1.63] (RF) and 1.30 [1.25, 1.36] (CNN) groups changed on average and 47% (RF) and 70% (CNN) of attacks evadable by changing a single group. Given the support check, this is expected: a record that takes several groups from a benign flow and must stay close to benign traffic is nearly a benign record, so this arm is an upper bound by construction. The protocol-state group is involved in most of these counterfactuals for the RF (88% of successful searches). Such counterfactuals are feasible as records but rarely as attacks: a flow that takes over the protocol, service and connection state of a benign flow is unlikely to still deliver its payload.

Restricting the search to the groups an attacker controls changes the picture. On TON-IoT only 39% [31, 45] of detected attacks can then be evaded for the RF (mean over seeds with seed × record 95% interval; per seed 31%, 41% and 44%) and 13% [5, 19] for the CNN (per seed 16%, 18% and 4%), concentrated in a few classes. Guiding the search by SHAP makes evasion less likely for the RF, because the most important groups are fixed and only the donor varies, but cheaper for the CNN, whose median number of queries to the first evasion falls from 22.5 to 4 (Supplementary Material, S6). For the CNN on TON-IoT, explanations are therefore useful to an attacker mainly as a way to save queries; for the RF they are not useful at all. Allowing the TON-IoT attacker only to increase source volume and duration lowers these rates further, to 14 [10, 19]% for the RF and 10 [7, 14]% for the CNN in seed 42 (S6).

On UNSW-NB15, too, every detected attack of both models can be evaded when any group may change, and the TTL group is involved in most counterfactuals for the RF (70% of successful searches). When the attacker may change only the sender's TTL and the source volume, 42% [38, 45] (RF; per seed 43%, 41% and 42%) and 24% [19, 29] (CNN; per seed 30%, 21% and 21%) of detected attacks remain evadable. For the CNN this is more than on TON-IoT in every seed; for the RF it is similar (more in seed 42 only). Source volume is the main route (it is changed in 95% of the successful RF counterfactuals); the sender's TTL alone is a weaker lever than the TTL-derived fields it influences. SHAP guidance does not help on this dataset: for the CNN it raises the median number of queries from 206 to 401.

The counterfactuals are changes to flow records, not demonstrated attacks: we do not check that the modified traffic would still achieve the attack's goal, and the attacker-controllable groups are a judgement about what a sender can change, not a guarantee.

### 5.4 Counterfactual hardening

Table 5 and Figure 4 report the hardening experiment. When counterfactuals may change any group, retraining does not help: on both datasets every jointly detected test attack remains evadable against the original RF, after adding counterfactuals (1799–1800 per seed on TON-IoT) and after adding either control, in every seed. This arm is degenerate by construction. On TON-IoT the protocol-state group alone holds 16 of the 25 features, and copying it from a benign flow makes most of the record benign; on UNSW-NB15 four of the 5 groups together cover 7–9 of the 10 features. Neither is a realistic attack.

Under the attacker-controllable threat model hardening works, and the two controls show where the gain comes from. On TON-IoT, 41% of the jointly detected test attacks (range over seeds 39–45%; seed 42: 39 [35, 44]) can be evaded against the original RF. After adding 697–755 counterfactuals generated from a separate donor pool, evasion falls to 16% (15–17%; seed 42: 16 [13, 19]), with macro-F1 0.9571 against 0.9569 and a benign false-positive rate of 0.79% against 0.82%. The interpolation control leaves evasion at 43% (40–48%), so more attack data alone does not help. The benign-donor control, however, which copies the same attacker-controllable groups from benign flows into training attacks without requiring the result to evade the model, lowers evasion almost as far, to 19% (16–22%). On TON-IoT the gain therefore comes mainly from teaching the model that attacks can carry benign values in the fields an attacker controls; restricting the augmentation to actual evasions adds little on average. The effect is uneven across classes (Figure 4): evasion of injection (53% to 2%) and password attacks (51% to 2%) almost disappears and MITM evasion falls from 98% to 21%, while most ransomware attacks remain evadable. On UNSW-NB15 the effect is smaller: evasion falls from 43% (41–45%) to 32% (30–35%) with counterfactuals, while both controls give 41% (range over seeds 39–42% for each), with macro-F1 0.6064 against 0.6071. On average, then, the evasion criterion, which selects the few copies that cross the decision boundary, provides most of the gain on UNSW-NB15. Because all models are evaluated on the same query records, we compare them by exact McNemar tests: the counterfactuals beat the benign-donor copies by 8 [5, 12] points on UNSW-NB15 (pooled over seeds p < 0.001) and by 3 [0, 6] points on TON-IoT (pooled p < 0.001; mean differences with seed × record intervals). With the pairing used, the counterfactuals beat the benign-donor control in every seed on UNSW-NB15. On TON-IoT the advantage is significant in 2 of 3 seeds and therefore not robust to retraining: McNemar's test treats each trained model as fixed, whereas the seed × record interval also includes variation between retrained models (per-seed tests and interval comparisons in Supplementary Material, S7).

What these evasions mean depends on the support check: an attacker-controllable evasion requires the features the attacker cannot change to lie already within the support radius of a benign training record. Evading attacks meet this condition by construction; among the attacks the original RF does not let evade, 22% on TON-IoT and 87% on UNSW-NB15 also meet it (pooled over seeds). On TON-IoT evadable attacks are therefore largely those whose uncontrollable part already resembles benign traffic, whereas on UNSW-NB15 the condition hardly separates evadable from other attacks. On the benign test records nearest to the added counterfactuals, the false-positive rate barely changes on TON-IoT (9.3% against 9.2%) and rises by about two points on UNSW-NB15 (65.0% against 63.2%), where most of these records are already misclassified; without the UNSW-NB15 query records that have a feature-space twin, the three models keep the same order (Supplementary Material, S7).

### 5.5 Shortcuts: ports and TTL

Removing `src_port` and `dst_port` from TON-IoT lowers the RF macro-F1 from 0.9565 to 0.9420. Ports help, but the model does not depend on them for most of its accuracy, so their high rank in the explanations reflects genuine predictive value as well as testbed layout.

On UNSW-NB15, `sttl` behaves as a host identifier, much like the TTL shortcut reported by Beltiukov et al. (2023) for other network data. In the deduplicated data it takes three main values (254, 31, 62, together 99.2% of records), and the value 31 occurs in 39,123 records, none of them attacks. A rule that predicts the majority training label of each `sttl` value classifies 88.7% of test records correctly as benign or attack (binary majority baseline 51.0%), and 63.7% correctly into the ten classes (macro-F1 0.2016; majority-class baseline 49.0%). When the TTL fields are removed before feature selection, the pipeline selects 10 volume, load, timing and connection features instead (`sbytes`, `dbytes`, `sload`, `dload`, `dinpkt`, `tcprtt`, `ackdat`, `smean`, `dmean`, `ct_srv_dst`). The RF macro-F1 falls from 0.6089 to 0.5794 and the CNN's from 0.5139 to 0.5106; the largest per-class drops for the RF are worms 0.53 → 0.30 and shellcode 0.58 → 0.53. Most of the RF's drop thus comes from worms, the smallest class (19 test records, so its F1 is very noisy), and most other classes barely change; the CNN's drop is far smaller than its range over seeds (0.5139–0.5305) and within noise, so the evidence that the TTL is a shortcut rests on its SHAP rank and on the majority rule rather than on a dependence of performance on it. The models thus use the TTL for part of their accuracy but can largely replace it, which makes it a convenient shortcut rather than the only route to their predictions. The removal of one artefact (`id`) led the models to the next; in both cases it was the explanations that first drew our attention to the artefact, although, once suspected, the TTL shortcut is also visible from simple statistics, and a schema check would have caught `id`.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 What the explanations are good for

The most consequential result of this study did not come from a metric. A single global SHAP ranking, read with domain knowledge, exposed an identifier that should never have been a feature, and following it revealed disabled deduplication and a test set in which 44.0% of records had been seen in training. The controlled decomposition shows that the identifier, through the file order it encoded, was a leak in its own right, raising accuracy with or without duplicates, while the duplicates distorted the test set; whether they also carried the identifier's macro-F1 effect on the smaller classes cannot be separated from the change in test composition. On the corrected data the same reading exposed a second artefact, the source TTL, which identifies testbed hosts rather than attack behaviour. Shortcuts in these benchmarks are layered, and removing one can simply promote the next. Global explanations are a cheap check for data pipelines, and we recommend inspecting them for identifiers, timestamps and file-order artefacts before any performance is reported. The evidence for their value is limited, however: this is one retrospective case, of an error in our own earlier pipeline, which a schema check would also have caught, and the TTL shortcut is visible from simple statistics once suspected. It shows that the audit can work, not how often it would, nor that it would beat simpler checks.

Counterfactual analysis can also support hardening, if the threat model is right. Counterfactuals that may change any field produce evasions that no retraining can close and that are rarely realistic attacks. Restricted to fields an attacker controls, they identify a smaller set of weak points, and training on them reduced evasion by more than half on TON-IoT and by about a quarter on UNSW-NB15, with macro-F1 and aggregate false positives unchanged within a few tenths of a point (Section 5.4 reports the local false-positive rate). On TON-IoT these weak points are largely attacks whose uncontrollable features already resemble benign traffic, so part of what the search finds is class overlap rather than a fault of the model. The controls qualify the role of the counterfactuals. On TON-IoT almost the same reduction came from training attacks with benign values copied into the attacker-controllable fields, whether or not they evaded the model, so the counterfactual analysis mainly tells the designer *which* fields to augment; on UNSW-NB15 the evasions themselves helped more than benign-donor copies on average, consistently across seeds (Section 5.4). The gain is not an artefact of attacking with the search that produced the training data: it persisted under a score-guided search and under partial moves with new donors, and against an attacker who tries all three searches the counterfactuals protected better than the benign-donor copies on both datasets, although the copies protected better against partial moves on TON-IoT. A designer who can afford only one augmentation should therefore prefer the counterfactuals, and one who expects interpolating attackers may add both. The counterfactuals are generated by a random search over dependency groups, not by SHAP, so the gain should be credited to counterfactual analysis rather than to feature attributions. The threat model, not the generator, decides whether counterfactual hardening is meaningful.

### 6.2 What they should not be used for

Single local explanations are less trustworthy than they look. For an individual alert, SHAP and LIME typically share two or three of their top five features, and how many depends on whether LIME is read by its weights or by its contributions; LIME at its default settings frequently disagrees with itself, and SHAP for the CNN at its default sampling budget is no more stable than LIME. An analyst shown one explanation for one alert would see a ranking that a second run might not reproduce. Where local explanations are shown to analysts, we recommend TreeSHAP for tree ensembles (it was both stable and the most faithful in the deletion test); for neural models, GradientExplainer with a sampling budget well above its default, bearing in mind that 1,000 samples still left the CNN's explanations less stable than TreeSHAP's; and, if LIME is used, a report of the surrogate fit, a ranking by contribution rather than by raw weight and, for tree ensembles, at least 5,000 samples (which stabilised the TON-IoT RF explanations, 0.81). For neural models, 5,000 LIME samples are not enough (0.62 for the TON-IoT CNN), and several runs should be averaged or compared.

### 6.3 Limitations

Both datasets come from testbeds, and UNSW-NB15 is not IoT traffic; results on CICIoT2023 or Edge-IIoTset may differ. Three seeds show the variability over retrained models but do not estimate it precisely: the confidence intervals are over records for seed 42, and the range over seeds can exceed them; global importance and stability were computed for seed 42 only, and the seed-42 CNN cannot be retrained to exactly the same weights. LIME was evaluated mainly in one configuration (continuous mode, default kernel width), with a smaller sensitivity check of three others; the global rank intervals reflect record sampling, not explainer noise. The support radius is small in scaled units and therefore weak on heavy-tailed volume features. The UNSW-NB15 models are weak, about a third of the UNSW-NB15 records we explain are misclassified, and the RF is too large for constrained devices. Every deletion strategy changes the input distribution in some way. Counterfactuals are sampled, so the reported number of changed groups is an upper bound; they are changes to flow features, not validated attack traffic, and the dependency and attacker-controllable groups are our judgement. In particular, copying a lower source volume from a benign flow may break volume-based attacks such as DoS, so the attacker-controllable evasion rates are upper bounds on what a working attack could achieve (Supplementary Material, S6 reports a variant in which source volume and duration may only increase). The transfer test adds two searches, but all three work in the same feature space under the same threat model and support check; an attacker who optimises directly against the hardened model over a different feasible set, or shapes traffic so that uncontrollable fields change too, may do better, and we did not test whether adding both augmentations combines their benefits. Finally, we evaluate explanations quantitatively and do not test whether they help human analysts.

## 7. Conclusion

We audited the explanations of two network intrusion detectors instead of illustrating them. Global SHAP exposed a leaking identifier in UNSW-NB15 and, behind it, the failure of duplicate removal; a controlled decomposition showed that the identifier inflated accuracy through file order with or without exact duplicates, and macro-F1 clearly only on the test set with them, and the corrected models turned to a second artefact the data offer, the source TTL. On the corrected models, SHAP and LIME typically shared two or three of their top five features for the same alert, default LIME was unstable on TON-IoT and for the CNN, TreeSHAP was stable while GradientExplainer was unstable at its default sampling budget and only partly stabilised by a larger one, and SHAP's top features were the more faithful in every RF test and most CNN tests under four deletion strategies, including a conditional one that matches neither explainer's sampling, whether LIME was ranked by contribution or by raw weight. Feasible counterfactuals evaded every model when any field could change, as expected by construction, and 13–42% of detected attacks when only attacker-controllable fields could; in that threat model, training on counterfactuals cut RF evasion from 41% to 16% on TON-IoT and from 43% to 32% on UNSW-NB15 without material loss of accuracy; an interpolation control did not help, and on TON-IoT a control that copied benign values into the same fields was almost as effective against the search that generated the counterfactuals. The gain transferred to a score-guided search and to partial moves with new donors, and against an attacker who tries all three searches, evasion fell from 44% to 18% on TON-IoT and from 45% to 38% on UNSW-NB15, with the counterfactuals ahead of the benign-copy control on both datasets in every seed. In one case study a global explanation exposed a data leak, and counterfactual analysis identified which sender-controlled fields to augment; feature attributions themselves played no part in the hardening, and single local explanations are not yet a reliable account of why an individual alert was raised.
