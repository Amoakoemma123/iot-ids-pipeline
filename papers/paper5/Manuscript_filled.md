## Title

**When SHAP Finds the Leak: Auditing Agreement, Faithfulness and Feasible Counterfactuals in Network Intrusion Detection**

## Abstract

Explanations are increasingly offered as the trust layer of machine-learning intrusion detection systems (IDS), but are rarely measured. We audit the explanations of a Random Forest (RF) and a compact 1D-CNN on TON-IoT and UNSW-NB15, over three seeds. In an earlier pipeline version, SHAP ranked the UNSW-NB15 row identifier first: it encoded file order and had disabled duplicate removal (38.6% of records are redundant copies). With features held fixed, the identifier raised RF accuracy by 0.053 with exact duplicates and 0.050 without, but macro-F1 clearly only on the test set with them. The corrected models lean on a second shortcut, the source TTL. SHAP and LIME share 1.86 of their top-5 features per RF alert on TON-IoT (chance-corrected Jaccard 0.14); default LIME is unstable on TON-IoT and for the CNN, whose default SHAP is less stable still. In deletion tests with four replacement strategies, SHAP's top features lowered confidence more than LIME's in every RF test and most CNN tests. Counterfactuals copying dependent fields from benign flows evade 13–42% of detected attacks when only sender-controlled fields may change (all when any field may, by construction). Retraining on such counterfactuals cut RF evasion from 41% to 16% on TON-IoT and from 43% to 32% on UNSW-NB15; on TON-IoT, copying benign values into training attacks without the evasion criterion was almost as effective. A global explanation exposed a leak in one case study, and counterfactual analysis identified which sender-controlled fields to augment; single local explanations do not reliably explain individual alerts.

**Keywords:** explainable AI; intrusion detection; Internet of Things; SHAP; LIME; counterfactual explanations; data leakage

## 1. Introduction

Machine-learning intrusion detection systems (IDS) for the Internet of Things (IoT) report high detection rates on public benchmarks, yet operators are slow to deploy them because the models do not say why an alert was raised. Post-hoc explanation methods, mainly SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) and LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016), are therefore increasingly attached to IDS models as a transparency layer. Most IDS studies that use them show a global importance chart and a few local examples, and then conclude that the model has become interpretable. Whether these explanations are stable, whether different methods agree for the same alert, and whether the features they name actually drive the prediction are rarely measured, although work outside the IDS literature shows that each of these can fail (Krishna et al., 2024; Nauta et al., 2023).

This paper measures these properties for two IDS models, a Random Forest (RF) and a compact one-dimensional convolutional network (CNN), on TON-IoT and UNSW-NB15. The study began as a case of explanations at work. In an earlier, unpublished version of our pipeline, SHAP ranked `id`, the sequential row number of the UNSW-NB15 file, as the most important feature of both models. A row number carries no information about network traffic, so its top rank meant that the models had learned an artefact of the file. Following the trail showed a second problem: because every row had a different `id`, the duplicate-removal step removed nothing from UNSW-NB15, although 67,601 of its records (38.6%) are redundant copies of another record once `id` is set aside. We report this audit as a case study, separate the effects of the identifier and the duplicates experimentally, correct the data, and evaluate the explanations of the corrected models with three training seeds, class-stratified samples and confidence intervals.

The paper makes four contributions:

1. An explanation-led data audit, reported as a case study, with a controlled decomposition (Table 1) showing that the leaking identifier inflated UNSW-NB15 accuracy through the file order it encoded whether or not exact duplicates were present, and macro-F1 clearly only on the test set that kept them (on records common to both test sets neither effect was detectable), and that the corrected models turn to a second shortcut the data offer, the source time-to-live (TTL).
2. A per-instance evaluation of explanation quality for both models on both datasets and three seeds: SHAP–LIME agreement against its chance level, the self-agreement of both LIME and SHAP and its dependence on their sampling budgets, and faithfulness by deletion with four replacement strategies, one of which matches neither explainer's sampling distribution, and paired confidence intervals; LIME is ranked by each feature's contribution, and its raw weights are reported as a robustness check.
3. Feasibility-constrained counterfactuals that copy dependent fields jointly from real benign flows and stay within the support of benign traffic, compared under three search strategies, including one restricted to fields an attacker controls.
4. A controlled hardening experiment showing that counterfactual retraining reduces evasion under the attacker-controllable threat model but not when any field may change, and, through two controls of the same size, where the gain comes from: mainly from benign values in the attacker-controllable fields on TON-IoT, whereas on UNSW-NB15 the counterfactuals helped more than either control on average, a difference we test per seed with paired tests on the shared query records.

Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 describes the data, the audit and the corrected models. Section 4 defines the explanation measures. Section 5 reports the results, Section 6 discusses what they mean for practice and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Explanations for intrusion detection

SHAP and LIME have become the default explainers for machine-learning IDS. Moustafa et al. (2023) survey explainable intrusion detection for the IoT, and many studies apply SHAP or LIME to a trained detector and report the features it relies on; for example, Zebin et al. (2022) explain a detector of DNS-over-HTTPS attacks with SHAP, Gaspar et al. (2024) apply both methods to a multi-layer perceptron IDS, and Mohale and Obagbuwa (2025) compare LIME, SHAP and ELI5 for seven classifiers on UNSW-NB15. Most of this work treats an explanation as a finding about the attack. Explanations can also be read as findings about the data: Layeghy and Portmann (2023) use SHAP to show that detectors fail across datasets because they learn dataset-specific feature–label correspondences, and Arp et al. (2022) recommend explanations as a check for spurious correlations, one of ten pitfalls they find common in security research. Our `id` audit is an instance of this second use.

### 2.2 Evaluating explanation quality

Outside security, explanation methods are known to disagree with one another (Krishna et al., 2024) and to need quantitative evaluation rather than anecdotal examples (Nauta et al., 2023; Bodria et al., 2023). Warnecke et al. (2020) proposed security-specific criteria, among them descriptive accuracy, which removes the features an explanation ranks highest and measures the effect on the prediction, the idea behind our deletion test (see also Samek et al., 2021). For network IDS, Arreche et al. (2024) evaluate SHAP and LIME on several metrics including stability and robustness across datasets and models; Kalakoti et al. (2024) score LIME and SHAP explanations of IoT botnet detectors for faithfulness, monotonicity, complexity and sensitivity, and Kalakoti et al. (2025) evaluate several explainers for faithfulness, complexity and robustness in the classification of alerts of a security operations centre; Kumar and Thing (2026) find that different explanation tools conflict for most deep-learning IDS they examine; and Maseno et al. (2026) show on TON-IoT that SHAP explanations of recurrent detectors become unstable under adversarial perturbation. Each of the measures we use therefore has precedent; our study applies them within one protocol, for a tree ensemble and a compact CNN, on two datasets and three seeds (Section 2.5).

### 2.3 Counterfactuals and explanation-guided evasion

Counterfactual explanations (Wachter et al., 2017), often generated with DiCE (Mothilal et al., 2020), describe the change that would alter a prediction. In intrusion detection they double as evasion recipes. Pawlicki et al. (2024) show that DiCE counterfactuals can be used to craft samples that flip a detector's label, and Okada et al. (2025) use the features that explanations rank highest to perturb real traffic and evade several deep-learning detectors while keeping the attacks functional. Galwaduge and Samarabandu (2025) compare counterfactual algorithms on three intrusion datasets and propose a diffusion-based generator for actionable counterfactuals, and Ben Kacem et al. (2025) combine a Random Forest IDS with SHAP, LIME and DiCE. Our counterfactuals differ in three respects: dependent fields are copied jointly from one real benign flow and candidates must lie close to benign traffic, so that each counterfactual is a plausible record; we compare unconstrained and attacker-controllable threat models; and we test, against a control, whether the counterfactuals can be turned into hardening data.

### 2.4 Data quality in intrusion detection benchmarks

Benchmark IDS datasets contain construction errors that inflate results. Engelen et al. (2021) document labelling errors and duplicated or misordered traffic in CICIDS2017 and release a corrected version, and Lanvin et al. (2023) show that such errors significantly change detection performance; Flood et al. (2024) identify recurring design flaws across seven highly cited benchmark datasets; and Cantone et al. (2024) show that detectors that are near perfect within a dataset fall to near chance across datasets. Jacobs et al. (2022) show that network-security models frequently learn shortcuts and spurious correlations, and Beltiukov et al. (2023) report detectors that separate benign from malicious flows almost entirely by the TTL. On UNSW-NB15 itself, `sttl` is repeatedly found to be the dominant feature: it receives by far the highest XGBoost importance in the feature-selection study of Kasongo and Sun (2020), and it is the most influential feature of several explained classifiers in Mohale and Obagbuwa (2025), who read it as a sign that attackers manipulate TTL values. Duplicated records, file-order effects and host-identifying TTL values in UNSW-NB15 are part of this picture. Our TTL result confirms the dominance of `sttl` and interprets it differently, as a property of the testbed hosts; our contribution here is to show how an explanation exposed these artefacts, and to measure how much each changed the results of a pipeline that believed it had removed them.

### 2.5 What is new

Each ingredient of our protocol has precedent: `sttl` dominance in UNSW-NB15 (above), disagreement between explainers (Krishna et al., 2024; Kumar & Thing, 2026), deletion-based faithfulness (Warnecke et al., 2020) and explanation-guided evasion (Pawlicki et al., 2024; Okada et al., 2025). What is new is their combination and three findings it makes possible: (i) a controlled decomposition separating a leaking row identifier from disabled deduplication, the identifier raising accuracy on its own and macro-F1 clearly only on the test set with duplicates, a difference that test composition may explain; (ii) a per-alert comparison of SHAP and LIME that measures agreement against chance and against each explainer's own self-agreement, and faithfulness under four replacement strategies, one of which matches neither explainer's sampling distribution; and (iii) a contrast of two threat models for feasibility-constrained counterfactuals, with hardening experiments whose two controls separate the effect of the evasion criterion from that of benign-looking attack data.

## 3. Data, Audit and Models

### 3.1 Datasets

TON-IoT (Alsaedi et al., 2020) contains network flows from an IoT/IIoT testbed with nine attack classes (backdoor, DDoS, DoS, injection, MITM, password, ransomware, scanning, XSS) and benign traffic. We use the train/test network file of 211,043 records. UNSW-NB15 (Moustafa & Slay, 2015) contains flows from an enterprise-style testbed with nine attack categories (analysis, backdoor, DoS, exploits, fuzzers, generic, reconnaissance, shellcode, worms) and benign traffic; we use its training file of 175,341 records. UNSW-NB15 is not an IoT dataset, and we retain it as a second, independent benchmark rather than as evidence about IoT traffic.

### 3.2 Pre-processing

Both datasets pass through a shared pre-processing pipeline, released with our code. Identifier-like and free-text columns (row identifiers, IP addresses, DNS queries, HTTP and SSL strings) are removed, strings are normalised, numeric columns are typed, categorical fields with more than 30 values are dropped and the rest are one-hot encoded (dropping the first level). Exact duplicate records are then removed, so that duplicates are judged on the final feature columns, and the data are split into stratified training, validation and test sets (70/15/15, seed 42). Missing-value filtering, median imputation, the replacement of infinite values and the removal of constant columns are fitted on the training rows only, and any validation or test record identical to a training record is removed. Features are selected on the training set by a mutual-information filter (features with mutual information below 0.05 are dropped) followed by recursive feature elimination with three-fold stratified cross-validation (RFECV; a 50-tree Random Forest scored by macro-F1 on a 50,000-record subsample, with a floor of 10 features), scaled to [0, 1], and the training set is oversampled with SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002), which raises every class to the size of the largest training class (the configured cap of 50,000 records per class does not bind on either dataset); validation and test sets are never resampled. On TON-IoT, RFECV keeps 25 of the 27 features that pass the filter, an interior optimum. On UNSW-NB15 it keeps 10 of 40, exactly the floor; its cross-validated score is highest there and lower for every larger feature set, so the floor and the optimum coincide, but a lower floor could have selected fewer features. Deduplication and the cross-split check operate on the cleaned columns before feature selection; Section 3.3 reports how many test records become identical to a training record once only the selected features are kept.

### 3.3 Case study: auditing the id column

In the earlier version of the pipeline the selected UNSW-NB15 feature set contained `id`, the record's position in the file, and SHAP ranked it first for both models. The file is ordered in blocks (Figure 1A): its first 47,911 records are all benign, and *generic* attacks appear only after the first 66.8% of the file. The position of a record therefore predicts its label, and the models used it. The identifier had a second effect. With `id` present every record was unique, so the deduplication step removed nothing; without it, 67,601 of the 175,341 records (38.6%) are redundant copies of another record (45.2% of all records belong to a group of identical records), leaving 107,740; after the pipeline has also removed identifier and high-cardinality columns, 105,955 distinct records remain. The duplicates are unevenly spread (Figure 1B): the *generic* class shrinks from 40,000 to 4,181 records. Among the remaining records, 8,472 share identical features with a record of a different attack category (458 even with a record of the other binary label), which no classifier can separate. Re-creating the original split shows that 44.0% of the original test records had an identical copy, label included, in the training set.

To separate the two problems, we retrained the RF with the feature set held fixed: the 10 features selected by the corrected pipeline, with and without `id`, on the data with and without duplicates (Table 1). The two conditions of each pair are built from the same load of the data and share their training and test records, so `id` is the only difference within a pair. With duplicates kept, adding `id` raises macro-F1 by 0.031 [0.016, 0.046] (seed 42, paired bootstrap 95% interval over test records; 0.030–0.031 over three RF seeds); with duplicates removed, by only 0.007 [-0.006, 0.020] (0.006–0.011), an interval that includes zero in every seed. The difference between the two effects, the interaction of `id` with the duplicates, is 0.024 [0.004, 0.044] (seed 42; independent bootstraps of the two test sets). The two pairs are evaluated on different test sets (26,302 and 15,894 records) with different class compositions, and macro-F1 weights classes equally, so part of the change could come from the test composition rather than from the duplicates. On the 2,370 records that occur in both test sets, the macro-F1 effect of `id` is 0.003 [-0.059, 0.066] with duplicates in training and 0.020 [-0.012, 0.052] without them: neither is detectable, and the order is reversed, so the larger macro-F1 effect with duplicates may reflect the composition of the test set rather than an interaction. Accuracy tells a different story: `id` raises it by 0.053 [0.049, 0.056] with duplicates and by 0.050 [0.045, 0.055] without them (seed 42, paired bootstrap; 0.053 and 0.050–0.052 over three RF seeds). The identifier is therefore a leak in its own right, through the file order it encodes, and it helps mainly the large classes, which dominate accuracy but not macro-F1; its effect on macro-F1, and so on the smaller classes, was clear only while the duplicates were present. "Without duplicates" means without exact duplicates on the cleaned columns; feature-space coincidences remain (below). We also compared the original model of the earlier pipeline with a model retrained without `id` and with feature selection run again, and a re-selected model with `id` against the corrected model on the deduplicated data; these comparisons also change other features (and, for the first, the earlier version of the pipeline), and give macro-F1 differences of 0.034 and 0.015. The copied test records were nevertheless not easy cases for the original model: they were classified *less* accurately than the others (accuracy 0.7527 against 0.8750; macro-F1 0.6257 against 0.6328), because duplicates are concentrated in classes with conflicting labels. On TON-IoT the corrected pipeline produces exactly the same data as before (identical split, features and values). All results that follow use the corrected data.

### 3.4 Models

The models come from a companion study by the same authors, a separate manuscript that benchmarks five detectors on the same corrected pipeline; the present paper reuses its seed-42 RF and CNN and their test performance (Table 2), and its own contribution is the analysis of their explanations and the counterfactual and hardening experiments, which the companion study does not address. We explain the RF, the best model of that study on both datasets (results in the released repository), and the CNN, the most compact competitive model (on UNSW-NB15 an autoencoder + RF scores slightly higher than the CNN, macro-F1 0.5177 against 0.5139). The RF (Breiman, 2001) has 200 trees (maximum depth 20, minimum leaf size 2, √p features per split). The CNN has two convolutional blocks (32 and 64 filters, kernel 3, batch normalisation, max-pooling), global average pooling and two dense layers (128 and 64 units, dropout 0.3), 23,946 parameters in total; it is trained with Adam (learning rate halved after 7 epochs without improvement), class weights computed on the oversampled training labels (in which all classes have the same size, so the weights are uniform and have no effect), and early stopping on validation loss (patience 15). The convolutions run over the fixed order of the feature vector, in which neighbouring features need not be related; we use the CNN as a compact neural detector, not because the order carries locality. Both models are trained with three seeds (42, 43, 44) on the same data, split and selected features. For the explanation and counterfactual stages the seed also sets the random draws of those procedures (SHAP backgrounds, LIME and GradientExplainer sampling, deletion donors, counterfactual and hardening search), while the explained test records are fixed; ranges over seeds therefore combine retraining with explainer and search randomness. The seed-42 models are those of the companion study; the seed-42 CNN was trained without a fixed TensorFlow seed, so its exact values cannot be reproduced by retraining (the saved model is released). Seed 42 provides the record-level confidence intervals in the tables, and the other seeds the range over retrained models (Section 4.9). After feature selection TON-IoT has 25 features and UNSW-NB15 10 (the original UNSW-NB15 set had 16, including `id`).

## 4. Explanation Measures

### 4.1 Explainers

For the RF we use interventional TreeSHAP (Lundberg et al., 2020) with probability output and a background of 200 random training records; for the CNN, SHAP's GradientExplainer (expected gradients; Erion et al., 2021) with the same background and its default of 200 samples per record (`nsamples` = 200). Models and explainers use scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011), TensorFlow (Abadi et al., 2016), SHAP 0.49 and LIME 0.2.0.1. LIME uses the tabular explainer without discretisation (continuous mode), which samples perturbations from independent Gaussians with the training mean and standard deviation of each feature (LIME's default sampling; its default representation would discretise the features into quartiles), weights them with LIME's default exponential kernel on the Euclidean distance in standardised feature space with the default kernel width 0.75√p (3.75 on TON-IoT and 2.37 on UNSW-NB15), and fits a weighted ridge surrogate on all features; it draws 1,000 perturbation samples unless stated otherwise. Section 5.2 reports how the results change with half and twice the kernel width and with discretisation. Without discretisation the surrogate is fitted on standardised features, so a LIME weight is a slope per standard deviation of the feature. We therefore rank LIME's features by their contribution, the weight multiplied by the record's standardised value (x − μ)/σ, which, like a SHAP value, measures how far the feature moves this prediction from the average; ranking by the raw weights, which ignores where the record lies, is reported in Section 5.2 as a robustness check. Every explanation refers to the class the model predicts for that record.

### 4.2 Global importance

Global importance is the mean absolute SHAP value over 1,000 random test records of the seed-42 models, averaged over the class-specific attributions of all classes (not only the predicted class). To show how firm the ranking is, we bootstrap these records 500 times and report the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of each feature's rank, rounded down; these intervals reflect the sampling of records only, not the randomness of the explainer, which is considerable for the CNN (Section 5.2). For the RF we also compute TreeSHAP interaction values on 100 records; these use the path-dependent algorithm, as the interventional one does not provide interactions.

### 4.3 Per-instance agreement

For a class-stratified sample of up to 30 test records per class, we compare the SHAP and LIME attributions of the same record and the same predicted class. We report the number of top-5 features the two share, the Jaccard index of the two top-5 sets, |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|, and Spearman's rank correlation of the absolute attributions over all features. Because two random top-5 sets already overlap, we also report the chance-corrected Jaccard index (J − J₀)/(1 − J₀), where J₀ is the expected Jaccard index of two random top-5 sets under the hypergeometric distribution (0.12 for the 25 TON-IoT features). Random top-5 sets are only a floor: two explainers of the same model will both favour globally important features. As a stronger baseline we therefore also report the Jaccard index between each local top-5 set and the model's global top-5 features (Section 4.2), and we relate SHAP–LIME agreement to the two explainers' self-agreement (Section 4.4) by the ratio J / √(J_SHAP,self · J_LIME,self). As one-hot columns of the same source variable can split one piece of evidence across several features, we repeat the comparison on fields, summing absolute attributions within each one-hot group (on UNSW-NB15 no selected feature is one-hot, so fields and features coincide). The earlier version of this analysis had compared class-averaged global SHAP with LIME weights pooled over 12 records, a comparison of two different objects, which these measures replace.

### 4.4 Stability

LIME samples its perturbations at random, so two runs on the same record can return different explanations. For 10 test records per class (seed-42 models), we run LIME 20 times with 1,000 samples and 5 times with 5,000 samples, and report the mean pairwise Jaccard index of the top-5 sets (self-agreement). SHAP's values also depend on its random background and, for GradientExplainer, on random sampling along the integration path; we therefore recompute SHAP with three independent backgrounds for the RF and five background–seed combinations for the CNN and report its self-agreement in the same way. To separate the two sources of variation, we also recompute the CNN attributions with a fixed background and new random seeds at 200 and 1,000 samples, and with new backgrounds at 1,000 samples, and the RF attributions with backgrounds of 200 and of 1,000 records. The comparisons involve different numbers of runs (20 and 5 for LIME, 3 and 5 for SHAP); mean pairwise agreement does not depend on the number of runs, but the share of records above a threshold does somewhat. We also compare a consensus LIME explanation (the mean absolute weights of the 20 runs) with SHAP. We use 0.75 as a pragmatic threshold for an explanation an analyst could rely on, and report the share of records that reach it.

### 4.5 Faithfulness

Agreement and stability do not show that an explanation names the features the model actually uses. We therefore apply a deletion test (Warnecke et al., 2020; Samek et al., 2021): for each record in the agreement sample we replace the top-k features of an explanation (k = 1, 3, 5; ranked by absolute attribution, as for agreement) and measure the drop in the predicted-class probability. Because the ranking is by absolute value, the top-k set can contain features that push against the predicted class, whose replacement should raise confidence; Section 5.2 therefore also reports a ranking by signed attribution, which replaces only the features that push towards the predicted class. How a feature is 'removed' matters, and each common choice favours one explainer: values of random training records are exactly the background distribution of interventional SHAP, independent Gaussian draws are LIME's sampling distribution, and with LIME ranked by contribution, replacing a feature by its training mean removes exactly that feature's contribution w·(x − μ)/σ from the surrogate, so mean replacement is, if anything, favourable to LIME. We therefore use four strategies: the training mean; the values of random training records (averaged over 10 records drawn from 5,000); Gaussian draws with the training mean and standard deviation (10 draws); and a conditional strategy, which is the sampling distribution of neither explainer: the values of the 10 training records (of the same 5,000, excluding exact copies of the record) that are closest to the record on the features that are *not* replaced, which keeps the replaced values consistent with the rest of the record. Excluding the copies changes no mean confidence drop by more than 0.008. The baseline replaces k randomly chosen features (10 draws per record). A faithful explanation produces a larger drop than the random baseline, and we compare SHAP with LIME by paired bootstrap intervals of the per-record difference. Two robustness runs use the seed-42 models and the first 15 records per class of the agreement sample, with the same SHAP background and donors: LIME at 0.5, 1 and 2 times its default kernel width and in its default discretised mode (in which a LIME weight is already a contribution relative to the record's quartile bin), for agreement, surrogate fit and deletion; and deletion with the top-k features ranked by signed attribution, for SHAP and for continuous LIME at the default width.

### 4.6 Feasible counterfactuals

A counterfactual explanation is a minimal change that alters the prediction (Wachter et al., 2017). For an IDS it describes an evasion: the smallest change that makes a detected attack look benign. Generic counterfactual generators move features to arbitrary values, including fractional values of one-hot flags and combinations of byte and packet counts that no flow can have; the counterfactuals of our earlier analysis had this problem. We instead build counterfactuals from real benign flows. Features are grouped into dependency groups whose values must change together: on TON-IoT, the protocol state (protocol, service, connection state and DNS fields), source volume (bytes, packets and IP bytes) and destination volume; on UNSW-NB15, the TTL fields, source and destination volume, TCP timing and connection history; remaining one-hot fields form one group each and other features are single groups (TON-IoT has 6 groups, UNSW-NB15 5). A candidate copies k groups jointly from one randomly chosen benign training record, and is accepted only if it is classified as benign *and* lies within the support of benign traffic, that is, if its Euclidean distance (on the scaled features) to the nearest of 20,000 benign training records does not exceed the 95th percentile of their nearest-neighbour distances (estimated on 5,000 of them). This radius is small, so accepted counterfactuals lie close to real benign flows. It also means that, in the attacker-controllable search, an attack can only evade if the features the attacker cannot change already lie close to some benign flow; such evasion partly measures the overlap of attack and benign traffic, and we report how often it applies (Section 5.4). For k = 1, …, 4 in turn we draw 200 candidates and stop at the smallest k with an accepted candidate.

We compare three search strategies: *random* (any k groups), *SHAP-guided* (always the k groups with the largest SHAP attribution for that record) and *attacker-controllable* (random groups among those an attacker can set from the attacking host: src port, src volume and duration on TON-IoT; the source TTL `sttl` alone and source volume on UNSW-NB15, where the TTL-derived `ct_state_ttl`, which also depends on the responder, is left unchanged; changing `sttl` alone can therefore produce combinations of `sttl` and `ct_state_ttl` that the dataset's derivation of `ct_state_ttl` would not generate, an inconsistency the support check guards against only partly). With only three and two such groups, the attacker-controllable search changes at most three groups on TON-IoT and two on UNSW-NB15. On UNSW-NB15 the five dependency groups cover all ten features, so a counterfactual that copies every group is a benign record and full evasion within four groups is expected by construction. For each attack class we query up to 30 correctly detected test records per model and seed, and report the share that can be evaded, the number of groups changed, the groups most often involved and the number of model queries to the first evasion (counting every candidate up to the first accepted one, including candidates the support check rejects, which an attacker who applied the check first would not need to submit). Because candidates are sampled, the reported k is an upper bound on the true minimum.

### 4.7 Counterfactual hardening

If counterfactuals point to real weaknesses, training on them should reduce evasion. For up to 200 correctly detected training records per attack class we generate counterfactuals against the RF, label them with their original attack class, add them to the (SMOTE-oversampled) training set and retrain the RF with the same settings. As a control we add the same number of synthetic attacks per class, each a random convex combination of two training attacks of that class, which tests whether any extra attack data would have the same effect. A second control applies the same benign-donor copying (the same set of groups, the same number of groups and the same support check) to random training attacks of each class, but without requiring the model to call the result benign (when none of 50 candidates passes the support check, the last one is kept, which happened for 25–30 of the 697–755 copies per seed on TON-IoT and 0–2 on UNSW-NB15); it tests whether the gain comes from the evasion criterion rather than from benign-looking attack data. Benign donors are split into two disjoint pools, one for generating training counterfactuals and one for evaluating evasion. Evasion is evaluated on one fixed query set per seed: the test attacks (up to 60 per class) that all four models detect correctly. This set and its donor pool differ from those of Section 4.6, so the evasion rates of the original model differ somewhat between the two sections. We run the experiment twice, once with counterfactuals that may change any group and once with attacker-controllable groups only (in both generation and evaluation), for three seeds, and report evasion, macro-F1 and the benign false-positive rate. Because all models are evaluated on the same query records, we compare the counterfactual model with each other model by exact McNemar tests on the per-record evasion outcomes, per seed and pooled over seeds (exact test on the summed discordant pairs, a stratified McNemar test). Because aggregate false positives over thousands of benign test flows cannot reveal a local cost of adding attack-labelled records close to benign traffic, we also report the false-positive rate on the benign test records nearest to the added records (the five nearest to each added record).

### 4.8 Shortcut checks

Fixed testbed services make port numbers a possible shortcut to the label, and in testbeds the TTL of a packet often identifies the sending host rather than its behaviour. On TON-IoT, where `src_port` and `dst_port` are selected, we retrain the RF without the port features. On UNSW-NB15 we remove the TTL fields (`sttl`, `dttl` and `ct_state_ttl`) from the raw data *before* feature selection, so that the pipeline can select replacements, and retrain both models. We also measure how far `sttl` alone predicts the label: for each `sttl` value seen in training we take the majority label of the training records with that value, and apply this rule to the test set.

### 4.9 Statistics

Tables report means with percentile bootstrap 95% confidence intervals over records (2,000 resamples) for the seed-42 models, and the range of the means for seeds 42, 43 and 44. The bootstrap resamples records with replacement without stratifying by class; the samples themselves are class-stratified (up to 30 records per class), so their means weight classes equally rather than by traffic volume. For the headline agreement, counterfactual and hardening results we also give the mean over the three seeds with a two-level bootstrap interval (seeds resampled, then records within each seed); in the text, evasion rates are means over seeds unless marked as seed 42. No correction for multiple comparisons is applied; counts of significant combinations are descriptive, because the combinations share records, and we lead with effect sizes. Computation used a laptop with an Intel Core i5-10200H CPU, 16 GB RAM and an NVIDIA GTX 1650 (4 GB); SHAP and LIME ran on the CPU.

**Agreement.** For the same alert, SHAP and LIME agree only partly. For the RF on TON-IoT they share on average 1.86 of their top-5 features (top-5 Jaccard 0.24 [0.23, 0.26]; range over seeds 0.22–0.24), and for the CNN 2.27 (Jaccard 0.31 [0.30, 0.33]). Agreement is well above chance (0.12) but far from complete: the chance-corrected Jaccard index is 0.14 [0.12, 0.15] for the RF and 0.22 [0.20, 0.24] for the CNN. Merging one-hot columns into fields raises agreement, more for the CNN (0.47 [0.45, 0.49]) than for the RF (0.31 [0.29, 0.32]; chance 0.17), so part of the disagreement concerns which column of a one-hot field carries the evidence. On UNSW-NB15, with 10 features, the two share 2.70 (RF) and 3.15 (CNN) of their top-5 features, against a chance Jaccard index of 0.35 (chance-corrected 0.05 [0.03, 0.08] and 0.20 [0.16, 0.23]). With so few features the raw overlap looks moderate, but for the RF it is barely above what two random rankings would give. A stronger baseline gives a similar picture: a record's SHAP top-5 overlaps the model's global top-5 more than it overlaps LIME's top-5 (Jaccard 0.45 for the RF and 0.26 for the CNN on TON-IoT; 0.49 and 0.57 on UNSW-NB15), except for the CNN on TON-IoT. Agreement varies strongly by class (Figure 2), from 0.13 (injection) to 0.41 (dos) for the RF on TON-IoT, and excluding misclassified records (35% of the UNSW-NB15 RF sample) slightly raises it (UNSW-NB15 RF: 0.41 [0.39, 0.43], against 0.39 [0.37, 0.40] for all records). Excluding the UNSW-NB15 records with a feature-space twin in training (36% of the sample) leaves agreement essentially unchanged (0.39 [0.37, 0.41] for the RF and 0.48 [0.45, 0.50] for the CNN). Cross-method agreement should also be read against each method's agreement with itself (below). Relative to the ceiling set by the two self-agreements, √(J_SHAP,self · J_LIME,self), SHAP–LIME agreement is 0.30 (RF) and 0.59 (CNN) on TON-IoT and 0.45 and 0.70 on UNSW-NB15 (self-agreements from the stability subsample). For the CNN, cross-method agreement thus reaches more than half of what the explainers' own noise allows, so part of that disagreement is noise within each method; for the RF, whose explanations are more reproducible, most of the disagreement is a conflict between the methods. The pooled comparison of our earlier analysis had reported 0.67 for the RF on TON-IoT; that figure compared aggregates and does not hold for individual alerts.

**Stability.** At its default of 1,000 samples, LIME does not reliably agree with itself on TON-IoT or for the CNN. Over 20 runs, its top-5 self-agreement on TON-IoT is 0.70 [0.68, 0.72] for the RF and 0.58 [0.56, 0.61] for the CNN, and only 29% and 9% of records reach 0.75; on UNSW-NB15, with fewer features, it is 0.82 [0.80, 0.84] (RF) and 0.73 [0.71, 0.75] (CNN), so LIME is reasonably stable for the UNSW-NB15 RF (75% of records at or above 0.75). With 5,000 samples the RF explanations stabilise (0.81 [0.79, 0.83] on TON-IoT; 66% of records at or above 0.75) but the CNN explanations improve less (0.62 [0.59, 0.65]; UNSW-NB15 0.77 [0.74, 0.79]). GradientExplainer at its default settings is not: recomputed with new backgrounds and random seeds, its CNN attributions have a top-5 self-agreement (Jaccard) of only 0.48 [0.46, 0.50] on TON-IoT and 0.63 [0.61, 0.65] on UNSW-NB15, less than LIME at its default budget (the TON-IoT value corresponds to about 3.2 of 5 features shared between two runs). Part of this is sampling noise. With a fixed background and new random seeds, CNN self-agreement is 0.50 [0.48, 0.52] at the default 200 samples and 0.60 [0.58, 0.63] at 1,000 on TON-IoT (0.62 [0.60, 0.64] and 0.76 [0.73, 0.78] on UNSW-NB15), and changing the background as well, still at 1,000 samples, lowers it only to 0.57 [0.55, 0.60] and 0.73 [0.71, 0.75]. Even with five times the default budget, the TON-IoT CNN attributions reach a top-5 Jaccard of only 0.60 (about 3.8 of 5 features shared). Averaging 20 LIME runs does not bring LIME closer to SHAP: on the stability subsample (10 records per class, so the single-run values differ slightly from Table 5), the paired difference between consensus and single-run agreement with SHAP is -0.002 [-0.011, 0.007] for the RF and -0.020 [-0.047, 0.006] for the CNN on TON-IoT (consensus 0.22 [0.20, 0.24] and 0.31 [0.28, 0.34]). The LIME surrogate also fits the CNN poorly (R² 0.06 [0.05, 0.07] on TON-IoT, against 0.29 [0.28, 0.31] for the RF).

**Faithfulness.** SHAP's top features are the more influential under every replacement strategy in the mean (Figure 3; Tables 5 and 6). Counting combinations of seed, strategy and k (36 per model and dataset), the SHAP − LIME difference is positive in all of them for the RF, and for the CNN in 35 of 36 on each dataset; it is significant (paired 95% interval above zero) in all of them for the RF, and for the CNN in 28 on TON-IoT and 31 on UNSW-NB15. In no combination is LIME significantly more faithful; for example, for the UNSW-NB15 CNN under mean replacement the difference at k = 3 is 0.02 [-0.01, 0.05]. On TON-IoT, replacing the top three SHAP features of the RF by training means lowers its confidence in its prediction by 0.76 [0.73, 0.78], the top three LIME features by 0.59 [0.55, 0.63] and three random features by 0.29 [0.28, 0.30]. The ordering does not depend on the replacement distribution: SHAP leads LIME with values from random training records, which is SHAP's own background distribution, but also with Gaussian draws, which is LIME's, and with conditional replacement by the nearest training records, which is neither. SHAP's top features beat random ones significantly in all 36 combinations for every model and dataset. LIME's do so in 36 (TON-IoT RF), 30 (TON-IoT CNN), 18 (UNSW-NB15 RF) and 30 (UNSW-NB15 CNN). LIME is thus weakest for the UNSW-NB15 RF, where its top features are no more influential than random ones under mean replacement (0.40 against 0.40), consistent with its near-chance agreement with SHAP there. Conditional replacement produces much smaller drops (for SHAP's top three features 0.22–0.55 times the mean-replacement drop, depending on model and dataset; 0.18–0.49 for LIME's and 0.09–0.43 for random features), because values consistent with the rest of the record disturb the model less; mean and Gaussian replacement inflate the random baseline for the CNN, which reacts strongly to unusual inputs. Without the UNSW-NB15 records that have a feature-space twin in training, SHAP − LIME remains significant in 36 (RF) and 31 (CNN) of the 36 combinations (k = 3, conditional replacement: 0.12 [0.09, 0.15] and 0.10 [0.06, 0.15]).

### 5.3 Feasible counterfactuals

Table 7 reports, per attack class, the share of detected attacks that can be evaded on TON-IoT, and Table 8 on UNSW-NB15. When any group may change, every detected attack of both models can be evaded on TON-IoT, with 1.56 [1.49, 1.63] (RF) and 1.30 [1.25, 1.36] (CNN) groups changed on average and 47% (RF) and 70% (CNN) of attacks evadable by changing a single group. Given the support check, this is expected: a record that takes several groups from a benign flow and must stay close to benign traffic is nearly a benign record, so this arm is an upper bound by construction. The protocol-state group is involved in most of these counterfactuals for the RF (88% of successful searches). Such counterfactuals are feasible as records but rarely as attacks: a flow that takes over the protocol, service and connection state of a benign flow is unlikely to still deliver its payload.

Restricting the search to the groups an attacker controls changes the picture. On TON-IoT only 39% [31, 45] of detected attacks can then be evaded for the RF (mean over seeds with seed × record 95% interval; per seed 31%, 41% and 44%) and 13% [5, 19] for the CNN (per seed 16%, 18% and 4%), concentrated in a few classes. Guiding the search by SHAP (over all groups, so to be compared with random search over all groups) makes evasion less likely for the RF (87% success against 100% for random groups, because the k most important groups are fixed and only the donor varies), but cheaper for the CNN: the median number of model queries to the first evasion falls from 22.5 to 4, with success 95% against 100% (medians over successful searches, so the slightly lower success of SHAP guidance is not included; computing the SHAP values themselves is not counted either). For the CNN on TON-IoT, explanations are therefore useful to an attacker mainly as a way to save queries; for the RF they are not useful at all. Restricting the TON-IoT attacker further, so that the source volume and duration may only increase (copied from a benign donor only when no value decreases), gives 14 [10, 19]% for the RF and 10 [7, 14]% for the CNN (seed 42; 34 [29, 40]% and 18 [13, 23]% without the restriction on the same records, in a re-run with new random candidates, so these differ slightly from Table 7).

### 5.4 Counterfactual hardening

Table 9 and Figure 4 report the hardening experiment. When counterfactuals may change any group, retraining does not help: on both datasets every jointly detected test attack remains evadable against the original RF, after adding counterfactuals (1799–1800 per seed on TON-IoT) and after adding either control, in every seed. This arm is degenerate by construction. On TON-IoT the protocol-state group alone holds 16 of the 25 features, and copying it from a benign flow makes most of the record benign; on UNSW-NB15 four of the 5 groups together cover 7–9 of the 10 features. Neither is a realistic attack.

Under the attacker-controllable threat model hardening works, and the two controls show where the gain comes from. On TON-IoT, 41% of the jointly detected test attacks (range over seeds 39–45%; seed 42: 39 [35, 44]) can be evaded against the original RF. After adding 697–755 counterfactuals generated from a separate donor pool, evasion falls to 16% (15–17%; seed 42: 16 [13, 19]), with macro-F1 0.9571 against 0.9569 and a benign false-positive rate of 0.79% against 0.82%. The interpolation control leaves evasion at 43% (40–48%), so more attack data alone does not help. The benign-donor control, however, which copies the same attacker-controllable groups from benign flows into training attacks without requiring the result to evade the model, lowers evasion almost as far, to 19% (16–22%). On TON-IoT the gain therefore comes mainly from teaching the model that attacks can carry benign values in the fields an attacker controls; restricting the augmentation to actual evasions adds little on average. The effect is uneven across classes (Figure 4): evasion of injection (53% to 2%) and password attacks (51% to 2%) almost disappears and MITM evasion falls from 98% to 21%, while most ransomware attacks remain evadable. The two augmentations also differ by class: for MITM the counterfactuals help more than the benign-donor copies (21% against 41%), for ransomware less (72% against 61%). On UNSW-NB15 the effect is smaller: evasion falls from 43% (41–45%) to 32% (30–35%) with counterfactuals, while both controls give 41% (range over seeds 39–42% for each), with macro-F1 0.6064 against 0.6071. On average, then, the evasion criterion, which selects the few copies that cross the decision boundary, provides most of the gain on UNSW-NB15. Judged by interval separation, the per-seed evidence looks weaker: the counterfactual model's 95% interval lies below those of the original model and the interpolation control in 3 of 3 seeds on TON-IoT and 2 of 3 on UNSW-NB15, and below that of the benign-donor control in 0 and 1 of 3 (seed 42 on UNSW-NB15: 35 [30, 39] against 42 [37, 46]). Interval separation is a conservative criterion for outcomes that are paired by record. Exact McNemar tests on the shared query records give, for counterfactuals against benign-donor copies, p < 0.001, p < 0.001 and p < 0.001 in seeds 42, 43 and 44 on UNSW-NB15 (pooled over seeds p < 0.001; mean difference in evasion 8 [5, 12] points, seed × record interval) and p < 0.001, p < 0.001 and p = 1.000 on TON-IoT (pooled p < 0.001; 3 [0, 6] points). Against the original model they give p < 0.001, p < 0.001 and p < 0.001 on UNSW-NB15 and p < 0.001, p < 0.001 and p < 0.001 on TON-IoT. With the pairing used, the counterfactuals beat the benign-donor control in every seed on UNSW-NB15. McNemar's test treats each trained model as fixed and so reflects variation between records only, whereas the seed × record interval also includes variation between retrained models; on TON-IoT the counterfactuals' advantage over benign-donor copies (3 [0, 6] points) is significant in 2 of 3 seeds and therefore not robust to retraining. These tests come from a second run of the hardening experiment with the same seeds, which stores the per-record outcomes; it reproduces the evasion rates above for the original and counterfactual models exactly, and those of the two controls to within 0.7 points.

What these evasions mean, and what the hardening costs, depends on the support check. An attacker-controllable evasion is only possible if the features the attacker cannot change already lie within the support radius of a benign training record. This holds for 100% of the attacks the original RF lets evade on TON-IoT and 100% on UNSW-NB15, as it must, since an evading counterfactual passes the support check on all features and keeps the uncontrollable ones; the informative figures are those for the attacks it does not let evade, 22% and 87% (pooled over seeds). On TON-IoT evadable attacks are therefore largely those whose uncontrollable part already resembles benign traffic, whereas on UNSW-NB15 the condition hardly separates evadable from other attacks. Adding hundreds of attack-labelled records close to benign traffic could raise false positives locally even when the aggregate rate is unchanged. On the benign test records nearest to the added counterfactuals (the five nearest to each; 349–368 records per seed on TON-IoT and 769–861 on UNSW-NB15), the false-positive rate is 9.3% after counterfactual retraining against 9.2% for the original RF on TON-IoT, and 65.0% against 63.2% on UNSW-NB15; near the benign-donor copies it is 9.8% against 10.1% (TON-IoT) and 70.9% against 70.2% (UNSW-NB15; means over seeds). The added counterfactuals thus barely change false positives near them on TON-IoT; on UNSW-NB15 most of the nearby benign records are already misclassified by the original RF, and the rate rises by about two further points. Without the UNSW-NB15 query records that have a feature-space twin in training (331–343 records per seed remain), evasion is 33% for the original RF, 24% after counterfactual retraining and 31% with benign-donor copies.

### 5.5 Shortcuts: ports and TTL

Removing `src_port` and `dst_port` from TON-IoT lowers the RF macro-F1 from 0.9565 to 0.9420. Ports help, but the model does not depend on them for most of its accuracy, so their high rank in the explanations reflects genuine predictive value as well as testbed layout.

On UNSW-NB15, `sttl` behaves as a host identifier, much like the TTL shortcut reported by Beltiukov et al. (2023) for other network data. In the deduplicated data it takes three main values (254, 31, 62, together 99.2% of records), and the value 31 occurs in 39,123 records, none of them attacks. A rule that predicts the majority training label of each `sttl` value classifies 88.7% of test records correctly as benign or attack (binary majority baseline 51.0%), and 63.7% correctly into the ten classes (macro-F1 0.2016; majority-class baseline 49.0%). When the TTL fields are removed before feature selection, the pipeline selects 10 volume, load, timing and connection features instead (`sbytes`, `dbytes`, `sload`, `dload`, `dinpkt`, `tcprtt`, `ackdat`, `smean`, `dmean`, `ct_srv_dst`). The RF macro-F1 falls from 0.6089 to 0.5794 and the CNN's from 0.5139 to 0.5106; the largest per-class drops for the RF are worms 0.53 → 0.30 and shellcode 0.58 → 0.53. Most of the RF's drop thus comes from worms, the smallest class (19 test records, so its F1 is very noisy), and most other classes barely change; the CNN's drop is far smaller than its range over seeds (0.5139–0.5305) and within noise, so the evidence that the TTL is a shortcut rests on its SHAP rank and on the majority rule rather than on a dependence of performance on it. The models thus use the TTL for part of their accuracy but can largely replace it, which makes it a convenient shortcut rather than the only route to their predictions. The removal of one artefact (`id`) led the models to the next; in both cases it was the explanations that first drew our attention to the artefact, although, once suspected, the TTL shortcut is also visible from simple statistics, and a schema check would have caught `id`.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 What the explanations are good for

The most consequential result of this study did not come from a metric. A single global SHAP ranking, read with domain knowledge, exposed an identifier that should never have been a feature, and following it revealed disabled deduplication and a test set in which 44.0% of records had been seen in training. The controlled decomposition shows that the identifier, through the file order it encoded, was a leak in its own right, raising accuracy with or without duplicates, while the duplicates distorted the test set; whether they also carried the identifier's macro-F1 effect on the smaller classes cannot be separated from the change in test composition. On the corrected data the same reading exposed a second artefact, the source TTL, which identifies testbed hosts rather than attack behaviour. Shortcuts in these benchmarks are layered, and removing one can simply promote the next. Global explanations are a cheap check for data pipelines, and we recommend inspecting them for identifiers, timestamps and file-order artefacts before any performance is reported. The evidence for their value is limited, however: this is one retrospective case, of an error in our own earlier pipeline, which a schema check would also have caught, and the TTL shortcut is visible from simple statistics once suspected. It shows that the audit can work, not how often it would, nor that it would beat simpler checks.

Counterfactual analysis can also support hardening, if the threat model is right. Counterfactuals that may change any field produce evasions that no retraining can close and that are rarely realistic attacks. Restricted to fields an attacker controls, they identify a smaller set of weak points, and training on them reduced evasion by more than half on TON-IoT and by about a quarter on UNSW-NB15, with macro-F1 and aggregate false positives unchanged within a few tenths of a point (Section 5.4 reports the local false-positive rate). On TON-IoT these weak points are largely attacks whose uncontrollable features already resemble benign traffic, so part of what the search finds is class overlap rather than a fault of the model. The controls qualify the role of the counterfactuals. On TON-IoT almost the same reduction came from training attacks with benign values copied into the attacker-controllable fields, whether or not they evaded the model, so the counterfactual analysis mainly tells the designer *which* fields to augment; on UNSW-NB15 the evasions themselves helped more than benign-donor copies on average, consistently across seeds (Section 5.4).4. The counterfactuals are generated by a random search over dependency groups, not by SHAP, so the gain should be credited to counterfactual analysis rather than to feature attributions. The threat model, not the generator, decides whether counterfactual hardening is meaningful.

### 6.2 What they should not be used for

Single local explanations are less trustworthy than they look. For an individual alert, SHAP and LIME typically share two or three of their top five features, and how many depends on whether LIME is read by its weights or by its contributions; LIME at its default settings frequently disagrees with itself, and SHAP for the CNN at its default sampling budget is no more stable than LIME. An analyst shown one explanation for one alert would see a ranking that a second run might not reproduce. Where local explanations are shown to analysts, we recommend TreeSHAP for tree ensembles (it was both stable and the most faithful in the deletion test); for neural models, GradientExplainer with a sampling budget well above its default, bearing in mind that 1,000 samples still left the CNN's explanations less stable than TreeSHAP's; and, if LIME is used, a report of the surrogate fit, a ranking by contribution rather than by raw weight and, for tree ensembles, at least 5,000 samples (which stabilised the TON-IoT RF explanations, 0.81). For neural models, 5,000 LIME samples are not enough (0.62 for the TON-IoT CNN), and several runs should be averaged or compared.

### 6.3 Limitations

Both datasets come from testbeds, and UNSW-NB15 is not IoT traffic; results on CICIoT2023 or Edge-IIoTset may differ. Three seeds show the variability over retrained models but do not estimate it precisely: the confidence intervals are over records for seed 42, and the range over seeds can exceed them; global importance and stability were computed for seed 42 only, and the seed-42 CNN cannot be retrained to exactly the same weights. LIME was evaluated mainly in one configuration (continuous mode, default kernel width), with a smaller sensitivity check of three others; the global rank intervals reflect record sampling, not explainer noise. The support radius is small in scaled units and therefore weak on heavy-tailed volume features. The UNSW-NB15 models are weak, about a third of the UNSW-NB15 records we explain are misclassified, and the RF is too large for constrained devices. Every deletion strategy changes the input distribution in some way. Counterfactuals are sampled, so the reported number of changed groups is an upper bound; they are changes to flow features, not validated attack traffic, and the dependency and attacker-controllable groups are our judgement. In particular, copying a lower source volume from a benign flow may break volume-based attacks such as DoS, so the attacker-controllable evasion rates are upper bounds on what a working attack could achieve (Section 5.3 reports a variant in which source volume and duration may only increase). The hardening experiment uses the same search for generating and evaluating counterfactuals (with disjoint donors), so an adaptive attacker with a different search would likely do better. Finally, we evaluate explanations quantitatively and do not test whether they help human analysts.

## 7. Conclusion

We audited the explanations of two network intrusion detectors instead of illustrating them. Global SHAP exposed a leaking identifier in UNSW-NB15 and, behind it, the failure of duplicate removal; a controlled decomposition showed that the identifier inflated accuracy through file order with or without exact duplicates, and macro-F1 clearly only on the test set with them, and the corrected models turned to a second artefact the data offer, the source TTL. On the corrected models, SHAP and LIME typically shared two or three of their top five features for the same alert, default LIME was unstable on TON-IoT and for the CNN, TreeSHAP was stable while GradientExplainer was unstable at its default sampling budget and only partly stabilised by a larger one, and SHAP's top features were the more faithful in every RF test and most CNN tests under four deletion strategies, including a conditional one that matches neither explainer's sampling, whether LIME was ranked by contribution or by raw weight. Feasible counterfactuals evaded every model when any field could change, as expected by construction, and 13–42% of detected attacks when only attacker-controllable fields could; in that threat model, training on counterfactuals cut RF evasion from 41% to 16% on TON-IoT and from 43% to 32% on UNSW-NB15 without material loss of accuracy; an interpolation control did not help, and on TON-IoT a control that copied benign values into the same fields was almost as effective. In one case study a global explanation exposed a data leak, and counterfactual analysis identified which sender-controlled fields to augment; feature attributions themselves played no part in the hardening, and single local explanations are not yet a reliable account of why an individual alert was raised.
